How someone finds the tool from its name

A person hears "a tool for civic accountability called X" at a meeting, on a podcast or from a friend. They have only the sound of the name, and they take four steps. A name has to survive all four.

  1. Hear it, spell itThey write down what they heard. A name with one obvious spelling, no silent letters and no sound-alike word survives.
  2. Search for itThey type it into a search engine. If the name is an ordinary word, laboratories, plumbers or another company fill the first page. If nobody else uses it, our pages come first.
  3. Type the addressMany people simply try "name.org" or "name.com". The project needs to own the obvious address.
  4. Look in a store or on GitHubTechnical members look for it on GitHub and phones look in app stores. Nobody else's software should come up.

One more signpost works for any name: the credit line at the foot of every published case ("Made with …") can link to the project's home. Then every case a group publishes is a way in.

Recommendation

Civicsmith

Your suggestion is the strongest name in either round. It passes all four steps, and it is the only candidate with every address free, the .com included. It also says what the tool is for, which matters for finding it: someone told about "a civic accountability tool called Civicsmith" remembers the word "civic" because it is the point. It reads well where it will most often appear: "Made with Civicsmith", "your group's copy of Civicsmith". Heedwork is the runner-up if you would rather the name carry a value than the field.

To secure it, before anyone outside hears it:

  • Register civicsmith.org and civicsmith.com, with civicsmith.app and civicsmith.net.
  • Have a lawyer run a trademark clearance search for software (US classes 9 and 42), then decide whether to file.
  • Claim the name on GitHub and the main social networks. I couldn't check GitHub from here.

Registering addresses and filing a trademark are acts only you can take. When you choose, I'll walk you through them step by step.

Brand check: Civicsmith

Your question: is it too long, will the people who use it relate to it, and will they happily say "I use Civicsmith to do this"? What follows is reasoned judgement, not research with real people. A ten-minute test you can run yourself is at the end of this section.

FineLengthThree syllables (CIV-ic-smith) and ten letters, like Salesforce or WordPress. It is short enough that people will say it whole. The shortenings, "Civic" and "Smith", are both awkward (Honda, a surname), which keeps the full name in use.
StrongPowerfulA smith forges durable things by hand: strength without aggression. It sounds like building, not attacking, in line with your choice of "pressure" over "attacks".
GoodWelcomingPlain English with no technical jargon (the "OS" goes). "Civic" includes everyone. It sounds human, more like a trade than an app.
WatchEasyThe name promises craft, not ease. A newcomer may hear "for skilled people". Ease has to come from the product (wizards, the plain voice), and the name shouldn't promise what the product must show.

What the name brings to mind

  • "-smith" works by analogy. Wordsmith, locksmith, blacksmith: someone skilled at a craft. People understand "civicsmith" on first hearing as someone skilled at civic work, or the tool they use for it.
  • Smith is also everyone's surname. For American ears it echoes Mr. Smith Goes to Washington, the ordinary citizen who stands up to a corrupt machine. That suits a tool for ordinary people.
  • "Civic" is shared ground. Civic duty, civic centre, civics class: used across the political spectrum, so the name takes no side. Its weak points are a whiff of school homework, and the car.
  • It names no place, so it travels to any city, as your rule requires.

Weak points, and what handles each

  • Promises craft more than ease. The product carries ease: wizards, the plain voice, the first screens. Don't add "easy" to the name.
  • Could read as a person or a firm in the credit. Write it as one word with no space, and make the credit link to a short page that says "Civicsmith is free software for civic groups".
  • Earnest for the youngest organisers. Accepted. Earnest is right for a tool whose output must persuade journalists and officials.
  • Searches for "civic" alone land elsewhere. The full word is unique. Every published case's credit points home.
  • Outside English. "Civic" is close to cívico, civique and civico; "smith" is a well-known English surname. Translating the interface, which comes later, doesn't depend on the name.

Next to the runner-up: Heedwork carries a value more warmly but has to be explained, and it sounds like "headwork". Civicsmith is understood on first hearing.

A ten-minute test before you commit

Real reactions beat my judgement. Try this with five to eight people from different audiences: a member, a professional, a journalist friend, someone outside civic work.

  1. Say, without showing it written: "There's a free tool for civic accountability called Civicsmith."
  2. Ask them to write the name down.
  3. Ask what they think it does, and who it's for.
  4. Ask whether they'd be comfortable saying "I use Civicsmith to…" to a colleague or an official.
  5. Repeat with Heedwork, for comparison.

If most people spell it right, guess roughly what it does, and would say it without hesitation, the name passes.

Before the mailing list

You plan to test the name on a broad Berkeley list (3 October). You agreed that "smith" points at the destination, expert civic accountability tools, so the path to it must be wide and welcoming for every audience. That carries into the journeys (step 3).

Lock down first

  1. Before sending: register civicsmith.org and civicsmith.com, ideally with .net and .app (about $10–20 a year each). Turn on auto-renew and privacy, under an account the project controls. A published name is what address speculators look for.
  2. Before sending: create a free GitHub organisation called civicsmith.
  3. Nice to have: a one-line holding page at civicsmith.org, so anyone who types the address finds the project and not a parking page. Also the social handles you would actually use.
  4. Can wait for the replies: the trademark clearance search, and a filing if you want one (US classes 9 and 42). Talking about a name on a mailing list carries little risk to it.
