1. Who we design for

The original brief named five audiences in order of how often they use the product; the requirements gathered twenty. Here they are reconciled into three rings. The newcomer member stays the centre of gravity.

Inside the group: members

Everyone who belongs to the group and works in its copy. They hold different roles at different moments; one person may be several of these.

Folded in, not separate audiences:

  • Group operator, founder: moments of the administrator's role, kept as steps in journey 1.
  • Project participant, member outside a project: states any member can be in, not separate audiences.
  • Residents addressed by an action: the people an awareness action speaks to; they meet the group's words, not its screens.
  • The assistant: an actor that works for members, not an audience; it appears in the journeys where it acts.

Across every ring: members who read in another language, screen-reader and keyboard users, and people working from a phone. These are not separate audiences; every journey must work for them.

Kinds of group

Groups differ as much as members do. Your examples, 4 October:

ProfessionalA newsroom or journalist, a law firm, an inside or outside auditor.
Issue-specificLibraries, labor, a ballot measure, schools.
Neighbourhood or communityA neighbourhood, a business community, parents.
Catch-allA whole city, a single agency, a county.

An inside auditor is worth noting: it means a public body itself may run a copy. The design must not assume the group always stands outside government. A group says which kind it is in journey 2.

2. The wide path

Your words, 3 October: the path to success must be "wide enough and inclusive enough for all audiences". Proposed as eight rules every journey is checked against.

  1. A front door in every journeyEach journey has a first step a newcomer can take alone, with no prior knowledge, and the screen shows where it starts.
  2. Wizards on the main roads, never gatesThe main journeys can be walked with a wizard. Nothing is locked behind one, and anyone can leave it at any step: stopping is a non-event.
  3. You can always see where you areEvery journey shows its stage and what comes next, so a newcomer never wonders whether they are done.
  4. Explained where it appears, and findable againA term is explained the first time a member meets it, at that spot, in plain words. After that it is never pushed again, but the explanation is always one hover, focus or tap away on the term itself, and in one list of every term, for when a member forgets.
  5. Heavier steps slow you downFriction rises with an act's weight, so no one stumbles into anything permanent, signed or outward.
  6. Any order, and pick up where you left offSteps can be taken in whatever order the work allows; only a step that truly needs another first waits for it. A member can leave a journey for other work at any point, and come back at any time to find it exactly as they left it, with their unfinished work listed where they will see it.
  7. Many front doorsA member can start from whatever drew them in: most often a problem they live with, or else a code section, a court case, a news story, a budget, a meeting, something they experienced, a tip. Civicsmith offers a sensible first step from each, and no journey assumes everyone starts from a question.
  8. Fast for those who knowProfessionals and experienced members move quickly: search, keyboard, acting on many items at once, no repeated teaching.

3. Where members start

Most people come to this work because something in their own lives isn't working: the street outside, the block they live on, the bill they pay, the services they were promised. Others come through something they read: a code chapter, a court case, a news story. Each can be a member's first motivating interest, and each needs a sensible first step. No journey should assume everyone starts from a question.

A problem they live with

The most common way in. At the city auditor's open forum you attended (4 October), these were the reasons people gave most often for coming. Each is a frustration; Civicsmith's job is to help turn it into a question about the city's own standards that the record can answer.

What this means for the design:

  • Frustration is welcome and the record stays disciplined: a member's own notes can say how angry they are; the question, the evidence and the case say what the documents show.
  • Civicsmith holds the city to its own laws, standards and statements. It takes no side on what policy should be, including on a ballot measure, while still checking the city's claims about one.
  • Every kind of document is captured, pointed at and cited the same way: a code section, a court filing, an article, a budget, minutes, a contract, a dataset.
  • The group's home asks "What brought you here?" and every empty project offers "Start from…", with a problem the member lives with first and these documents after; none is the "right" way in.
  • The assistant can suggest first questions from whatever the member starts with, such as one question per requirement in a code section, each labelled as a suggestion.
  • Professionals bring their own angles: lawyers start from filings, accountants from financial reports, policy analysts from codes and rates. Their journey (8) routes questions to them.

4. Seventeen journeys

In the order people meet them, from a group's first day to a stranger reading its case. Each card says who takes the journey, its newcomer's front door, its wizard, and what changed from the list gathered earlier. Open "Steps" for the path: steps are listed in their usual order, but members may take them in any order the work allows, leave, and come back later. Marked steps are the heavy ones: permanent, signed, outward (the world sees it), reasoned, terminal or irreversible.

6Checking a claim about the city's performanceNew

The city says it filled 90% of reported potholes this year. Is that true? Civicsmith does not count potholes itself; it holds the claim, the city's own records, members' spot-checks and the reasoning together, so anyone can check the answer. Who: members; often an analyst or accountant member.

Front doorMost members start with "is that really true?"; capturing the claim is a complete first step, and the journey explains what Civicsmith can and cannot check.
WizardCheck a claim (optional)
What changedAdded after your question (4 October): a newcomer will wonder what Civicsmith can really do about potholes or dumping. The honest answer is that it checks the city's claims against the city's own records and members' spot-checks; it is not a system for logging every pothole. One gap: Civicsmith can capture and read a spreadsheet, but the requirements show no way to compute over one inside the product, so for now the calculation is the assistant's or a member's, shown with its method. A built-in, repeatable calculation step may be needed.
Steps (7)
  1. memberCaptures the claim where the city made it: a budget, a report to council, a press release.
  2. memberPins down what it means in the city's own words: what counts as reported, as filled, and over what period. If the city never says, that itself is recorded as "Undetermined, because the city does not define it".
  3. memberGets the city's own records of every report, from its open-data portal or by request (journey 5): when each was reported, when it was closed, and why.
  4. memberWorks out whether the records support the claim, with the method shown: the assistant's calculation labelled as machine work, or a member's own, checked by a second member.Reasoned

  6. memberConcludes with what the evidence supports, for example: "the city's records show 90% closed; of 40 closed reports members visited, 11 were not repaired". Its strength is shown against the project's bar.Reasoned
  7. ownerPublishes the case or acts on it (journeys 7 and 13).

5. Wizards

Outlines only: what each wizard covers, where it starts, and its steps. A step may offer an optional side trip into another wizard; when that wizard finishes, or is stopped, the member returns to the step they left. Any wizard reachable as a side trip can also be run on its own later. Each step says what to do and why; a wizard points at the real control and never presses it; what it puts in a field is a labelled draft until the member keeps it; stopping is fine. The three required wizards must always work, or the release is held.

Required

Set up and claim

Starts at: The installer page, then the setup page
  1. Choose your language, from the languages Civicsmith comes with.
  2. Before you start: a Cloudflare account with a payment method, a Claude subscription account, about twenty minutes.
  3. Choose the short name. Why: it appears in addresses and signatures, and can never change; a group that wants to stay unnamed picks one that reveals nothing.
  4. Install and let it test itself.
  5. Claim it with the one-time password; choose your password.
  6. Name the group, add a logo if you have one, verify your web address, choose your places and languages.
  7. Record who holds the hosting account. Optionally add a second administrator. Why: so the group is never stuck if one person is away.
  8. Optional side trip: say who your group is (its own wizard), then come back here to finish. You can also do it later.
Required · outline only for now

Welcome a new member

Starts at: The invitation link
  1. Choose your language. Why: everything that follows, including what your choices mean, should be in words you read easily; it starts from your device's setting.
  2. Choose a handle. Why: the record shows it on your work. It needn't be your legal name. A name people know lends your work credibility and lets reporters reach you, but puts it on public cases; a pen name shields you from pressure, and your administrators still know who you are. You can also choose, act by act, to be credited only as the group.
  3. This is your group's home: what it is working on, and what is waiting on you.
  4. Two things to know first: a question's strength against its project's bar, and "Undetermined".
  5. Your first act: join a project, capture a document, or record what you saw.
Required

Publication ceremony

Starts at: A case ready to publish
  1. What you are publishing: the case, its edition, its scope.
  2. Check: each finding's strength against the bar; anything still open.
  3. What the case leaves out, and who acknowledged it.
  4. The group's declared bias.
  5. Preview the public page, as a stranger will see it.
  6. Sign with your key. Why: publishing is permanent; corrections come as a new edition.
Optional · offer first

Get a record

Starts at: Any office, or a question that needs records
  1. Look first: is it already public? Search the office's site and other public sources; if you find it, capture it here and you are done.
  2. If not: which office holds the records.
  3. What records, in plain words.
  4. The law it goes under, from your group's profile.
  5. Your draft, labelled as a draft: edit it until it is yours.
  6. Send it. Why the warning: this tells the office what you are looking at.
  7. The due date is set and shows in your queue.
Optional

Check a claim

Starts at: A claim the city made, captured
  1. What exactly does the city claim, and where did it say so?
  2. What do its words mean: what counts, over what period? If it never says, record that.
  3. Get the city's own records behind the claim: online, or by request.
  4. Work out whether they support it, with the method shown, and have a second member check.
  5. Plan a spot-check: which sample of records members will visit, and how they record what they find.
Optional · offer first

Your first question

Starts at: A project page
  1. What drew you in? A problem you live with (a street, a service, a bill), or something you read: a law or code, a court case, a news story, a budget, a meeting, a tip. Start from there.
  2. What do you want to know? Say it as a question; the assistant can suggest some from what you started with.
  3. Find or capture a document that bears on it.
  4. Point at the passage that matters.
  5. Say whether it supports the question or cuts against it.
  6. See the question's strength against the project's bar, and what would raise it.
Optional

Say who your group is

Starts at: The end of setup, or the group's settings at any time
  1. What kind of group are you? Pick one or more, or describe your own.
  2. What do you focus on: issues, places, offices or agencies?
  3. Why does the group exist, in your own words?
  4. Who should see this: members only, or also your public page and the network directory?
Optional

Invite a member

Starts at: The roster
  1. Who: their handle, and the cover the group knows them by (not a legal name).
  2. What they may do: contribute, by default.
  3. Send the link; it waits for them.
Optional

Use another group's case

Starts at: A link to a published case, or the directory
  1. Import the case into a read-only project.
  2. Let Civicsmith recreate each finding.
  3. Accept what recreated, with a reason.
  4. Watch the publisher's docket for new editions.
Optional

Translate the interface

Starts at: Settings, for administrators or members given the grant
  1. Choose the language.
  2. See the words still untranslated.
  3. Ask the assistant to draft them; each is marked "Draft".
  4. Check and adopt each one, or type it yourself.
Optional

Start and send

Starts at: An option on a plan
  1. The option you chose and why.
  2. Prepare what is sent; the assistant may draft it.
  3. Approve it and send it, after the outward-act warning.
  4. The clock and the next checkpoint go into the queue.
