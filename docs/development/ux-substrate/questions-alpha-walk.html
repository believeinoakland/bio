Eight questions from the alpha’s live walks

The walk of the deployed alpha found about a hundred problems. Most are the design’s or the builders’ to fix, and are being fixed. These eight touch what Civicsmith must do or who may see what, so they are yours. Each has options and a recommendation.

All eight answered on October 27. Your answers are recorded and go to the beta's planning through BOB.

The eight questions

  1. Who is a question about?
  2. Whose fiscal year does Money count in?
  3. Who sees where a handed-in file came from?
  4. Can a member look at their own photograph?
  5. Two things called “Capture”, and publishing an ungraded case
  6. Signing keys: queue noise and the device box
  7. Documents from other projects in pickers
  8. Can a typed page be cited once a second member has checked it?

A1. Who is a question about?

Answered October 27

As recommended (A). A question asks, optionally, who it is about when it is opened, and a member can change it later with a reason kept. Recorded as DEC-457.

The issue

A question can name the person or organization it is about. That name is what lets Civicsmith check each passage a member cites against who the passage connects to, and grade it. Without it, every passage reads “Unrated” on the connection measure, however good it is. Today a member can name the subject only when the question is first created through the system’s back door; no screen asks for it, and no one can add or change it later.

How it works today

No screen asks “who is this question about?”. A question without a subject still works, but its strength never counts connections, and nothing tells the member why.

An example

Mai opens the question “Is Public Works repairing reported potholes within seven days?”. She cites the city’s report, which names L. Chen, Director of Public Works. Every passage shows “Unrated” for connection, because the question never said it is about Public Works.

Recommended

A. Ask at opening, and allow it later

When a member opens a question, an optional field asks “Who is this question about?”, choosing from the people and organizations the team follows. A member can add or change it later, with a reason kept in the question’s history. A question with none says so plainly, with the control to name one.

GainsConnections get graded for every question that names a subject; the member understands why “Unrated” shows.
CostsA new act and a change to how questions are stored (the subject becomes changeable, with history).

B. Ask only at opening

The same field when opening a question, but the subject is fixed once chosen.

GainsSmaller build; no history of changes to keep.
CostsEvery existing question stays without a subject for good, and a wrong pick cannot be corrected.

C. Suggest it from the question’s words

Civicsmith proposes a subject from the people and offices the question’s words name; a member confirms it.

GainsLess typing.
CostsToday Civicsmith finds no names in free wording (the walk confirmed it), so this needs new reading work and still needs A’s act.

Recommendation

A. It is the only option that fixes the questions already open, and it keeps the member in charge of what a question is about.

Risk: Members may leave it blank; the plain sentence and the control on the question are what keep that from going unnoticed.

A2. Whose fiscal year does Money count in?

Answered October 27

As recommended (B). Each government's own fiscal year, from the place's records or stated by a member; Money never prints “*”. Recorded as DEC-458.

The issue

Money can group a government’s figures by fiscal year. To do that it needs to know when that fiscal year starts. Today it looks only in the place’s records, and when they hold nothing, the screen prints a bare “*” where the year should be.

How it works today

The fiscal-year view is offered whether or not Civicsmith knows the year, and shows “*” when it does not.

An example

Lakeshore Tenants follows Oakland’s budget. Oakland’s fiscal year runs July to June. If Civicsmith’s records for Oakland do not carry that, Ana picks “by fiscal year” on Money and sees “*”.

A. A fiscal year for the team

A team states its own fiscal year once, in Settings, beside its time zone. Money counts every government’s figures in it.

GainsOne simple setting.
CostsThe figures belong to governments, not to the team: a team following both Oakland (July to June) and the State (also July to June) is fine, but one following a county on another year would see its figures cut wrongly.
Recommended

B. The government’s own fiscal year

Each government’s fiscal year comes from the place’s records. When they hold none, a member states it for that government, with how they checked it, the way the time zone is set. Money offers the fiscal-year view only for governments whose year is known, and never prints “*”.

GainsCorrect for every government; matches how budgets are actually published.
CostsA new stated fact per government, and one more small form.

C. Calendar years only

Drop the fiscal-year view.

GainsNothing to build.
CostsBudgets are adopted by fiscal year; members would have to convert by hand.

Recommendation

B. The year belongs to the government whose money it is, and stating it follows the same pattern you already approved for the time zone.

Risk: For a government no one has stated a year for, the fiscal-year view is simply not offered, which a member may not notice; a sentence on Money says why.

A3. Who sees where a handed-in file came from?

Answered October 27

As recommended (A). Listed members see “A source” on Document and Held; others see “Handed in through the doorbell”. Recorded as DEC-459.

The issue

When someone outside the team hands in a file through the team’s doorbell, Civicsmith keeps who handed it in (“the source”) confidential: only the members the team listed for this can read about the source. Today the link “A source” appears only in the inbox. Once the file is kept, the Held list shows a machine reference instead of the file’s name, and the Document page shows no link at all.

How it works today

Inbox: “A source” links to what is known about whoever knocked, readable by listed members only. Held: “From knock:KNOCK-…”. Document: nothing.

An example

A city employee hands in a leaked memo. Dev, a listed member, can follow “A source” from the inbox. Later Teo, not listed, opens the memo’s Document page and sees nothing about where it came from, which is right; but Dev, on the same page, has lost the link too.

Recommended

A. Show the link only to listed members

Document and Held show “A source” for members the team listed; every other member sees “Handed in through the doorbell” with no link. Held shows the file’s own name.

GainsListed members keep the trail everywhere; the source stays protected from everyone else.
CostsThe page differs by viewer, which a member comparing screens with a colleague may find odd.

B. Keep the link in the inbox only

Fix Held to show the file name; no link anywhere else.

GainsLeast exposure.
CostsListed members have to go back to the inbox to trace a file.

C. Show the link to every member

Every member sees “A source”.

GainsSimplest.
CostsBreaks the confidentiality the team chose for its sources.

Recommendation

A. It keeps the confidentiality rule exactly as it stands while putting the trail where listed members work.

Risk: None beyond the per-viewer difference; the plain sentence for others names that the file came through the doorbell, which they could already infer.

A4. Can a member look at their own photograph?

Answered October 27

“B, but only drop metadata is necessary.” A safe picture redrawn from each photo, dropping only the metadata safety requires; when and where it was taken stays, shown beside it. Recorded as DEC-460.

The issue

Civicsmith never opens a file from outside directly: it shows a “safe view” made from it, so a harmful file cannot act on a member’s device. Safe views exist today for documents, presentations, spreadsheets and plain text. A photograph has none, so a member who photographs a pothole cannot look at the photo in Civicsmith at all; the only path is to open the original on their own device. The screen also says “The safe view needs no check” right under the notice that there is no safe view.

How it works today

“Your team’s Civicsmith makes safe views of documents, presentations, spreadsheets and plain text, and this file is none of them, so it has no safe view.” Only “Open the original on your own device” remains.

An example

Mai photographs a pothole on Seminary Avenue and adds the photo. She opens it to check it is the right one and is told it has no safe view.

A. Keep it; fix the words only

Remove the contradictory sentence; say plainly how to see the photo on one’s own device.

GainsNo build.
CostsMembers still cannot look at the photographs they take, which are central evidence.
Recommended

B. A safe view for pictures

Civicsmith makes a safe picture from each photo (redrawn from its pixels, carrying nothing else from the file) and shows that, as it does for documents.

GainsEvery member can look at every photo safely, the same way as any document.
CostsA new safe-view maker and its security review.

C. The uploader may open their own

A member may open the original of a file they added themselves, once it passed the scan.

GainsSmall change.
CostsAn exception to the rule that originals open only after a deeper check; other members still see nothing.

Recommendation

B, with A’s wording fix right away. Photographs are evidence members need to look at, and a redrawn picture keeps the safe-view rule whole.

Risk: The redrawn picture drops the photo’s hidden data (such as where it was taken), which is what keeps it safe; that data stays in the record’s own copy.

A5. Two things called “Capture”, and publishing an ungraded case

Answered October 27

As recommended (B). The two measures get their own names, and signing lists every ungraded passage; an edition signed with some says so. Recorded as DEC-461.

The issue

The word “Capture” labels two different measures. One is how well the record could have captured a document (a ceiling the record sets). The other is the grade a member gives for how well the copy the team holds shows the passage cited (only a member’s grade counts). A case can be published while that member grade is missing everywhere, and the public page then says “Not graded yet: no member has graded this.” Whether a case may be published that way is your policy question from earlier (the publish gate).

How it works today

The Document page shows “Capture B” (the ceiling). The question says “Not graded yet on Capture”. Nothing sends the member to the fold where they grade.

An example

Dev publishes Edition 2. Every passage’s copy is good (the record allows a B), but no one recorded a grade. Readers see “Capture: Not graded yet” on every finding.

A. Rename, and guide the member

Name the two measures apart (“How well it could be captured” and “Your team’s grade for the copy”), and point the member to the grading fold from the question. Publishing stays open.

GainsClear words; no change to who may publish.
CostsCases can still go out ungraded.
Recommended

B. A and a warning when signing

As A, and the signing screen names every passage still ungraded; the member can grade them there or sign anyway, and the edition says it was published with them ungraded.

GainsMembers choose knowingly; readers are told.
CostsOne more step at signing.

C. A and a block

As A, and an edition cannot be signed until every passage is graded.

GainsEvery published case is graded.
CostsBlocks publishing on bookkeeping; a team in a hurry cannot publish.

Recommendation

B. It keeps publishing in the team’s hands and makes the gap visible both to the member signing and to readers.

Risk: A team may sign past the warning every time; the edition’s own statement is what keeps that honest.

A6. Signing keys: queue noise and the device box

Answered October 27

As recommended (B). One queue item per member for new signing keys, and a sentence beside the box. Recorded as DEC-462.

The issue

When a member makes their own signing key in their browser, every other administrator gets a queue item to confirm they know about it. The box “Keep it on this device” starts unticked, so a key made without it is lost when the browser closes, and the next attempt makes another key and another item. The walker saw five items for one member.

How it works today

One item per key; the box unticked; nothing replaces an earlier key.

An example

Teo signs, does not tick the box, and signs again the next day four times. Ana’s queue holds five “Teo registered a signing key” items.

A. Tick the box by default

The box starts ticked; a member unticks it on a shared computer.

GainsFar fewer lost keys and items.
CostsA member on a library computer who misses the box leaves their key there.
Recommended

B. One item per member

The queue groups a member’s new keys into one item listing each, with when it was made; the box stays unticked, with a sentence saying a key not kept is gone when the browser closes.

GainsA quiet queue without changing what is kept where.
CostsLost keys still pile up in the record (each is still listed).

C. Both

A and B together.

GainsQuietest.
CostsCarries A’s shared-computer risk.

Recommendation

B. It removes the noise without making a choice about devices for the member.

Risk: Members may keep losing keys; the sentence beside the box is what teaches them.

A7. Documents from other projects in pickers

Answered October 27

As recommended (B). This project's documents first; other projects' on request, each labeled. Recorded as DEC-463.

The issue

When a member links a document to another (say, to record a contradiction), the picker lists the member’s own project’s documents first, then every other document they may see, including those of their other projects, by title. Nothing is shown that the member may not see, so this is not a leak; the question is whether drawing links across projects should be that easy.

How it works today

One long list: this project’s documents, then the rest, mixed.

An example

Mai works on Pothole repairs and on The Coliseum lease. Linking a pothole report, she sees the lease amendment in the same list and could link it by mistake.

A. Keep one list

Rule today’s behavior intended.

GainsNo work.
CostsLong, mixed lists; links across projects by mistake.
Recommended

B. This project first, the rest on request

The picker shows this project’s documents; “Show documents from your other projects” adds the rest, each labeled with its project.

GainsA deliberate choice when crossing projects; shorter lists.
CostsOne more click when a link across projects is meant.

C. This project only

No links across projects.

GainsSimplest.
CostsBlocks real connections, such as a city report bearing on two projects.

Recommendation

B. Links across projects stay possible but deliberate, and each document names its project.

Risk: Small: a member looking for a document in another project needs the extra click.

A8. Can a typed page be cited once a second member has checked it?

Answered October 27

“B, with the proper grade assigned. Having a review further attest to a reading is better than not, but still only incrementally better.” A typed page can be cited at once, graded as its typist's word; a second member's check raises the grade by one step only. Recorded as DEC-464.

The issue

When Civicsmith cannot read a scanned page, a member can type it in. Until a second member checks the typing against the page, the screen says it “counts for no more than” the typist’s word. Once checked, the screen says “Checked by Ana, October 26”. But no rule says whether a checked typing can be cited passage by passage, as text Civicsmith read itself can, and the screen offers no Cite control on typed words at all. This touches what counts as evidence, so it is yours.

How it works today

Typed words are shown as a plain quotation with no Cite. Civicsmith’s grading rules already let a second member’s check count toward how much a typed page can be trusted, and never let the typist’s own check count.

An example

Page 4 of the 2019 Coliseum lease is a faint scan. Dev types the clause on rent adjustments; Ana checks it against the page image. Mai wants to cite that clause in her answer to “Were the 2024 adjustments made under the lease?”, and cannot.

Recommended

A. Citable once a second member has checked it

A checked typing can be cited like any passage. The citation carries “typed by Dev, checked by Ana” and never presents the words as Civicsmith’s reading or as the original.

GainsThe check is what makes it evidence, as your grading rules already treat it; faint pages become usable.
CostsA team of one can never cite a typed page; a check against a faint scan can still be wrong.

B. Citable at once, labeled

A typing can be cited as soon as it is typed, labeled “typed by Dev, not yet checked”, and graded as unrated until checked.

GainsWork is never blocked.
CostsWeaker evidence enters answers, and readers may overlook the label.

C. Never citable

Members cite only the page image; the typing helps reading but is never evidence.

GainsThe strictest standard.
CostsFaint pages cannot serve as evidence at all, and the check does nothing.

Recommendation

A. It follows the rule already on screen: a typing counts for no more than its typist’s word until someone else checks it.

Risk: a careless check; the citation names both members, so readers can weigh it.

One line, for example A1 A, A2 B, A3 A, A4 B, A5 B, A6 B, A7 B, A8 A, or “all as recommended”. Change any letter, or write a sentence where none fits.
