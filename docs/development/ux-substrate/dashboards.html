Dashboards as the way into each part of Civicsmith

You asked the design lane to research how Civicsmith could open onto a high-level dashboard for each major area, the way a car's screens open onto driving, navigation, climate and settings. This page reports what the research found, what changes from the screens you have now, and three ways to do it, with an early recommendation.

Decided 14 October (S20: A). One home for everyone, arrangement C: what needs you first, then each area at a glance. What you asked for

"Dashboard" like interfaces that is the high level primary entry point for major functional areas of the system like investigation, action, membership, administration, and so forth. These dashboards are organizationally similar to modern automotive driver experiences that have high level dashes for driving, navigation, environment control, entertainment, vehicle settings, and so forth.

An earlier reading of "and so forth" added publication as a fifth area. You said no on 14 October: publication is what an investigation produces, so a case's state (in review, waiting to be signed, published) sits under Investigation. That gives four areas: Investigation, Action, Membership and Administration.

Your two points of 13 October, evening

"The home dashboard needs to be a real home, not just a set of buttons to the other dashboards. It has to have a nerve-center like completeness." "The dashboard screens should have the rail like all the other screens, though it might be collapsed."

Taken as two rules for every treatment. First, the home is complete in itself: what needs you, what needs the group, every area's state (its readings, what needs you in it, a line of what changed), the projects, and what members did since you last looked. An area's dashboard is one step away for the detail, but nothing is found only by going there. Second, every dashboard keeps the rail every other screen has (Home, Queue, Find, Add, Projects, People, Settings); where a dashboard wants the room, the rail collapses to icons, as any member can already make it (its width is theirs). The rail never turns into the list of areas.

What that changed below. Model A as first drawn, five cards that were doors, is withdrawn: a card per area stays only as a complete block. Model B as first drawn, no home and the rail replaced by the areas, is withdrawn too. The three models are now three orders of the same complete home, so the choice left to you (S20) is which order a member meets first, not what the home contains. The screens page draws all three as states of one screen, with the rail on every one.

What changes from today's screens

The screens on the layouts page are organized by what you do or what a thing is, not by area. The rail down the left reads: Home, Queue, Find, Add, Projects, People, Settings. Your direction groups the same places by area instead. Most screens stay as they are; what changes is the front door to each group of them.

What the research found

From cars: the structure transfers, the dials don't

Every current system studied (Android Automotive in Polestar and Volvo, Tesla, Rivian, BMW's new iDrive, Mercedes, Apple's CarPlay Ultra) keeps a few critical readings in one fixed place that never moves, whatever else is on screen. The areas are few and stay where they are, and each home tile shows a live state plus one or two controls, so the common action needs no trip inside. Mercedes' screen that rearranges itself by guessing what you need is the exception. European safety ratings and BMW are moving away from it, because people can't build habits on a screen that moves.

For Civicsmith that means borrowing the car's structure: a small fixed status zone, a few areas in fixed places, and cards that carry state and an action. It means leaving out the car's look. Usability research is clear that gauges and dials are a poor way to show numbers on a screen.

The driving-safety limits (no glance over 2 seconds, no task over 12) don't apply at a desk. They translate into two tests for every dashboard: a volunteer sees what needs them within about five seconds, and does the commonest next step in three steps or fewer.

From work software: the home page has become "your work", not charts

GitHub made a new home page its default on 1 October 2026. It shows your own open work, capped at twelve items a list, and moves the activity feed to a tab of its own. Linear's home is an inbox, and in September 2026 it added a tab that separates what needs you now from what can wait. ServiceNow describes each area's landing page as "actionable data to help the user orient their day". The long-standing advice from dashboard designers is the same: a wall of number tiles with no threshold or action gets ignored.

Each area's dashboard would therefore follow one pattern in three tiers: status first (two to four readings, each tied to a threshold and opening its list), then what needs you (a short list, each item with its action on it), then everything else (recent work, and the way into the area's lists). There would be no charts to start with. Each dashboard would also have a written empty state for a new group, saying what will appear there and offering the one first step.

From records-request and legal tools: whose move, and when

MuckRock's statuses separate requests waiting on the requester ("Fix Required", "Payment Required") from those waiting on the agency ("Awaiting Response"). The agencies' own records-request software sorts by due soon and overdue, on a holiday-aware calendar, and emails staff a daily list. Legal practice software derives a chain of deadlines from one triggering event under a jurisdiction's rules, which is how Civicsmith's clocks already work.

Every item in Action would carry Our move or Their move, and a clock would read as a sentence with its date and its rule, never as a countdown:

Received 13 October. The city must answer by Friday 23 October: 10 days under the Public Records Act (Gov. Code § 7922.535). It may extend once, by written notice, by up to 14 days, to Friday 6 November.Their move · on track

Many volunteers won't open Civicsmith on a schedule. A daily or weekly email that mirrors "what needs you" would reach them; you ruled it out on 14 October: Civicsmith does not email members. Nothing reaches a member outside the app; the home is where what needs them waits, and a reminder exists only because a member asked for it (principle 1.3).

The examples you named on 13 October

monday.com. A work-management tool whose dashboards are a wall of widgets drawn from one or more "boards" (its tables of work): charts, a Numbers tile that sums a column against a goal, a Battery showing overall progress, and a Gantt timeline, each pulling live from the boards. Its own pitch aims them at leaders ("executive summary reports… instant clarity on progress and priorities"), with the work itself done on the boards: a dashboard summarizes, and a click takes you to the board where you act. Two lessons. The structure is sound: a few readings from live data, each opening the list behind it, and no second place to do the work. The audience is not ours: monday's dashboards are for the manager looking over a team's week; Civicsmith's are for the volunteer who comes in once a week to find what is theirs. So the Numbers-and-Battery tiles belong on an area's dashboard (how many questions are open, how far the case has come), while the home stays "what needs you".

Incredable (healthcare credentialing) and ops.work (onboarding and compliance for small healthcare employers). Neither site describes its screens in words, but both are built around the same job, and it is close to ours: a set of items each with a status and an expiry date, where the whole point of the front page is to show what is missing, pending or about to expire. Incredable tracks each credential "from submission to approval", sends expiration alerts 30, 60 or 90 days ahead, and reports on time-to-credential; an ops.work customer praises "the clear dashboard highlighting any missing/pending items". That is the compliance-tracker pattern, and it is Model C's "needs you" list by another name: a records request due Friday, a reply overdue, a review window closing, a document waiting to be vouched for, each with its date and whose move it is. The lesson is to lead with exceptions and dates, not with totals, and to say how far ahead a member is warned.

Lovable's dashboard templates. Ten starter apps for people building software with Lovable: a finance dashboard, cash flow, budget against actual, customer health, sales attribution, a supply-chain simulator, and so on. They are chart-first (a beeswarm of customer scores, a butterfly revenue funnel, cohort trends), which is the right shape for a finance team and the wrong one for us: they are the "wall of number tiles" the research above warns against, unless each reading opens a list and an action. The two that look most like Civicsmith are the ones that pair a reading with a queue: the Approval Tracker (a form feeding a manager's review queue) and the Budget dashboard (planned against actual, with the variance named). A third-party guide to such templates adds the layout rules we already follow: predictable menus, the same layout on every view, the most critical readings first with the rest behind a toggle, and touch-sized controls on a phone.

What the four change. Nothing in the three models, and they strengthen the recommendation: the compliance trackers are Model C's home, monday's widgets are what an area dashboard's status tier should hold (two to four live readings, each opening its list), and the chart-first templates show what to avoid on a volunteer's front page. One refinement: where a car fixes the critical readings in one place, the credentialing tools add how far ahead you are warned (30, 60, 90 days). Civicsmith's clocks should carry that too: "due in 9 days" with the warning distance the group chose.

Accessibility and phones

WCAG 2.2, the standard Civicsmith meets, puts limits on a status zone that stays on screen. It must never cover what a keyboard user is focused on. The page must still work at 320 pixels wide. Due soon and overdue need words or an icon, not color alone, and a reading that changes must be announced to screen readers. A phone's bottom bar holds three to five places. With four areas, a home and the four fit; the screens as drawn keep the bar as Home, Queue, Add, Find and More, with Members and Administration under More (they are used least, as a car keeps vehicle settings behind its launcher), and the status zone shrinks to one chip, such as "3 need you", that opens a sheet.

Three ways to do it

All three share what the research and your two points settle: one complete home with the rail; four areas in fixed places, each with a dashboard in the three tiers above, one step from the home; "whose move" and due-soon and overdue on every action item; a written empty state everywhere; and the accessibility rules above. They differ in what a member meets first and how dense the first screen is.

Each area first, complete, with what needs you inside it

What needs you first, then each area at a glance

The home opens on one list in two parts, Needs you and Needs the group: clocks to act on, letters to approve, documents to check, versions to decide on, replies to read, cases to sign, invitations; sorted by urgency and by whose move it is, each item carrying its act. Then every area's block, complete, as in A; the projects; what members did since you last looked. The strip stays on every screen, and the four area dashboards are one step away for the detail.

Gains

  • Best for a volunteer who comes in once or twice a week: see what's theirs, do it, leave; the whole group's state is still on the page
  • Work that crosses areas sits side by side (a reply, the finding it bears on, the follow-up letter)
  • Where GitHub and Linear have moved this year

Costs

  • The areas come second, so the home has to be named well as the group's state, not a to-do list
  • It needs firm rules for what enters the list, snoozing, and who owns a shared item

Risk

The list fills with noise unless items have owners and a way to wait. Signing or sending from the list must still go through the full dialog, never one tap.

Decided: one home for everyone, arrangement C (S20: A, 14 October)

Your answer of 14 October: "one home for everyone, arrangement C". You asked first whether all three could be supported, selectable by the member; they can, cheaply, since the three are the same blocks in three orders, and that choice is held in reserve: one shared home is easier for a group to talk about and to keep coherent, and the choice can be added later without undoing anything, if the test instance shows members missing another order. Models A and B below stay as the comparison that was made, set aside.

The recommendation was Model C, with the strip kept to four readings. Most Civicsmith members are volunteers who come in between other things. For them, the first question is "what do I need to do?", and only after that "how is the group doing?". C answers the first on arrival and the second on the same page, in every area's block, with a dashboard per area one step away for the detail. The strip carries the one thing a car gets right that matters here: a legal deadline that is ours to meet stays in view wherever you are. After your two points the three differ only in order and density, so trying C costs nothing that A or B would need back.

The risk is the one each model shares in a different form: a front page that fills with things nobody acts on. The guard is the same in each: every reading has a threshold, and every item has an owner, an action, and a way to wait.

How I'd test it before asking you to choose: sketch the home and the Action dashboard on the layouts page, then walk them with three to five volunteers against the two tests (what needs me within five seconds; the commonest next step in three steps or fewer). Then decide whether the strip earns its place.
