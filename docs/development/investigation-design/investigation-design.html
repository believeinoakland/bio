2 · Your direction so far

In your words, newest first. Where a later word changes an earlier one, the later governs.

"AI-based features are seen by some groups as so expensive that their use needs to be justified by the (potential) value they provide. They need to be judicious about that use. And yet, the AI-based capabilities of the system offer significant benefits related to: a more friendly, efficient, and effective UX using the natural-language capabilities; the level of member support the system can provide; interviewing members when they identify a new or evolving challenge they want investigated; a smart, proactive role they can take on formulating member input into questions and other structures on which investigations are based.

Up to this point, we've added support for groups and individual members to specify which AI account they use, put limits on the cost of usage, specify which AI-based civicsmith features are enabled, and track usage over time. But as we explore the investigation design, I think we need to recognize that some projects may have additional resources available or have different priorities (whether higher and lower) after affects which account is used, what limits to put on usage, and which AI-based capabilities are enabled.

The assistant is based on Claude, a frontier AI service. Depending on feedback from groups related to the quality and cost, future releases may be able to support a range of AIs, that includes frontier and open models. So I think we need to recognize the continuum of interest across groups in using AI-based capabilities. Some groups won't use it at all, while other groups may want to maximize the benefits AI offers in Civicsmith. There will almost certain also be situations where the interests of the group and some of its members are different.

I think that the system should recognize and support this diversity of AI usage, and give groups and members who are using their own accounts to select which types of uses to enable, put limits on the maximum cost, specify which AI-based civicsmith features are enabled, and track usage over time."

3 · The model as it stands

Who does what

Members

  • pose questions under the project's objective
  • take steps on a question, or for the project
  • hold hunches and hypotheses; follow leads
  • close a step: its outcome, what it produced, what was learned
  • tie a chance find to the step it helped

The system, including the AI

  • takes steps on a question: searching, capturing, reading
  • has its own hunches, hypotheses and leads, labelled as its own
  • judges how a find bears on the question: supporting it, or contradicting what is gathered
  • offers what it found to the members working on the question
A member's act decidesdismiss the information, or add it to the evidence on the question, by its grade and the rules of evidence

4 · Questions

Open now

Background for D38 and D39 · AI use as it stands after your answer

What is built today. A group chooses at setup how its members use the assistant: the group's key (an API key held by an administrator), each member's own subscription (used only by that member), each member's own API key, or no AI (K1755). Each member sets her own daily limit, counted in tokens and calls. An administrator may set a lower limit for the whole copy. Use is tracked per member, per day and per kind of AI work. Administrators see each month's totals by kind of work, naming no member; each member sees only her own (K1450).

What your answer adds.

  • A project may have its own AI settings: which account its AI work uses, its limit, and which uses are on. One project may have a grant-funded key and want everything on; another may want AI kept to a minimum. Its owners set these, since authority over a project is theirs. This replaces the earlier idea of a separate AI budget per investigation (D31).
  • Settings are by type of use: conversation, member support, the intake interview, turning members' words into questions, reading documents, exploring questions, and so on. The exact list is a detail of the design.
  • Limits are on maximum cost. Today they are counted in tokens and calls. A money limit can be exact for API keys, but a subscription's provider does not report cost per use, so for a subscription the limit stays in tokens and calls.
  • Later releases may offer other AI models, frontier and open. Settings should not assume Claude alone.

Two questions follow from that.

D38 · When group, project and member AI settings disagree, which governs

Why it arises. You expect "situations where the interests of the group and some of its members are different". With settings at three levels, each able to turn uses on or off and set limits, they will conflict. The system needs one rule for which one applies.

A distinction that matters. A setting can exist for one of two reasons.

  • Money: "don't spend our group key on exploring". This is about whose account pays.
  • The material: "don't send this project's documents to an AI service at all". Such a setting protects, for example, a hidden project holding a whistleblower's papers. It is about what the AI may see, whoever pays.

Examples.

  • (1) The group turns off exploring to save its key. Ana, on her own subscription, wants it on for her question in "Grandview rebuild".
  • (2) Grandview's owners turn off AI reading of documents because the project holds a whistleblower's papers. Ana, on her own subscription, wants the AI to read one of them.
  • (3) Grandview has a grant-funded key with a $200 limit. Ben's own daily limit is lower. Ben starts a run on the project's key.
  1. A · The narrowest setting wins. Each level can only narrow the one above. In (1) Ana cannot explore. In (2) she cannot have the document read. In (3) the lower of the two limits applies. Simple and safe, but a member with her own means is held to the group's budget choices.
  2. B · Whoever pays decides. Settings bind only work paid from that level's account. In (1) Ana explores. In (2) Ana's AI reads the whistleblower's document, because the project's setting does not bind her account. In (3) the project's limit applies to the project key. This respects members' own means, but leaves the project unable to protect its material.
  3. C · Split by reason. The group and each project may set limits on the material, which bind everyone. Money limits bind only the account they belong to, and each payer decides what her own money is spent on. In (1) Ana explores. In (2) the document stays unread. In (3) the project key's $200 limit applies, and Ben's own limit applies only to his own account.

Recommended: C. It supports the range of AI use you described, so a member with her own account is not held back by the group's budget. It still lets a group or a project protect what its material is exposed to.

D39 · Exploring without being asked each time, where that use is enabled

Why it arises. You said the system may "proactively explore a question" (D33). Your answer on AI use makes exploring one of the uses a group, a project or a member can turn on, within a cost limit. Your ruling of 5 October (K1481) says every AI run starts with a member's act, except a standing question a member writes, which re-runs on a schedule and only reads. Exploring is more than reading: it searches, captures documents and judges them. If exploring is turned on, can the system start it by itself?

Example. Grandview's owners turn on exploring for the project, on the project's key, with a $50 monthly limit. The question "When did the district first know the rebuild would run late?" is open. Under A, the system notices a new board agenda, explores it overnight, and offers two finds the next morning, labelled as its work and naming who turned exploring on. It stops when the $50 is used and says so once.

  1. A · Yes, where enabled. Whoever pays turns exploring on for a project or a question. A cost limit must be set first; exploring cannot be turned on without one. Within that, the system explores when it judges it useful. Every run is labelled with who enabled it. It stops at the limit and says so once.
  2. B · Only on a schedule a member sets. A member sets a question to be explored, say weekly until a set date, as a standing question does today but exploring instead of only reading. The system does not choose when to explore.
  3. C · No. Every exploring run still starts with a member pressing a button.

Trade-offs. A is the proactive role you described and the most useful while members wait on records. It revises K1481, but keeps a person's choice and a cost limit in front of every run. B keeps more control but makes members do the scheduling. C changes nothing.

Recommended: A, with the fences stated: enabled only by whoever pays, never without a cost limit, labelled, and stopping at the limit.

D35 · Where steps that serve no question are kept

Background. You said a step is work done in pursuit of an answer to a question (D32). Steps on a question are kept with the question. A question can be shared by several projects, so those steps are seen from every project that draws on it. You also said some steps serve no particular question: "to provide context for members, in the development of a question, to understand how to respond to findings, and so on". Those steps need a home, and the home decides who sees them.

The two homes that exist. The project is seen by its joined members only, and a hidden project stays invisible to everyone else. The group is seen by every member of the group.

Examples.

  • "Read the district's last three budgets to understand how capital projects are paid for": context for this investigation.
  • "Talk to the parent who first raised the delay, to work out what we should be asking": helps form a question. Once the question exists, the step can be linked to it.
  • "Find out what other districts did when a rebuild ran a year late": helps decide how to respond to findings. Today the action plan is where a project weighs what to do about its findings: options, their pros and cons, and the members' choice.
  1. A · The project. Such steps belong to the project they were taken for. A step that formed a question is linked to that question once it exists. Work on how to respond to findings may be a step on the project, or recorded in the action plan.
  2. B · The group. Such steps are kept for the whole group, seen from every project.

Trade-offs. The group home makes context reusable, but it would expose work done for a hidden project, which is a reason projects can be hidden at all. Knowledge with lasting value for every project is already kept where the group can reuse it: documents in the record, and later the reading guides. A step records the work, not the knowledge.

Recommended: A. Work stays where it was done and is seen by the people doing it; what it produced remains in the record for anyone allowed to see it.

D36 · Who receives what the system finds

Background. Under your D33, the system offers potentially useful finds to "members working on a question". An offer is one item in a member's queue, the list of things waiting for her in the product. It is told once, with no email and no repeats (DEC-69, DEC-94). A question can be drawn on by several projects, each with its own members. Someone has to decide which members' queues an offer reaches. Too many recipients becomes noise; too few means finds go unseen.

Example. The question "Did change orders exceed the limit the law sets?" is drawn on by "Grandview rebuild" (five members) and "District bond oversight" (three members). Two of the eight have taken steps on it. The system finds the district's change-order log.

  1. A · Everyone in every project drawing on the question. All eight get the offer.
  2. B · Only those actively on it. Only the two who took steps, plus anyone who chose to follow the question.
  3. C · Everyone, with a choice. All eight by default, and each member may stop following a question she does not care about, or start following one she does.

Trade-offs. A reaches everyone who might care, but a member in a large project could be offered finds on questions she never touches. B is quiet, but a member who has not yet taken a step misses finds she would want. C starts from A and lets each member trim it. In every option, a member receives only what she is already allowed to see.

Recommended: C. Nobody misses a find by default, and nobody is held to a question she has left.

5 · Research

What the study set out (6 October)

Fourteen realistic investigations, from a school rebuild (your Grandview example) to a sewer-fee diversion, five best-practice papers, six capability studies and three reviews. The proposal: four new parts (a library of reading guides, a record of why each document is held, the plan, the investigation view), one AI planner that fetches nothing, and one AI flow that reads one held document for one purpose. Everything else adds to existing parts. Eight stages, the first three with no AI. Cost per investigation, as an order of magnitude only: about $15–40 small, $100–300 for a Grandview-sized case, $300–1,000 or more over years.

What the product has today

  • Questions with evidence links (legs), alternative accounts (basis versions), claims, conclusions with a falsifier, strength against the project's bar, contradictions, hypotheses, dated waits.
  • Documents captured with provenance and grade, read by a tiered reader with OCR; ZIP records productions unpacked; search inside a document.
  • Objectives with computed progress and gaps; promises and deadlines with their basis; a timeline of what happened and what we did; watching of sources.
  • Cases published by a project's owner; determinations against standards; action plans and actions with legal clocks.
  • No step of investigative work anywhere. The nearest: actions (outward only), the action plan's steps (after a finding), to-dos (routed by the system), recheck triggers on a question, objective gaps, and your deferred "backward question" ("what else would have to be true?").

Words that mean more than one thing

basis

  1. a question's legs
  2. one named version of them
  3. why a date is due
  4. what an action rests on
  5. what a proposal rests on
  6. a member's grounds for her testimony

finding

  1. a concluded question
  2. something the machine noticed
  3. a check's result
  4. a recorded absence
  5. a missing document in a sequence

claim

  1. the statement on a basis version
  2. what a published case asserts
  3. a case's completeness statement
  4. a legal claim

plan and step

  1. the action plan and its steps
  2. the "Plan" inside one action
  3. "an action: a step someone takes"
  4. wizard and progress steps

The design should give members one meaning each for "finding" and "basis", with other words for the rest (owed to the design stream).

Rules your plan of 7 October touched

  • Your "no budgets" of 29 September (for action plans: no costs, assignees or hours).
  • The Capability Ladders' "no drift into BI, case management or dashboards". My reading: a plan serving the investigation is within it; timesheets, workload dashboards and scores on members are not.
  • No measuring members (DEC-68), no nagging (DEC-69, DEC-94), no record of who opened which file (K1892).
