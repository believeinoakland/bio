2 · The design

Everything here follows from your rulings or from canon already in force. Where I have settled a detail myself to keep the design consistent, it is marked detail and can be changed.

2.1 · An investigation

  • An investigation is a project. A project has an objective, what it is trying to establish. It pursues the objective by posing questions, which may or may not be answered.
  • A question can serve several projects and objectives. Each project has its own relationship with a question: it draws on it, it may hold its own conclusion on it, and it may defer or dismiss it. A project's deferral or dismissal never affects another project.
  • A question never shows which projects draw on it. A member sees the question, the work on it and whether it has been answered, never who else is asking.
  • A project's contents are seen only by its participants, whether or not it is hidden. A non-hidden project's name is seen across the group so others can ask to join. A hidden project is invisible to everyone outside it.

2.2 · Steps

  • A step is work done in pursuit of an answer to a question. It is also work done to give members context, to develop a question, or to understand how to respond to findings.
  • Who does it: a member, or the system, including the AI.
  • Where it is, and who sees it. A step is seen wherever it is taken; nobody chooses.
    • On a question: seen by everyone who can see the question, with the member's handle, naming no project.
    • In a project: project-specific work, such as the intake interview, seen by that project's participants.
    • For the group: general context outside any one project, seen across the group.
  • When it ends, a step records its outcome (it helped answer the question, it was a dead end, or it was set aside), what it produced, and, optionally, what was learned in the member's words. Dead ends are kept, because they can still add to understanding.
  • What it produces stays in the record whatever the outcome: documents, content, connections and other records, and looks, including "looked, absent".
  • A chance find needs no step. A member may later tie it to the step it helped.
  • Dependencies: a step may wait on another step, on a document arriving, or on a date. It shows what it is waiting on and opens when that is done. A loop is refused. detail
  • By when: a step may carry a date with its basis: a law's clock, a meeting, or the member's own choice. A nearing date changes only how it looks; an overdue one is told once. detail, from your notification rules

2.3 · Evidence: members decide

Members

  • pose questions and take steps
  • hold hunches and hypotheses; follow leads
  • close steps with their outcome
  • decide what becomes evidence
  • conclude, publish, act

The system, including the AI

  • takes steps: searching, capturing, reading
  • explores questions where an account owner has turned exploring on
  • has its own hunches, hypotheses and leads, labelled as its own
  • judges how a find bears on a question, supporting it or contradicting what is gathered
  • offers finds to the members working on the question
A member's act decidesdismiss the information, or add it to the evidence on the question, by its grade and the rules of evidence
  • The system's judgement of a find is a labelled signal, shown with how it was worked out and its measured rate of false alarms. It is never a grade, never a stored score, and never used to hide or rank what members see.
  • The system's hypotheses are never facts. A member may take one up as her own.
  • Who receives a find: the joined members of every project that draws on the question. Each receives it once, in her queue, never by email. Each may stop following a question, or start following one. Nobody receives anything she may not already see.
  • The system's finds are labelled as the system's work. Which account paid for them is shown only to that account's owners. detail: so that a find never reveals which project asked

2.5 · AI use

Groups vary from no AI at all to wanting all it can offer, and a group and its members may differ. The design supports that range.

  • Account owners: the group, each project and each member can have an AI account. Today that means the group's key held by an administrator, a member's own subscription (used only by her), or a member's own API key. A project may have its own key, set up by its owners, for example one funded by a grant. Later releases may offer other models, frontier and open.
  • Types of use are switched on or off: conversation, member support, the intake interview, turning members' words into questions, reading documents, exploring questions, and others as they are built.
  • Limits: each account owner sets an overall limit and may set a limit per type of use. A per-use limit is either inclusive, counted within the overall limit, or exclusive, a separate allowance on top of it. A limit is in money where the account reports cost. A subscription does not report it, so there the limit is counted in tokens and calls.
  • Exploring has its own setting per account owner:
    • No: not paid from this account. It does not pass to anyone else's setting.
    • Ask every day: the owner gets one queue item a day, only when something is worth exploring, listing the questions and a rough cost. No answer means no.
    • Yes: the system explores when it judges it useful, within the limits.
    It is set once per account, with an override where a project or question is open.
  • Two kinds of "no". A limit about the material, "no AI at all", binds everyone whoever pays: a group's, or a project's for its own material, for example a whistleblower's papers. A money limit, "not on my dime", binds only the account it belongs to. A member spending her own account is never held back by anyone else's budget, only by a "no AI" on the material.
  • A project's "no AI" covers what is its own: its project steps, notes and interview, and any question or document only it draws on. Material it shares with other projects follows the group's setting, since a project cannot tell what others are doing with it. detail: keeps one project from blocking, and so revealing itself to, another
  • Tracking: use is tracked per account, per type of use, over time. A member sees her own; a project's owners see the project account's; administrators see the group key's totals by type of use, naming no member.

2.6 · What holds throughout

  • Who did what is recorded by handle. Nothing measures, scores or ranks a member, and there is no record of who opened which file.
  • No nagging: a reminder is one the member asked for; a nearing date changes only how it looks; an overdue one is told once; nothing goes by email or push.
  • Working material (steps, notes, the interview, the project's thinking) is never published.
  • The group's own dates are never findings about government.
  • The machine proposes; a member accepts.

3 · Grandview, start to finish

How the design works for a parent whose child's school rebuild is a year late.

  1. Maria opens a project, "Grandview rebuild". Its objective: establish why the rebuild is late and who knew when. The intake interview is a step in the project, done with the assistant or by a script.
  2. From her answers come questions. "When did the district first know the rebuild would run late?" is new. "Did change orders exceed the legal limit?" already exists, because a district-oversight project asked it. Maria's project draws on it too, without either project seeing the other.
  3. Maria takes steps. One is a records request for the construction manager's reports, on the first question, with the law's ten-day clock as its "by when". Another is reading the bond measure for background, a project step.
  4. Grandview has a small grant-funded key, with exploring set to "Ask every day" and a $30 exploring limit inclusive in $100 a month. One morning Maria's queue asks whether to explore two questions, roughly $3. She says yes. The system finds three board agendas, judges one as cutting against the district's account, and offers it.
  5. Maria adds that agenda to the evidence as a leg. She dismisses another find with a reason. The oversight project's members are offered the same finds on the shared question, labelled as the system's work, not as Grandview's.
  6. A step ends as a dead end: the state architect's tracker had nothing. Its outcome, what was looked at, and "the tracker only covers projects over $10m" are kept.
  7. The district's documents arrive. Maria ties a document a neighbour forwarded by chance to the records-request step.
  8. The members conclude on the first question at the project's bar. The other project may conclude differently. If the two conclusions cannot both hold, each side is told only that something it rests on conflicts with a record it cannot see.
  9. The project publishes a case and plans what to do about it.

4 · Open questions

D27 · Milestones

What exists. Many dates already exist, each with its basis: a records request's legal clock, a promise or contract date in the record, a wait on a question, a step's own "by when". The project's computed stage (forming, investigating, matured) is the only milestone today.

What you asked for on 7 October: milestones, named points such as "contract and change orders in hand before the 14 November board meeting".

Why it is a question now. One question can serve several projects, each on its own timeline. Grandview needs the change-order answer before the November board meeting. The oversight project needs it only for its March report.

  1. A · A project's milestones. A project's members set named, dated milestones. Each lists the questions or steps it waits on and is met when they are done. Another project sharing a question sets its own.
  2. B · Milestones may also sit on a question, seen by everyone who can see the question.
  3. C · No milestones: only step dates and the computed stage.

Trade-offs. A lets each project keep its own timetable without imposing it. B shows one project's dates to others who do not share them, and hints that another project exists, against your principle for shared questions. C loses what you asked for.

Recommended: A.

D28 · Removing steps

Background. On 7 October you said steps are "deleted because no longer necessary". Since then you agreed that a step keeps its outcome, what it produced and what was learned, because even dead ends add to understanding. So a step that was worked on stays. What is left is a step that was planned and never started. Example: "check the state architect's tracker", made pointless when the documents arrived another way.

  1. A · An untouched step can be deleted outright. Nothing was done, so nothing is lost.
  2. B · It is withdrawn with a reason and stays readable.

Trade-offs. A keeps the list of work realistic, as you wanted. B keeps a record of intentions that rarely matters, and clutters questions many projects share.

Recommended: A.

D29 · Resources a step needs and uses

Background. On 7 October you named people, money and other material resources, information, connections, expertise and AI budget. Most are now covered:

  • AI: section 2.5.
  • Information: what a step seeks and produces.
  • Expertise: members' declared expertise and the existing "ask for a check".
  • People: who is doing the step.

What is left is money, materials and connections. On 29 September you kept costs and budgets out of action plans. Steps are different work.

Why it is a question now. A step on a shared question serves every project drawing on it. A $40 copying fee for the change-order log serves both Grandview and the oversight project, so totalling costs per project is not straightforward.

Examples: a records-request copying fee of about $40; a board-meeting video to buy; "someone who reads Spanish"; "a contact at the state architect's office".

  1. A · A step notes what it needs and used, in words, with amounts where known and no totals. A connection is named by role and organisation, unless a member names a person in her own note.
  2. B · As A, with amounts totalled per question and per project.
  3. C · Steps record no resources beyond AI.

Trade-offs. A gives members a place to say what a step takes, with no bookkeeping. It fits your earlier wish for "a means of collecting and presenting associated resources". B's totals blur when a step serves several projects, and edge toward the budgets you kept out of action plans. C leaves members to track it elsewhere.

Recommended: A, with totals considered if groups ask for them.

D30 · Reports on where things stand

Background. You asked for status reports by leaders, then withdrew leaders. Every member can already see, at any time, the questions and their strength, the steps and their outcomes, what is waiting on whom, and what was done since a date she picks. The question is whether members also need a written, dated report to read later or take to a meeting.

  1. A · No written reports. The always-current view is enough.
  2. B · Any member may write one on a project or a question. The system drafts it from the record since the last report: steps taken and ended, what was found, what is waiting. She edits it, adds her own words, and it is kept with the project, dated. It is never published and carries no figures about individual members.
  3. C · As B, on a fixed schedule the project sets.

Trade-offs. A adds nothing to build, but leaves members who meet monthly with nothing to bring. B gives them that, written by whoever chooses to. C turns it into a duty, which your no-nagging rule warns against unless a member sets the reminder for herself.

Recommended: B.

6 · Research

The study (6 October)

Fourteen realistic investigations, from your Grandview example to a sewer-fee diversion, five best-practice papers, six capability studies and three reviews. It proposed a few new parts and one AI planner and one AI reading flow, with everything else built on what exists, in eight stages, the first three without AI. Order-of-magnitude cost per investigation: about $15–40 small, $100–300 for a Grandview-sized case, $300–1,000 or more over years. Nothing has been measured yet.

What the product has today

  • Questions with evidence links, alternative bases, claims, conclusions with a falsifier, strength against the bar, contradictions, hypotheses and dated waits.
  • Documents captured with provenance and grade, OCR, unpacked ZIP productions, and search inside a document.
  • Objectives with computed progress and gaps; promises and deadlines with their basis; timelines; watching sources.
  • Cases, determinations, action plans and actions with legal clocks; AI accounts, daily limits and use tracking.
  • No step of investigative work. That is the main thing this design adds.

Words that mean more than one thing

basis

  1. a question's evidence links
  2. one named version of them
  3. why a date is due
  4. what an action rests on
  5. what a proposal rests on
  6. a member's grounds for her testimony

finding

  1. a concluded question
  2. something the machine noticed
  3. a check's result
  4. a recorded absence
  5. a missing document in a sequence

Members should meet one meaning each for "finding" (a concluded question) and "basis" (what a question rests on). The design stream will draw the other words.
