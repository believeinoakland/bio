2 · The design

Everything here follows from your rulings or from canon already in force. Where I have settled a detail myself to keep the design consistent, it is marked detail and can be changed.

2.1 · An investigation

  • An investigation is a project. A project has an objective, what it is trying to establish. It pursues the objective by posing questions, which may or may not be answered.
  • A question can serve several projects and objectives. Each project has its own relationship with a question: it draws on it, it may hold its own conclusion on it, and it may defer or dismiss it. A project's deferral or dismissal never affects another project.
  • A question never shows which projects draw on it. A member sees the question, the work on it and whether it has been answered, never who else is asking.
  • A project's contents are seen only by its participants, whether or not it is hidden. A non-hidden project's name is seen across the group so others can ask to join. A hidden project is invisible to everyone outside it.

2.2 · Steps

  • A step is work done in pursuit of an answer to a question. It is also work done to give members context, to develop a question, or to understand how to respond to findings.
  • Who does it: a member, or the system, including the AI.
  • Where it is, and who sees it. A step is seen wherever it is taken; nobody chooses.
    • On one or more questions: seen wherever any of those questions is seen, with the member's handle, naming no project.
    • In a project: project-specific work, such as the intake interview, seen by that project's participants.
    • For the group: general context outside any one project, seen across the group.
  • When it ends, a step records its outcome (it helped answer the question, it was a dead end, or it was set aside), what it produced, and, optionally, what was learned in the member's words. Dead ends are kept, because they can still add to understanding.
  • What it produces stays in the record whatever the outcome: documents, content, connections and other records, and looks, including "looked, absent".
  • A chance find needs no step. A member may later tie it to the step it helped.
  • Dependencies: a step may wait on another step, on a document arriving, or on a date. It shows what it is waiting on and opens when that is done. A loop is refused. detail
  • By when: a step may carry a date with its basis: a law's clock, a meeting, or the member's own choice. A nearing date changes only how it looks; an overdue one is told once. detail, from your notification rules
  • Milestones belong to a project. Its members set named, dated milestones, such as "contract and change orders in hand before the 14 November board meeting". Each lists the questions or steps it waits on and is met when they are done. Another project sharing a question sets its own. Like every date of the group's own, a milestone is never a finding about government.

2.3 · Saving steps, and saving questions

The starting point. The record is the data: documents in a read-through cache with change tracking, their content, connections, and the maps (money, organisations, people's ties, regulations, court decisions, policies, standards). Investigations and actions are processes on that data. Steps are smaller operations within them, mini-investigations whose purpose is to find answers. Everything a step finds is already in the record as data, with its provenance. That includes what it did not find: a look that found nothing is kept in the record's observation log, and a refusal or a silence is kept in the records request's correspondence.

What is saved for a step. A step is saved as a record associated with the questions that refer to it. A step may be referred to by several questions. It does not remember a purpose of its own, since it may serve several; its purposes are the questions that refer to it. How it found its data is history the record does not need. What it keeps:

  • the questions, or the project, that refer to it;
  • whether it is open or ended, and its outcome (it helped, a dead end, set aside), with its dates;
  • who is doing it: a member by handle, or the system;
  • what it produced, through the provenance of that data;
  • optionally, what was learned, in the member's words.

Steps are searchable, so work is not duplicated. Before a step is created, the member or the system looks for an existing one that does the same work. If one exists, the question refers to it instead. When that shared step finds an answer, the answer is available to every question that shares it, in whichever projects draw on them, without any project seeing the others.

What is saved, searched and used later is the question. The question carries the work done on it (its steps and their outcomes), its evidence, and its answer. So:

  • Before starting, a member or the system finds the question and sees what has been done on it and when.
  • From any document, passage or connection, provenance leads to the step that produced it, and so to the questions it serves.
  • Across questions, the data shows what has been asked of a public body and what came back, including refusals and silence.
  • When the data changes, change tracking brings it to the questions that rest on it.
  • A published case states what was searched and with what outcome, computed from the record's looks.
  • A member joining later reads the question, its work, its evidence and its answer.

2.4 · Evidence: members decide

Members

  • pose questions and take steps
  • hold hunches and hypotheses; follow leads
  • close steps with their outcome
  • decide what becomes evidence
  • conclude, publish, act

The system, including the AI

  • takes steps: searching, capturing, reading
  • explores questions where an account owner has turned exploring on
  • has its own hunches, hypotheses and leads, labelled as its own
  • judges how a find bears on a question, supporting it or contradicting what is gathered
  • offers finds to the members working on the question
A member's act decidesdismiss the information, or add it to the evidence on the question, by its grade and the rules of evidence
  • The system's judgement of a find is a labelled signal, shown with how it was worked out and its measured rate of false alarms. It is never a grade, never a stored score, and never used to hide or rank what members see.
  • The system's hypotheses are never facts. A member may take one up as her own.
  • Who receives a find: the joined members of every project that draws on the question. Each receives it once, in her queue, never by email. Each may stop following a question, or start following one. Nobody receives anything she may not already see.
  • The system's finds are labelled as the system's work. Which account paid for them is shown only to that account's owners. detail: so that a find never reveals which project asked

2.6 · AI use

Groups vary from no AI at all to wanting all it can offer, and a group and its members may differ. The design supports that range.

  • Account owners: the group, each project and each member can have an AI account. Today that means the group's key held by an administrator, a member's own subscription (used only by her), or a member's own API key. A project may have its own key, set up by its owners, for example one funded by a grant. Later releases may offer other models, frontier and open.
  • Types of use are switched on or off: conversation, member support, the intake interview, turning members' words into questions, reading documents, exploring questions, and others as they are built.
  • Limits: each account owner sets an overall limit and may set a limit per type of use. A per-use limit is either inclusive, counted within the overall limit, or exclusive, a separate allowance on top of it. A limit is in money where the account reports cost. A subscription does not report it, so there the limit is counted in tokens and calls.
  • Exploring has its own setting per account owner:
    • No: not paid from this account. It does not pass to anyone else's setting.
    • Ask every day: the owner gets one queue item a day, only when something is worth exploring, listing the questions and a rough cost. No answer means no.
    • Yes: the system explores when it judges it useful, within the limits.
    It is set once per account, with an override where a project or question is open.
  • Two kinds of "no". A limit about the material, "no AI at all", binds everyone whoever pays: a group's, or a project's for its own material, for example a whistleblower's papers. A money limit, "not on my dime", binds only the account it belongs to. A member spending her own account is never held back by anyone else's budget, only by a "no AI" on the material.
  • A project's "no AI" covers what is its own: its project steps, notes and interview, and any question or document only it draws on. Material it shares with other projects follows the group's setting, since a project cannot tell what others are doing with it. detail: keeps one project from blocking, and so revealing itself to, another
  • Tracking: use is tracked per account, per type of use, over time. A member sees her own; a project's owners see the project account's; administrators see the group key's totals by type of use, naming no member.

2.7 · What holds throughout

  • Who did what is recorded by handle. Nothing measures, scores or ranks a member, and there is no record of who opened which file.
  • No nagging: a reminder is one the member asked for; a nearing date changes only how it looks; an overdue one is told once; nothing goes by email or push.
  • Working material (steps, notes, the interview, the project's thinking) is never published.
  • The group's own dates are never findings about government.
  • The machine proposes; a member accepts.

3 · Grandview, start to finish

How the design works for a parent whose child's school rebuild is a year late.

  1. Maria opens a project, "Grandview rebuild". Its objective: establish why the rebuild is late and who knew when. The intake interview is a step in the project, done with the assistant or by a script.
  2. From her answers come questions. "When did the district first know the rebuild would run late?" is new. "Did change orders exceed the legal limit?" already exists, because a district-oversight project asked it. Maria's project draws on it too, without either project seeing the other.
  3. Maria takes steps. One is a records request for the construction manager's reports, on the first question, with the law's ten-day clock as its "by when". Another is reading the bond measure for background, a project step.
  4. Grandview has a small grant-funded key, with exploring set to "Ask every day" and a $30 exploring limit inclusive in $100 a month. One morning Maria's queue asks whether to explore two questions, roughly $3. She says yes. The system finds three board agendas, judges one as cutting against the district's account, and offers it.
  5. Maria adds that agenda to the evidence as a leg. She dismisses another find with a reason. The oversight project's members are offered the same finds on the shared question, labelled as the system's work, not as Grandview's.
  6. A step ends as a dead end: the state architect's tracker had nothing. Its outcome, what was looked at, and "the tracker only covers projects over $10m" are kept.
  7. The district's documents arrive. Maria ties a document a neighbour forwarded by chance to the records-request step.
  8. The members conclude on the first question at the project's bar. The other project may conclude differently. If the two conclusions cannot both hold, each side is told only that something it rests on conflicts with a record it cannot see.
  9. The project publishes a case and plans what to do about it.

5 · Open questions

D28 · Removing steps

Background. On 7 October you said steps are "deleted because no longer necessary". Since then you agreed that a step keeps its outcome, what it produced and what was learned, because even dead ends add to understanding. So a step that was worked on stays. What is left is a step that was planned and never started. Example: "check the state architect's tracker", made pointless when the documents arrived another way.

  1. A · An untouched step can be deleted outright. Nothing was done, so nothing is lost.
  2. B · It is withdrawn with a reason and stays readable.

Trade-offs. A keeps the list of work realistic, as you wanted. B keeps a record of intentions that rarely matters, and clutters questions many projects share.

Recommended: A.

D29 · Resources a step needs and uses

Background. On 7 October you named people, money and other material resources, information, connections, expertise and AI budget. Most are now covered:

  • AI: section 2.6.
  • Information: what a step seeks and produces.
  • Expertise: members' declared expertise and the existing "ask for a check".
  • People: who is doing the step.

What is left is money, materials and connections. On 29 September you kept costs and budgets out of action plans. Steps are different work.

Why it is a question now. A step on a shared question serves every project drawing on it. A $40 copying fee for the change-order log serves both Grandview and the oversight project, so totalling costs per project is not straightforward.

Examples: a records-request copying fee of about $40; a board-meeting video to buy; "someone who reads Spanish"; "a contact at the state architect's office".

  1. A · A step notes what it needs and used, in words, with amounts where known and no totals. A connection is named by role and organisation, unless a member names a person in her own note.
  2. B · As A, with amounts totalled per question and per project.
  3. C · Steps record no resources beyond AI.

Trade-offs. A gives members a place to say what a step takes, with no bookkeeping. It fits your earlier wish for "a means of collecting and presenting associated resources". B's totals blur when a step serves several projects, and edge toward the budgets you kept out of action plans. C leaves members to track it elsewhere.

Recommended: A, with totals considered if groups ask for them.

D30 · Reports on where things stand

Background. You asked for status reports by leaders, then withdrew leaders. Every member can already see, at any time, the questions and their strength, the steps and their outcomes, what is waiting on whom, and what was done since a date she picks. The question is whether members also need a written, dated report to read later or take to a meeting.

  1. A · No written reports. The always-current view is enough.
  2. B · Any member may write one on a project or a question. The system drafts it from the record since the last report: steps taken and ended, what was found, what is waiting. She edits it, adds her own words, and it is kept with the project, dated. It is never published and carries no figures about individual members.
  3. C · As B, on a fixed schedule the project sets.

Trade-offs. A adds nothing to build, but leaves members who meet monthly with nothing to bring. B gives them that, written by whoever chooses to. C turns it into a duty, which your no-nagging rule warns against unless a member sets the reminder for herself.

Recommended: B.

7 · Research

The study (6 October)

Fourteen realistic investigations, from your Grandview example to a sewer-fee diversion, five best-practice papers, six capability studies and three reviews. It proposed a few new parts and one AI planner and one AI reading flow, with everything else built on what exists, in eight stages, the first three without AI. Order-of-magnitude cost per investigation: about $15–40 small, $100–300 for a Grandview-sized case, $300–1,000 or more over years. Nothing has been measured yet.

The fourteen scenarios

The study's problem set: fourteen realistic investigations a member might bring, chosen to span the kinds of public matters groups pursue. California is the default jurisdiction. Each is shown with the member's opening words, what she needs, the documents that answer it, the competing explanations, and what an answer and action look like.

S1 · Grandview Elementary: the rebuild that will not end

schools, public works, procurement

"My kids' school, Grandview Elementary, is finally being rebuilt. The process of designing the new campus, putting the construction out for bid, choosing the winning bid, and construction starting took 3 years. We were promised that reconstruction would take no longer than 1 school year. But now it's been 2 years, and the project still isn't close to being done. What's going on?"

Needs: Find the binding schedule against the promise; actual progress and forecast; why the gap, and who caused it; whether law or policy was broken; what the member can do.

Documents: bond measure; board approval items; state architect approval and tracker; bid documents and award; the contract and notice to proceed; schedules; change orders; construction-manager reports; oversight committee reports; contractor licence and payroll records.

Competing explanations: unforeseen site conditions; design errors; state re-review; contractor distress; owner-caused delay; funding gap; supply chain. Usually several at once.

Answer and action: a chronology of promised, contracted and forecast completion; each slip attributed with its evidence; money table; what is unknown and which record would settle it. Records requests, comment, oversight committee, monitoring until the school opens.

S2 · The sewer fees that went to the general fund

budgets, utilities: BIO's origin case

"The auditor said the city moved $52 million of our sewer fees into the general fund. They promised to fix it by 2023. Did they? Are they still doing it?"

Needs: Did the transfers stop or change label; were the audit's recommendations done; is any current transfer lawful under Prop 218; what came of the records request.

Documents: audit and follow-ups; annual financial reports; budgets and transfer data; cost-allocation plan; council resolutions; records-request production.

Competing explanations: legitimate cost allocation; a relabelled fee; an accounting reclassification.

Answer and action: a fund-flow series with relabelling dated; the legal comparison; grand jury and State Controller; a records-act petition; a Prop 218 challenge through counsel.

S3 · The emergency contract nobody bid

procurement, homelessness services

"The county gave a nonprofit $14 million to run a shelter without any bidding because of an 'emergency'. The shelter opened late and has half the beds they said. Who decided this and where did the money go?"

Needs: Was the emergency exemption properly used and renewed; what the contract required; what was delivered and paid.

Documents: emergency declaration and renewals; contract, scope and amendments; invoices; performance reports; the nonprofit's tax filings.

Competing explanations: a real emergency with one capable provider; site delays; favouritism; weak contract management.

Answer and action: deliverables against payments; the exemption's chronology; comment, county auditor, grand jury if warranted.

S4 · The officer still on patrol

policing

"An officer who has had complaint after complaint, and was in a shooting, is still working my neighbourhood. Has anything been done?"

Needs: Which records of force and sustained findings exist; how they were handled; whether discipline policy was followed.

Documents: records disclosable under state police-transparency law; oversight reports; lawsuits and settlements; force and discipline policies.

Competing explanations: not sustained; sustained but overturned; deadline missed; record not disclosable.

Answer and action: per-incident table of what is public and what is legally unknowable; pattern questions to the oversight body. A named person: heightened care.

S5 · The landlord who never fixes anything

housing, code enforcement

"Our building has had no heat, mold, and broken locks for two years. We've called the city again and again. Inspectors came, but nothing changes. Why isn't the city making the owner fix it?"

Needs: What the enforcement record shows; whether the city used its powers; who really owns the building.

Documents: inspection files; notices to repair; liens; ownership and LLC filings; rent-board petitions.

Competing explanations: partial compliance before each re-inspection; understaffing; a policy of voluntary compliance; owner insolvency.

Answer and action: enforcement chronology against the city's legal tools; requests to escalate; the owner's other buildings.

S6 · The promised affordable units that never came

land use

"When they approved the tower on our corner, the developer promised affordable units and a park. The tower's open. Where's the park? Are the units really affordable?"

Needs: Find the binding conditions; test each; who enforces each.

Documents: conditions of approval; development agreement; environmental mitigation programme; recorded affordability agreement; compliance reports.

Competing explanations: conditions amended later; fee paid instead; pending a later phase; never enforced.

Answer and action: a conditions register, each with status and evidence; requests to enforcement; comment.

S7 · The water bill that doubled

utilities

"Our water rates went up 40% over three years for 'pipe replacement'. I don't see any pipes being replaced. Is the money being spent on what they said?"

Needs: What justified the increase; whether the capital programme is delivered; whether the rate process was lawful.

Documents: rate notice and hearing record; rate study; capital programme; enterprise fund reports; for a private utility, the state commission's rate case.

Competing explanations: contracting delays; costs rose; revenue diverted.

Answer and action: promised against delivered capital by year; reserves; comment at the next rate hearing.

S8 · The council vote and the donor

elections, campaign finance

"Our council member voted to give a towing contract to a company whose owners gave to her campaign. Is that legal?"

Needs: Map contributions to the decision; test against conflict and contribution rules; check disclosure.

Documents: campaign statements; economic-interest statement; the agenda item and vote; the procurement file; corporate filings.

Competing explanations: lawful contributions; a merit award; no financial interest.

Answer and action: a sourced contribution-and-decision timeline; a complaint to the ethics body. Named people: precise claims, no insinuation.

S9 · The smoke from the recycling yard

environmental permits

"There's a metal recycler next to our school. Fires, smoke, dust on our cars. Does it even have a permit? Has anyone ever fined them?"

Needs: Which permits apply; violation history; whether enforcement escalated.

Documents: air-district permits and deviation reports; notices of violation; federal compliance history; water, waste and fire records.

Competing explanations: within limits but unregulated dust; violations penalised but recurring; agencies each assuming another has jurisdiction.

Answer and action: a per-agency compliance table; complaints to the right agency; comment at permit renewal.

S10 · The lead in the school water

public health

"I just learned the water fountains at my daughter's school tested high for lead years ago. Did they ever fix it? What about other schools?"

Needs: Results by fixture and date; remediation and retesting; required notice to parents.

Documents: the state's school lead-sampling dataset; notices; work orders; retests.

Competing explanations: fixed without a posted record; retest below threshold; never acted on.

Answer and action: fixture-level status; a pattern across schools; a watch on the dataset. Small: days and a dozen documents.

S11 · The bus line that was supposed to open in 2022

transit

"The rapid bus line on our avenue was supposed to open years ago. The road has been torn up forever and businesses are closing. Why is it so late and how much over budget?"

Needs: Baseline against current schedule and cost; causes; whether oversight flagged it.

Documents: federal funding agreement; federal oversight reports; agency board reports; change orders and claims; utility agreements.

Competing explanations: utility relocation; design changes; contractor disputes; agency capacity.

Answer and action: S1's pattern at larger scale: chronology and attribution over years.

S12 · The eviction court that defaults everyone

courts

"In our county, tenants who go to eviction court seem to lose by default because hearings are rescheduled without notice and there are no interpreters. Is the court following its own rules?"

Needs: Measure defaults and continuances; test against court rules and its language-access plan.

Documents: dockets; court statistics; language-access plan; interpreter budget. Courts follow a different access regime from agencies.

Competing explanations: tenants not appearing for outside reasons; staffing; a practice change at one courthouse.

Answer and action: a measured pattern with its method shown; a letter to the presiding judge. Members' court-watching is evidence.

S13 · Library hours cut while the budget grew

budgets

"They cut our branch library to three days a week 'for budget reasons', but the city budget went up. Where did the money go?"

Needs: Test the 'budget reasons' claim; test any library measure's minimum-service terms.

Documents: budgets and actuals; vacancy reports; the measure and its oversight reports.

Competing explanations: growth elsewhere; vacancies swept; measure money substituted for general support.

Answer and action: budget against actual against vacancies; the measure's terms compared; comment at budget hearings.

S14 · Schools closed by a decision nobody saw

open meetings, schools

"The school board voted to close four schools. Parents only found out the next morning. It feels like it was decided behind closed doors."

Needs: Test the process against the open-meetings law and the closure policy.

Documents: agendas and their posting times; late materials; closed-session reports; meeting video; the closure policy.

Competing explanations: lawful but poorly communicated; vague but adequate agenda; a real serial meeting.

Answer and action: a posting-time chronology; a cure-and-correct demand within its few days. Deadlines that bind the member.

What they have in common.

  • Two shapes recur: schedule-and-cost (S1, S11, partly S3 and S7) and compliance against a standard (most of the rest).
  • Document kinds are few and repeat.
  • A records request with a clock appears in ten of the fourteen.
  • Every answer is built on a chronology.
  • Most of an investigation's life is waiting.
  • Scale varies a hundredfold.
  • Members produce evidence themselves: site visits, logs, court-watching.

What they do not cover. Every opening is already a fairly clear grievance about a public body. None is a muddled first message, a complaint with several issues tangled together, a rumour, or a request for help with a document. Those are what the assistant must also handle (D42).

What the product has today

  • Questions with evidence links, alternative bases, claims, conclusions with a falsifier, strength against the bar, contradictions, hypotheses and dated waits.
  • Documents captured with provenance and grade, OCR, unpacked ZIP productions, and search inside a document.
  • Objectives with computed progress and gaps; promises and deadlines with their basis; timelines; watching sources.
  • Cases, determinations, action plans and actions with legal clocks; AI accounts, daily limits and use tracking.
  • No step of investigative work. That is the main thing this design adds.

Words that mean more than one thing

basis

  1. a question's evidence links
  2. one named version of them
  3. why a date is due
  4. what an action rests on
  5. what a proposal rests on
  6. a member's grounds for her testimony

finding

  1. a concluded question
  2. something the machine noticed
  3. a check's result
  4. a recorded absence
  5. a missing document in a sequence

Members should meet one meaning each for "finding" (a concluded question) and "basis" (what a question rests on). The design stream will draw the other words.
