Investigation Design

The design of record for investigations in Civicsmith · for Bob's review · 9 October 2026

How a group investigates a public body in Civicsmith, from what comes in to a published case another group can check and accept. Section 1 is the design; section 2 follows one investigation through it.

Approved by Bob, 9 October 2026. Every question is answered. This is the design of record; BOB now writes it into canon, keeping in the investigation document only what is new to investigations and pointing to the canon document that already rules everything else.

1 · The design

Everything here follows from Bob's rulings or from canon in force. Where the design lane settled a detail itself to keep the design consistent, it is marked detail.

1.0 · Why Civicsmith exists, in canon's words

"the objective is BETTER GOVERNMENT through greater understanding, increased accountability, and the separation of narrative from evidence; … bad actors are identified by EVIDENCE, never assumed by role; less narrative allows the full realm of possibilities to be seen and considered. The product is therefore the trustworthiness of the record."

System Design §1, as Bob revised it on 9 October

"Accountability is built on credible facts. Nothing else holds up." "Follow the evidence wherever it goes. When the evidence changes, the findings change."

Roadmap §4, §5

"The tool should make a supported case easy to build and an unsupported one hard to state."

Case Making §4a

"Facts are verifiable, analysis is reproducible, and judgment is debatable."

Functional Architecture, Layer 2

Everything below is held to these. A member's act commits the group, but what it rests on is graded evidence held to the project's bar. Judgement belongs to what a finding signifies and whether to act on it, never to what the facts are.

1.1 · An investigation

  • An investigation is a project. A project has an objective, what it is trying to establish. It pursues the objective by posing questions, which may or may not be answered.
  • The objective is the measure, throughout, and the evidence decides it. An investigation gathers information to make a determination: was the contract award done properly; is there a legal justification for the transfer; is the city meeting its stated standards. The question at every point is whether the objective has been met, whether or not every question has been answered. That is determined by rigorous assessment of the evidence, never by anyone's say-so.
    • The objective states what the record must hold for the matter to be decided, not which answer is hoped for. For example: for every contract award in the period, the solicitation and the public vote, or a records request answered that none exists, each at the project's required grade. Progress toward that is computed from the record and never reported; what falls short is listed as gaps, and the gaps are the work list. An objective with no such condition is shown as one whose progress cannot be computed, never as met.
    • When the record holds what the objective needs, the matter can be determined: award by award, against the rule that binds it, complied, did not, or unclear. The evidence decides which. An investigation that shows a rule was followed or broken has met its objective either way; compliance is recorded with the same care.
    • Members draw legs on what the record holds, each with its grade, and conclude questions; a conclusion always shows its strength beside the project's bar. Only conclusions that meet the bar can carry a published case or a claim that a rule was broken.
    • Members write the objective and its condition, and may revise them with a reason as the case evolves. Finds that cut against what members expect are shown as prominently as those that support it.
    • The project's page always shows the objective, its condition, where the evidence stands: for it, against it, and the gaps.
  • A question can serve several projects and objectives. Each project has its own relationship with a question: it draws on it, it may hold its own conclusion on it, and it may defer or dismiss it. A project's deferral or dismissal never affects another project.
  • A question shows which non-hidden projects draw on it. A hidden project that draws on it is never shown, named or counted, so a member sees the question, the work on it, whether it has been answered, and the non-hidden projects asking it, and can never tell whether a hidden one is asking too.
  • A project's contents are seen only by its participants, whether or not it is hidden. A non-hidden project's name is seen across the group so others can ask to join. A hidden project is invisible to members outside it. Administrators see that it exists, its name and its owners, so they can do their part for the group (a legal hold, a complaint about it); they see its contents only if its owners add them.

1.2 · Steps

  • A step is work done in pursuit of an answer to a question. It is also work done to give members context, to develop a question, or to understand how to respond to findings.
  • Who does it: a member, or the system, including the AI.
  • Where it is, and who sees it. A step is seen wherever it is taken; nobody chooses.
    • On one or more questions: seen wherever any of those questions is seen, with the member's handle and the project it was taken in, if that project is not hidden. One taken in a hidden project names no project.
    • In a project: project-specific work, such as the intake interview, seen by that project's participants.
    • For the group: general context outside any one project, seen across the group.
  • When it ends, a step is ended or set aside, and records what it produced and, optionally, what was learned in the member's words. Each question it serves records its own outcome: it helped that question, or it was a dead end for it. A step that helped one question may be a dead end for another. Someone working on the question records it; the system never does, and leaves it "not yet judged". Dead ends are kept, because they can still add to understanding.
  • What it produces stays in the record whatever the outcome: documents, content, connections and other records, and looks, including "looked, absent".
  • Removing a step: a step nobody started can be deleted outright. One that was worked on keeps its record. Where other questions also refer to an untouched step, deleting it removes only this question's reference. detail, for shared steps
  • What a step costs: a step may carry what it costs in money, such as a records-request copying fee or a video to buy. AI use is not counted here; it follows the AI settings.
  • Sharing a cost: when a costed step is shared by several projects and none of them is hidden, the owners of each are told the total cost and which projects share the interest. Each may write a message, which is passed to the others; it carries its writer's handle and names no project unless she writes it in. They work out among themselves how, and how much, each pays. Civicsmith splits nothing. If any project sharing the step is hidden, no such notice is sent.
  • A chance find needs no step. A member may later tie it to the step it helped.
  • Dependencies: a step may wait on another step, on a document arriving, or on a date. It shows what it is waiting on and opens when that is done. A loop is refused. detail
  • By when: a step may carry a date with its basis: a law's clock, a meeting, or the member's own choice. A nearing date changes only how it looks; an overdue one is reported once. detail, from the notification rules
  • When the work goes quiet (every step ended, nothing awaited), the system shows once where the evidence stands against the objective's condition. If the condition is satisfied, the members turn to writing up and acting. If it is not and nothing is left to try, they choose to watch (keep watching the sources, and reopen the work when something arrives) or close, the gaps recorded as what remains unknown. Or they revise the objective, with a reason. Nothing more is asked after that. A quiet project shows as quiet on its page; that is a display, not a stage.
  • Waits on a question ("the district's reply, due 1 November") are seen wherever the question is, by handle, and naming the project it was set in only if that project is not hidden, as steps are. A reminder a member asked for stays hers alone.
  • Milestones belong to a project. Its members set named, dated milestones, such as "contract and change orders in hand before the 14 November board meeting". Each lists the questions or steps it waits on and is met when they are done: a question when this project has concluded on it (another project's conclusion does not count), a step when it has ended, whatever its outcome. A question this project deferred or dismissed, or a step set aside, is not done; the milestone shows it as stuck, and a member may take it off, which is recorded. So a milestone never reads "met" when the work was dropped. Another project sharing a question sets its own. Like every date of the group's own, a milestone is never a finding about government.

1.3 · Saving steps, and saving questions

The starting point. The record is the data: documents in a read-through cache with change tracking, their content, connections, and the maps (money, organisations, people's ties, regulations, court decisions, policies, standards). Investigations and actions are processes on that data. Steps are smaller operations within them, mini-investigations whose purpose is to find answers. Everything a step finds is already in the record as data, with its provenance. That includes what it did not find: a look that found nothing is kept in the record's observation log, and a refusal or a silence is kept in the records request's correspondence. A step that looked and found nothing is updated if the data is found later: change tracking brings the new data to the step and to the questions it serves, the earlier look stays in the record with its date, and the step's outcome for each question can be revised by a member working on it.

What is saved for a step. A step is saved as a record associated with the questions that refer to it. A step may be referred to by several questions. It does not remember a purpose of its own, since it may serve several; its purposes are the questions that refer to it. How it found its data is history the record does not need. What it keeps:

  • the questions, or the project, that refer to it;
  • whether it is open, ended or set aside, with its dates, and for each question it serves, whether it helped or was a dead end;
  • who is doing it: a member by handle, or the system;
  • what it produced, through the provenance of that data;
  • optionally, what was learned, in the member's words.

Steps are searchable, so work is not duplicated. Before a step is created, the member or the system looks for an existing one that does the same work. If one exists, the question refers to it instead. What a shared step finds is available to every question that refers to it, in whichever projects draw on them, without any project seeing the others. Each project concludes for itself.

What is saved, searched and used later is the question. The question carries the work done on it (its steps and their outcomes), its evidence, and its answer. So:

  • Before starting, a member or the system finds the question and sees what has been done on it and when.
  • From any document, passage or connection, provenance leads to the step that produced it, and so to the questions it serves.
  • Across questions, the data shows what has been asked of a public body and what came back, including refusals and silence.
  • When the data changes, change tracking brings it to the questions that rest on it.
  • A published case states what was searched and with what outcome, computed from the record's looks.
  • A member joining later reads the question, its work, its evidence and its answer.

Why a document is held. Read from the record, never written separately: the step that produced it and the questions that step serves. A document that came without a step (a chance find, something forwarded, a doorbell drop) shows who brought it and from where, and, once a member ties it to a step, that step's questions. A member who wants to say why she grabbed something writes it in the step's "what was learned" or in a lead.

Status reports. Any member may write a status report on a project or a question. The system drafts it from the record since the last status report: steps taken and ended, what was found, what is waiting. She edits it and adds her own words, and it is kept with the project, dated. It is never published, carries no figures about individual members, and is never required on a schedule.

1.4 · Evidence: members decide, by grade and the rules of evidence

Members

  • pose questions and take steps
  • hold hunches and hypotheses; follow leads
  • close steps with their outcome
  • decide what becomes evidence, by its grade and the rules of evidence
  • conclude on the evidence; publish what meets the bar; determine, against the rule that binds, whether a body complied; act on a determination

The system, including the AI

  • takes steps: searching, reading, and asking to capture pages the record already points to; a page it finds anywhere else it names to a member, who captures it
  • explores questions where an account owner has turned exploring on
  • has its own hunches and leads: labelled guesses, shown with how they were worked out and their false-alarm rate, never counted as evidence, which a member may take up as her own
  • judges how a find bears on a question, supporting it or contradicting what is gathered
  • offers finds to the members working on the question
A member's act decidesdismiss the information, or add it to the evidence on the question, by its grade and the rules of evidence
  • Accepting what the system proposes: one act for every kind of proposal (a find, a hunch, a proposed question or step, the reading of a passage). The member takes it up as proposed, edited, or writes her own instead, and the record keeps which. Any joined member of a project drawing on the question may act on it. Where accepting would make her vouch for a legal or authored statement, she writes it in her own words. How often each kind is accepted unchanged is measured across the group only, never per member.
  • A fact the AI found loses no strength for that. When the publisher's own words are in the document, checked there by code, and any number or date in them is read there by code and checked by the member, the passage keeps the document's own capture grade. How it bears on the question, and any connection it makes, is graded by how that link is established, as for any leg. The machine only pointed. What the passage means is read by the member when she accepts it, and recorded as proposed or edited. Only text the AI read from a picture of a page, where the words themselves may be misread, is weaker until measured.
  • The system's judgement of a find is a labelled signal, shown with how it was worked out and its measured rate of false alarms. It is never a grade, never a stored score, and never used to hide or rank what members see.
  • The system's hunches and hypotheses are kept apart from members', under a heading of their own on the question, seen by everyone who sees the question and offered alongside its finds. They are never facts. A member who takes one up holds it as her own, noted as having come from the system. The members' own list holds only what members hold.
  • Who receives a find: the joined members of every project that draws on the question. Each receives it once, in her queue, never by email. Each may stop following a question, or start following one. Nobody receives anything she may not already see.
  • The system's finds are labelled as the system's work. Which account paid for them is shown only to that account's owners: what a project spends is its own business.

1.6 · AI use

Groups vary from no AI at all to wanting all it can offer, and a group and its members may differ. The design supports that range.

  • Account owners: the group, each project and each member can have an AI account. Today that means the group's key held by an administrator, a member's own subscription (used only by her), or a member's own API key. A project may have its own key, set up by its owners, for example one funded by a grant. Later releases may offer other models, frontier and open.
  • Types of use are switched on or off: conversation, member support, the intake interview, turning members' words into questions, reading documents, exploring questions, and others as they are built.
  • Limits: each account owner sets an overall limit and may set a limit per type of use. A per-use limit is either inclusive, counted within the overall limit, or exclusive, a separate allowance on top of it. A limit is in money where the account reports cost. A subscription does not report it, so there the limit is counted in tokens and calls.
  • Exploring has its own setting per account owner:
    • No: not paid from this account. It does not pass to anyone else's setting.
    • Ask every day: the owner gets one queue item a day, only when something is worth exploring, listing the questions and a rough cost. No answer means no.
    • Yes: the system explores when it judges it useful, within the limits.
    It is set once per account, with an override where a project or question is open.
  • Two kinds of "no". A limit about the material, "no AI at all", binds everyone whoever pays: a group's, or a project's for its own material, for example a whistleblower's papers. A money limit, "not on my dime", binds only the account it belongs to. A member spending her own account is never held back by anyone else's budget, only by a "no AI" on the material.
  • A project's "no AI" covers what is its own: its project steps, notes and interview, and any question or document only it draws on. Material it shares with other projects follows the group's setting, since a project cannot tell what others are doing with it. detail: keeps one project from blocking, and so revealing itself to, another
  • The intake interview asks the same questions on both paths (what happened; which public body, and where; since when; what was promised or expected, and by whom; what you already have; what you want to come of it). By hand, it is one page to fill in quickly. With the assistant, it is a conversation that skips what she has already said and follows up. The member checks the answers before they are kept. The answers are narrative (1.9).
  • How answers are worded: in Civicsmith's own voice, an answer shows each baseline side by side with what it rests on and the measured difference ("900 days since the notice to proceed; contract time 540 days"). It gives no verdict, likelihood or rating words, and says "cause not established" unless a concluded finding establishes the cause. A cause a member suspects stays a labelled hypothesis; a cause the district or contractor gave is shown as their stated cause, quoted. Members' conclusions may say "late" where a finding at the bar shows the contract's date has passed.
  • Pages that are only pictures: the AI may transcribe scans that Civicsmith's own text recognition cannot read, on the paying account and within its limits. The text is labelled as the AI's reading and graded "undetermined" until its accuracy is measured. A member who checks a passage against the page may confirm it, and it then carries the document's grade.
  • A note beside a source: a short note on what a document says about the question, and what it does not. Every sentence is tied to a quote, and any sentence that cannot be tied is left out. It is never shown in place of the source, never stored as the document's content, and never cited as evidence.
  • Reading guides say what to look for in one kind of document (a construction contract's contract time, damages and change-order clause). By hand, a guide is a checklist; for the AI, it is what to read for. Civicsmith keeps a library shipped to every group, approved by Bob or someone he names, each guide only after measured use shows it works. Each group keeps its own: a member's guide is usable by her at once, and becomes the group's after another member reviews it. Guides are shared across groups: a group may offer one to others, and a group that takes it up reviews its copy before it becomes its own; a guide many groups use may be proposed for Civicsmith's library. The AI may draft a guide but never approves one. When the AI reads a kind of document, it loads that kind's guide as its skill, so the AI's reading and the by-hand checklist are the same thing. A guide says what to look for, never how the AI may behave: the AI's rules of conduct come only from the rules Bob approves, no guide can loosen them, and code checks that none does.
  • Test investigations: before members get an AI part, it must pass Civicsmith's own set of test investigations: real public matters, frozen with their documents, with answers written by people. A group may add its own; the AI part is then also measured against them, and the results shown to that group. Test transcripts are kept for grading only and never published.
  • Several steps at once: one act may start several AI steps. The estimate before covers them all; each runs within the limits, and one that reaches a limit stops and says so while the others go on.
  • The rules the AI follows are approved by Bob in plain language, as part of this design (for example, "the assistant never writes a member's reason"). The AI's working instructions may only quote those rules, and code checks that they do.
  • Cost before and after: before an AI act or an exploring run, an estimate as a range ("about $0.50 to $2"), or "not known yet" until enough runs are measured; after it, the actual cost. Both are shown to the paying account's owners only; on a subscription, runs and tokens instead of money. Nobody sees another member's spending.
  • Reading inside a document: the AI may read a document the group holds a few pages at a time, within a reading limit, and never one under a "no AI" limit. As it reads, it builds the record: it proposes the passages worth citing that bear on the investigation (figures, policies, decisions, statements and the like), and the connections they make (to bodies, people in their public roles, other documents and the questions). Each proposal is tied to its exact quote, labelled as the system's work, and taken up by a member's act. A passage keeps the document's capture grade; a connection is graded by how the link is established, never by the machine's say-so, and the machine never grades one D.
  • Tracking: use is tracked per account, per type of use, over time. A member sees her own; a project's owners see the project account's; administrators see the group key's totals by type of use, naming no member.

1.7 · What holds throughout

  • Who did what is recorded by handle. Nothing measures, scores or ranks a member, and there is no record of who opened which file.
  • No nagging: a reminder is one the member asked for; a nearing date changes only how it looks; an overdue one is reported once; nothing goes by email or push.
  • Working material (steps, notes, the interview, the project's thinking) is never published.
  • The group's own dates are never findings about government.
  • The machine proposes; a member accepts on the evidence, and the record shows its grade. The machine never concludes, determines or attests.

1.8 · Where a first message leads

  • Both kinds of member come in through one door: "What happened?". With the assistant, the member writes freely and the assistant proposes where it should go. By hand, the same door offers plain-language choices (the guided starts), shows existing work that matches, and allows a blank start. detail
  • It leads to one of six places: start a project; add to an existing question or project; keep it as a lead, with a watch if wanted; a step for understanding; an action; or "not something Civicsmith does", saying where to go instead. The member always decides, and can move a lead into a project later.
  • A member's own matter with a public body (a suspension, a refused permit) is helped: getting her own records, checking the body's own policy, writing to it. It lives in a hidden project of hers, seen by her alone unless she adds others. Nothing about the private person concerned is ever published. If a public pattern appears, she may start a group question about it, carrying none of her private material unless she chooses.
  • Tangled messages become one project with several questions when they share a subject, separate projects otherwise. Rumours become leads, never questions aimed at a person. detail

1.9 · Narrative and evidence

  • What a member recalls of what others said or did is narrative. The intake interview and a member's story are kept in her own words. They are valuable: they say where to look. But memory is not always exact, and a member's recollection of what a public body said is not the body's statement. What she saw or heard herself, and a source's own account, can be recorded as testimony: evidence, labelled with whose word it is. Everything else must be found in the record.
  • A claim in the narrative becomes something to find. When a member says "the district said the campus would be closed for one school year", the system (or the member, by hand) turns it into a step: find where the district said it (a newsletter, a board item, the bond materials). Until it is found, the claim is shown as the member recalls it, never as what the district said.
  • A member who was there (she heard it said at a board meeting) may record it as her own firsthand account. That is evidence, graded as testimony and labelled as her account. The grade says it rests on her word and is harder for others to check, not that it is less true. Corroboration strengthens it; an anonymous account is corroborated before a finding rests on it.
  • A private note may be shared with a project the member is in. Its participants see it, in her words and labelled as hers. It is narrative, never evidence and never published. She may withdraw it, and the record says it was shared and withdrawn. If it names a person in no public role, she is warned at the act (1.10).
  • In an answer, each baseline names what it rests on: a document, a member's firsthand account, or "as recalled", with whether anyone has looked: "not yet looked for", or "looked for and not found", saying where.

1.10 · People

  • An investigation may concern anyone whose acts bear on a public body's act: a contractor's owner, a donor, a landlord whose building the city inspects. Officials are investigated in their official acts.
  • No investigation has as its subject a private person's private life. When an objective or question names a person in no public role, the member is warned at the act, and decides.
  • The system never explores a person on its own. It gathers about a person only when a member has tied that person to the question, and only up to a fixed amount per run.
  • Publication names a person outside a public role only where their act bears on a finding, as canon already says.

1.11 · The parts that will be built

  • Steps: steps, where each is seen, outcomes per question, what a step produced, waits and dates, costs and the cost notice, the search that prevents duplicates, and following a question.
  • Question explorer: the system exploring a question unasked, judging its finds and offering them. Built separately so steps, milestones and status reports work fully without AI. Not offered to members until it passes its test investigations (1.6).
  • Investigation: a project's milestones and its members' status reports.
  • Reading guides: the library of reading guides, Civicsmith's and each group's, shared across groups.
  • Planning, a new kind of AI work inside the existing AI parts: the assistant's interview, turning a member's words into proposed questions and steps, and turning remembered claims into "find the record" steps. It has its own test bar.
  • Everything else extends parts that already exist: the guided starts and front door, reading inside documents, proposals and AI use, notes, waits and watching.

1.12 · Publishing a case, and what happens after

Getting ready. Only findings that are part of a project can be published, and only by an owner of the project. Before anything is signed, the system runs every check without writing anything and lists what blocks publication. It refuses a case where:

  • a load-bearing finding falls below the project's bar;
  • a question it rests on is not concluded;
  • any hunch is left uncleared ("the case must still hold with the hunch removed");
  • the authored statements are missing: what the case says, why its subject, what was left out and why, its scope, and the bias acknowledgement;
  • an anonymous account it rests on is uncorroborated;
  • material it relies on cannot travel in the case file whole.

Review. A member may send a review copy to anyone first: marked as what it is, as complete as the real thing, and naming what is missing. A second member's check of the "what was left out" statement is shown on the case, but never required, because a group may be one person.

Signing and publishing. The owner walks through five screens: what becomes permanent; what this rests on; what you are leaving out; the edition this creates; and sign. She signs in her own browser, and no machine ever signs. Each finding and its evidence crosses into the published record by its own signature. Publishing can be set for a later time, and everything is re-checked at that time. Publishing cannot be undone: a correction is a new edition, which must say what changed and why. Publication is followed by a docket of responses, and by withdrawal if it comes to that.

Bias. A group's declared bias is a set of statements that affect how it weighs evidence. A bias may raise scrutiny, constrain inference, or assert a pattern backed by evidence; it may never issue a verdict. The bias in force is printed whole into every published case, with the owner's own account of what that lens did to this edition's findings.

The case's account, and nothing but evidence. The system drafts the case's written account from the evidence, and may offer more than one framing (in time order, by question, by rule), each labelled as the system's. The member writes the account, from a draft or from nothing, and it is published as hers. Before publication, every sentence is checked against the evidence it cites. A sentence the evidence does not support is flagged: she ties it to evidence or removes it. A sentence the record contradicts is refused outright. Only framing that follows a documented bias statement may stay, marked as such in the text; an unsupported claim cannot simply be relabelled as bias. Lying is not bias. Besides the account, a case keeps four short statements about itself, held to the same check: what it says; why this subject; what was left out, and why; and, for a correction, what changed. It carries no stories, context or human-interest accounts, however relevant: "There are other publications where those stories belong - in a Civicsmith published case is not one of those places."

Bias in the case. Printing the bias in force stays required. Framing carried under it is optional, and allowed only when marked in the text and tied to a bias statement printed in the case.

Re-checking a case under another lens. When a bias statement is applied (more scrutiny on a source, an inference blocked), the leg or conclusion it touches records that statement, and the case file carries those records. The standalone checker takes a lens: as published, bias removed, or the reader's own. For each finding it reports whether it still meets the bar, and which statements made the difference, stating on every report the honest limit that a re-check re-weighs the analysis that exists but cannot write what another lens would have written.

Approval. A group may set its own rule requiring one or more named members' approval before any of its cases is signed. It is off by default, so a group of one is never blocked.

Reviewers. A body of evidence may hold more than a case needs; the publisher decides which evidence to cite and which to leave out, and states what was left out. A reviewer may disagree. The publisher decides whether to include a reviewer's comments in the case. A reviewer whose comments were not included may add them to the published case afterwards, as anyone outside may.

Another group's case. Any group can import a published case into a new, read-only project. Civicsmith then recreates every finding: signatures and fingerprints check, each passage is found where it is said to be, each grade recomputes the same, each relied-on finding meets the declared bar, and the case passes the same publication checks. The result is "recreated", "recreated in part", or "did not recreate". Each finding is also shown against the importing group's own bar; the source's bar never travels as the importer's. Recreating is not endorsing. The group may then accept findings by a reasoned act, naming the edition. An accepted finding can support the group's own work, marked as another group's, and never stronger than the edition it came from. If the source withdraws or revises, the acceptance is raised for re-evaluation. A group may accept finding by finding, or the whole case in one act: every finding that recreated, with one reason, and those that did not listed. Once readers can re-check under a lens, the importing group's own lens is applied in the assessment. An accepted finding becomes read-only evidence in the importer's record, marked as another group's. Anyone, member or not, can recreate a case with the standalone checker, without Civicsmith.

2 · Grandview, start to finish

How the design works for a parent whose child's school rebuild is a year late.

  1. Maria opens a project, "Grandview rebuild". Its objective: for each change to the completion date, the document that set it and the one that changed it, with the reason each gave, quoted; and the earliest district record showing the delay was known; each at grade B or better. It is met when every slip is accounted for, or a records request shows no record exists. The intake interview is a step in the project: a conversation with the assistant, or one page she fills in by hand.
  2. Her answers are kept as her account. Her memory that the district promised "one school year" becomes a step: find where the district said it. From her answers come questions. "When did the district first know the rebuild would run late?" is new. "Did change orders exceed the legal limit?" already exists, because a district-oversight project asked it. Maria's project draws on it too, and each sees the other, since neither is hidden.
  3. Maria takes steps. One is a records request for the construction manager's reports, on the first question, with the law's ten-day clock as its "by when". Another is reading the bond measure for background, a project step.
  4. Grandview has a small grant-funded key, with exploring set to "Ask every day" and a $30 exploring limit inclusive in $100 a month. One morning Maria's queue asks whether to explore two questions, roughly $3. She says yes. The system finds three board agendas on the district's site and names them to her; she captures them. It marks one, as its own labelled signal, as possibly cutting against the district's account.
  5. Maria adds that agenda to the evidence as a leg. She dismisses another find with a reason. The oversight project's members are offered the same finds on the shared question, labelled as the system's work, not as Grandview's.
  6. A step ends as a dead end: the state architect's tracker had nothing. Its outcome, what was looked at, and "the tracker only covers projects over $10m" are kept.
  7. The district's documents arrive. Maria ties a document a neighbour forwarded by chance to the records-request step. The district's newsletter turns up: it said construction would take "about 14 months", not one school year. Her memory stays her account; the newsletter is the evidence.
  8. The members conclude on the first question; its strength shows beside the project's bar. The project's page shows where the evidence stands against the objective, and the gaps; when every slip is accounted for, the objective is met. The other project may weigh the same evidence differently. If the two conclusions cannot both hold on the facts, each is told which other project's conclusion conflicts with its own, since neither is hidden.
  9. The members determine, against the rules that bind the district, whether it complied: for example, whether it reported the delay to its oversight committee as its bond rules require. Complied, did not, or unclear; the evidence decides.
  10. Maria writes the case's account, starting from one of the system's drafts. The system flags a sentence of hers ("the district hid the delay"): the record shows a delay reported late, not hidden. She rewrites it to what the evidence shows. The story of her son's year in portable classrooms stays out of the case.
  11. The group requires its board's approval before publishing (its own rule). A board member's review disagrees with leaving out one change order; the owner leaves it out, stating why, and the reviewer adds her comment to the published case afterwards. The owner signs, and the case is published with the group's bias printed in it.
  12. A parents' group in the next district imports the case. Civicsmith recreates every finding; they accept the case in one act and use its findings, marked as Grandview's, in their own investigation. Then the members plan what to do about it.
