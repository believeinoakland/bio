## U6 · A grand jury complaint, and the mandated reply months later

### 1. Situation

A tenants' association in the San Antonio district ("Fruitvale Avenue Tenants", invented) has a published case. On these invented facts, the City of Oakland's Department of Transportation has not delivered the annual sidewalk-repair progress reports that a 2023 City Council resolution requires. A determination, noncompliant against the resolution, sits beside the case. Letters to the Director of Transportation got no answer. On 2026-10-12 the group files a citizen complaint with the **Alameda County Civil Grand Jury** (19 jurors empanelled for the fiscal year, July to June, by the Superior Court). Then it waits.

- The jury never says whether it is investigating.
- If it takes the matter up, its final report comes out around June 2027, with findings (F1, F2 …) and recommendations (R1, R2 …).
- The report names who must respond, here the **Oakland City Council** and perhaps the Mayor or the City Administrator.
- The City Council's reply is due to the Presiding Judge 90 days later, around September 2027.
- The group reads the reply and decides whether the City did what it said. The 2027–28 jury may follow up.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Filing the complaint.**
- The jury's published complaint form is filed by mail or email. It asks:
  - what happened;
  - which agency;
  - what the complainant has already done, including which other agencies were contacted (*verify the current Alameda form's fields*);
  - supporting documents.
- Experienced complainants (League of Women Voters chapters, good-government groups, and retired jurors, who are often members of the California Grand Jurors' Association, CGJA) take care with the complaint:
  - They keep it short and factual, organised by finding.
  - They attach the primary documents rather than argument.
  - They point to the specific law or policy the agency is bound by.
  - They say what records exist and who holds them.
- The jury may investigate the operations of a county (Penal Code §925), a city (§925a), or a special district or joint powers agency (§933.5). It chooses what to investigate and owes the complainant nothing beyond an acknowledgement, if that.

**Secrecy.**
- Jurors are sworn to secrecy (§911; *verify* §924.1 for the disclosure offence).
- Witnesses called before the jury are typically admonished not to disclose their testimony (*verify* the rule for civil, as opposed to criminal, matters).
- A complainant is not a juror. Practice nonetheless treats the complaint itself as confidential to the jury. Publicising "we have complained to the grand jury" is seen as unwise, because it can look like pressure and can make the jury wary.
- The jury may release evidentiary material with a final report (§929), with names redacted.

**Before release.**
- A subject agency is given the part of the report about it two working days before public release, and must not disclose its contents before then (§933.05(f)).
- The jury may meet the subject beforehand to discuss findings (§933.05(e)).
- This is an embargo set by statute.

**The mandated reply** (Penal Code §933(c), §933.05).
- *Who replies, and when:*
  - The **governing body** of a public agency comments to the Presiding Judge within **90 days** after the final report is submitted.
  - An **elected county officer or agency head** for which the jury has responsibility under §914.1 comments within **60 days**, with a copy to the Board of Supervisors.
  - For a city, the report's "Responses required" list names who must respond. Read that list; do not assume.
- *What each reply must say:*
  - For each **finding**, the respondent agrees, or disagrees wholly or partly, saying which part and why (§933.05(a)).
  - For each **recommendation**, the respondent reports one of four things (§933.05(b)):
    1. it has been implemented, with a summary;
    2. it has not been implemented but will be, with a timeframe;
    3. it requires further analysis, with the analysis's scope and a timeframe not beyond **six months** from the report's publication (*verify the six-month text*);
    4. it will not be implemented because it is not warranted or not reasonable, with an explanation.
- *Where replies go:* they are filed with the court and kept with the report (§933(b), (c)). The Alameda jury posts reports and replies on its website.
- *How it reaches Oakland's agenda:* the City Administrator's staff drafts the reply, and the Council adopts it on an agenda item, typically on the consent calendar. The agenda item and its staff report are public on Legistar before the vote.

**Following up.**
- CGJA publishes a sample "past due response" letter that juries send to late respondents.
- Later juries often run a "response review" or "continuity" report on whether last year's replies were complete and whether "will be implemented" turned into implementation.
- Activists do the same by hand: they diff each "will implement by X" against the record after date X.

**Common failure points.**
- The complaint is a narrative with no documents and no law.
- The group goes public with the complaint.
- It is filed in April or May, when the outgoing jury is closing its work. A complaint may be carried to the next jury, but often is not (*verify Alameda practice*).
- The reply is late, or says "requires further analysis" with no scope or date.
- The reply says "will be implemented" and nobody checks.
- The jury has no enforcement power: a missing or non-compliant reply is itself a breach of §933(c)/§933.05, enforceable only through the court's or the next jury's attention.

### 6. Candidate questions for Bob

**Q-U6a · Is a confidential referral kept off everything published?**
*Background.* A grand jury complaint, an auditor's whistleblower report or a confidential tip to an oversight body is useful only if it stays confidential. Civicsmith already sends nothing itself. But the group's own "what we did" list, which can be published with a case, lists every letter the group sent. A complaint could become public through it.

*Options.*
- (a) **Mark it confidential, kept off every published product.** A member marks the action confidential, with a reason. It never appears in a published case or timeline. Inside the group it is visible only to members who can see the action.
  - Example: the case's timeline shows the letters to the Director, but not the grand jury complaint.
- (b) **Shown but redacted.** Published as "a referral to an oversight body (confidential)", with no body named.
  - Example: the timeline reads "12 Oct · confidential referral".
  - This reveals that a referral exists, which juries may dislike.
- (c) **No special rule.** Members leave such actions out of the case by hand.
  - This is error-prone, and the timeline is assembled by the system, not chosen line by line.

*Recommendation:* **(a).** It honours DEC-31 ("nothing non-public leaves by a system path") and Bob's K597 (5) ruling, and it is how complainants actually behave.

A sub-point for the same answer: if a member later testifies before the jury, Civicsmith records only that she appeared and when, never what was said, unless she chooses to record her own account as testimony. This follows the admonition practice (*verify*).

**Q-U6b · Does a referral to an oversight body need a determination first?**
*Background.* Today any action that claims a law was broken must rest on the group's own determination, made after publishing. A grand jury complaint, an Auditor whistleblower report or a referral to the State Controller asks a body with investigative power to look. The canon lists these as "file freely", and anyone may start one.

*Options.*
- (a) **Treat referrals as evidence-seeking, never gated.**
  - Example: a group with only a strong suspicion files a grand jury complaint before it has published anything.
- (b) **Treat referrals as breach actions, gated with the open override** (as now if marked `breach: true`).
  - Example: the same group must publish and determine first, or override with a reason, and the override's disclosure appears first on the complaint itself.
- (c) **Let the member choose per action, with an explanation of both.**

*Recommendation:* **(a)**, with one condition. If the referral's text asserts a breach as the group's finding, it must cite the published finding, or say plainly that it reports a suspicion. A referral is a request to investigate. Making it wait on the group's own determination would invert the order the canon intends: oversight bodies exist to find out. The rule "an action that seeks evidence is never gated" (Action §4 rule 2) already points this way.

## U7 · A demand letter citing the law: Brown Act cure-and-correct, its window, then litigation

### 1. Situation

At its regular meeting on Tuesday 2026-10-06, the **Oakland City Council** voted to approve a $4.2 million sole-source contract amendment that was not on the posted agenda (invented facts). It was raised under "open forum" and acted on that night. A neighbourhood transparency group (invented) has the posted agenda, the video and the action minutes captured.

Under the Brown Act, an action taken in open session in violation of the agenda requirement (Gov. Code §54954.2) can be voided. Before suing, the group must send a written demand to cure or correct, within **30 days** of the action: by about 2026-11-05.

- The Council then has **30 days from receipt** to cure, or to say in writing it will not. Silence counts as a refusal.
- After that, the group has **15 days** to file suit, or is barred.

Oakland's Sunshine Ordinance (OMC ch. 2.20) adds its own agenda rules for the Council and its committees.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**The statute** (Gov. Code §54960.1).
- *(b) The demand.* Before suing to void an action taken in violation of §§54953, 54954.2, 54954.5, 54954.6, 54956 or 54956.5, the district attorney or any interested person makes a written demand on the legislative body to cure or correct it. The demand must "clearly describe the challenged action … and nature of the alleged violation".
- *(c)(1) The demand window.* Within **90 days** of the action, or **30 days** if the action was taken in open session in violation of §54954.2.
- *(c)(2)–(3) The body's answer.* Within **30 days of receipt**, the body cures or corrects and informs the demander in writing, or informs them of its decision not to. Inaction for 30 days is deemed a decision not to cure.
- *(c)(4) The suit window.* The suit must be filed within **15 days** of receiving the written decision, or within 15 days of the 30-day period's end, whichever is earlier (*verify the exact wording of (c)(4)*).
- *(d) Exceptions.* Some actions cannot be voided, such as bonds, and contracts on which a party has relied in good faith (*verify the list*).
- *Fees.* Attorney's fees are available to a prevailing plaintiff (§54960.5).
- *The other route.* It is separate: a **cease-and-desist letter** under §54960.2 for a past practice. It must be sent within **nine months** of the alleged violation. The body may give an "unconditional commitment" within 30 days. Suit must follow within **60 days** of the response or of the 30-day period's end. That route seeks to stop future violations (§54960) and voids nothing.

**The Sunshine Ordinance.** It requires agenda posting earlier than the Brown Act's 72 hours for the Council and its committees (*verify*: OMC §2.20.080, 10 days for regular Council and committee meetings). Its enforcement runs through the **Public Ethics Commission** (OMC §2.20.270; *verify* whether a PEC complaint is a prerequisite to suit and on what timeline).

**What practitioners do.**
- Groups such as the First Amendment Coalition and Californians Aware publish template cure-and-correct letters and answer the public's questions.
- The letter:
  - names the body, the meeting date, the agenda item (or its absence) and the action taken;
  - quotes the agenda and the minutes;
  - cites §54954.2 and §54960.1;
  - asks that the action be rescinded, or re-noticed and reconsidered;
  - asks for a written response within 30 days.
- It is addressed to the legislative body, delivered to the City Clerk (Clerk of the Council), by a means that proves receipt: email to the Clerk with the read receipt kept, hand delivery with a stamped copy, or certified mail. The 30-day period runs from **receipt**.
- A common "cure" is legitimate: the body re-notices the item and re-takes the same action properly.
- Practitioners calendar three dates on day one:
  - the demand deadline;
  - the response deadline, counted from receipt;
  - the suit bar.
- They line up counsel *before* sending, because 15 days is too short to find a lawyer.

**Common failure points.**
- Missing the 30-day window. Many groups assume they have 90 days.
- A vague demand.
- No proof of receipt.
- Treating the City's letter ("we believe the item was properly before the Council") as the start of negotiation, and missing the 15-day bar.
- Not knowing whether the action is one that cannot be voided.
- Sending a demand the group cannot follow through on.
- Personal attacks on councilmembers in the letter. These invite defamation claims and dilute the point.

### 6. Candidate questions for Bob

**Q-U7a · A law gives only days to act, but the group's own rule says publish and determine first. What then?**
*Background.* Your rule (Action §4 rule 2, option (c)) refuses a letter that claims a breach unless the group has published a finding and determined the breach. For a Brown Act demand, the law gives 30 days from the meeting.

*Options.*
- (a) **Keep it as is.** The member overrides, and the City receives a letter that begins "Rests on an unestablished premise: …".
  - Honest, but it undermines the letter, and the group cannot use escalation for it.
- (b) **Make the fast route real.** Civicsmith helps a group publish a short case (one finding, from the agenda, video and minutes) and determine within days, so no override is needed. The override stays for true emergencies.
  - Example: a three-finding case is published on day 4, determined on day 5, and the demand goes on day 6.
- (c) **Keep the disclosure inside the group.** The override is recorded and shown on the group's copy and in the record, but not printed on the letter sent.
  - Easier for the group; weaker on your rule that "a departure … is visible to every reader".
- (d) **Let an overridden action join the escalation** once a determination is later made, with the override's disclosure kept in the history.

*Recommendation:* **(b)**, with (d) as a companion. (b) keeps your standard and fits the work, because a Brown Act case is usually small and documentary. (d) removes a trap: once the group has determined, the escalation should be able to pick up the letter already sent. I do not recommend (c). The letter is the one place the City reads.

**Q-U7b · How risky is a demand letter that starts a short legal clock?**
*Background.* Your Design Requirement 8 lists "Brown Act violation reports" as Tier 1 (file freely). *Options.*
- (a) **Tier 1.** Template, no advisory.
- (b) **Tier 2.** Template with the advisory note ("legal review recommended") and the three dates shown.

- (c) **The tier depends on intent.** A "violation report" to the City or the DA is Tier 1; the §54960.1 demand, as the prerequisite to suit, is Tier 2.

*Recommendation:* **(c).** It keeps your Tier 1 list true for plain reports. It warns where a procedural slip has a legal cost, which is the line your Tier 2 draws. It also makes the group line up counsel before the 15 days start.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Intake.**
- A conflict check and an engagement letter, which defines the client. Here the client is the association, an organisation (Evid. Code §951 "client" includes an association; the privilege is §954).
- The communications between the client's representatives and the lawyer for legal advice are privileged. So are the lawyer's work product (CCP §2018.030) and, in some circumstances, material shared under a common-interest arrangement.
- Sharing privileged material with outsiders, such as a reporter or another group, can **waive** the privilege.
- A pre-existing document does not become privileged by being sent to a lawyer.
- A chronology the group wrote for itself before consulting counsel is generally *not* privileged. One prepared at counsel's request may be work product (*verify for California's qualified/absolute split, CCP §2018.030(a)–(b)*).

**What the lawyer wants first.**
- A one-page summary.
- A **chronology** with a source for each line.
- The **key documents** as exhibits, numbered.
- **Who knows what** (witnesses: who attended the meeting, who has the video).
- What the client wants: void the contract, or stop future violations.
- Money: fees under §54960.5; CCP §1021.5 private attorney general fees; pro bono or contingency.

**Exhibits and authentication.**
- In California courts, writings must be authenticated (Evid. Code §§1400–1401).
- Official records and certified copies have their own routes (§§1280, 1530).
- A printed representation of computer information is presumed accurate (§1552), and images likewise (§1553). The presumption shifts the burden, but it can be rebutted (*verify*).
- A video of a public meeting is usually authenticated through the City's own archive (Granicus/Legistar) or by a witness.
- Federal courts accept certified electronic records under FRE 902(13)–(14). The canon already notes this (Action §4 rule 13).
- What counts: hashes, capture time, the URL, and who captured. The weak point is an exhibit only one person can vouch for.

**The lawyer's timetable.**
- The statutory bar: 15 days here. Elsewhere:
  - a Government Claims Act claim: 6 months or 1 year (Gov. Code §911.2), then suit within 6 months of a written rejection (§945.6);
  - a CPRA petition: no fixed limitation, but fees are mandatory for a prevailing requester (Gov. Code §7923.115, *verify the section number after recodification*);
  - a writ of mandate: depends on the underlying duty.
- Internal dates: draft, client review, verification signatures and filing.
- The lawyer asks the client for decisions by dates of her choosing. These are the group's commitments, not law.

**Preservation.** Once litigation is reasonably anticipated, the client must preserve relevant material. The lawyer sends a litigation-hold notice.

**Common failure points.**
- Counsel found too late.
- Exhibits that cannot be authenticated.
- A chronology without sources.
- Members discussing strategy in writing, in discoverable places.
- Forwarding the lawyer's advice to the whole mailing list or to a reporter (waiver).
- Deleting chat logs after a threat.
- The group sending its own letters to the City after counsel is engaged, contradicting counsel.

## U9 · Escalation through the seven stages when the body does not answer

### 1. Situation

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Auditors' follow-up.**
- City auditors, including Oakland's (Charter §403), and the State Auditor track each recommendation's status: implemented, partly, not implemented, no longer applicable.
- They do this on a fixed cycle, and they publish the list of open recommendations.
- Escalation means re-reporting to the governing body and to the public.

**Activists' ladder**, in practice:
1. A letter to the responsible office.
2. A follow-up letter with a stated date.
3. Public comment at the Board of Education's meeting. The Board must allow public comment on agenda items, and on non-agenda items within its jurisdiction (Gov. Code §54954.3).
4. A records request for the internal record of the delay (CPRA, Gov. Code §7922.535: 10 days, extendable by 14).
5. A request to the oversight body itself (here the bond oversight committee), asking for an agenda item.
6. A complaint to an outside oversight body (the Civil Grand Jury, U6). Or a request to the Legislature's Joint Legislative Audit Committee for a State Auditor audit (*verify the request process*).
7. Press (U10).
8. A request to elected officials: the Board of Education (elected), and the state legislator for the district.
9. If a ministerial duty is unperformed, a writ of mandate (CCP §1085), which is counsel's work. For waste of public funds, a taxpayer action (CCP §526a, Tier 3 in the profile).

**Practices that make the ladder work.**
- Each rung states the deadline the group set, and its basis.
- Each non-response is recorded as a dated fact: "we asked on X; nothing by Y".
- Each next letter cites the previous ones.
- The group keeps one chronology that a newcomer, a reporter or a lawyer can pick up.

**Common failure points.**
- Escalating before the record is strong, which lets the district discredit the whole case.
- Stopping after the first non-answer.
- Asking a body with no power over the matter.
- Confusing "they replied" with "they complied".
- No exit test, so the campaign drifts forever.
- Burnout. Groups rarely record why they stopped.

- Reminders only as the member chose when starting each action (DEC-94).
- Addressees are offices: "Superintendent, OUSD", with the holder shown, never addressed.

### 5. Gaps

1. [capability-unbuilt] Profile and registry: OUSD offices (Superintendent, Board of Education, the bond oversight committee) are not counterparties, and their `oversees`/`appoints` lines are not held. Stage 1's office trigger and stage 7's targets read "undetermined" (escalation R4, R12; Ladders ORG L2 "not built", stage 1 O1).
2. [doctrine-question] "Consequences addressed" for harm that cannot be undone. Escalation cannot end without it (R14; consequences R9). See Q-U9a.
3. [never-designed] Evidence-seeking rungs inside an escalation. A records request or a request for comment cannot be attached (R9 needs `breach: true`), so the escalation's history omits rungs practitioners count.
4. [never-designed] "We stopped": a group that ends its pursuit without compliance has only `suspend`. Nothing records a reasoned stop that is neither success nor pause. See Q-U9b.
5. [reach] No screen for any escalation act. Queue doors for `escalation-stage-proposed` are not drawn. Affordances derives only four action acts per object (R6 §4 item 2), so a screen cannot learn that escalation acts exist on an action.
6. [capability-unbuilt] The stage view ("where are we; what's proposed; since when") as one read across escalation, plan, actions and clocks: the "what we did" timeline lane (Ladders EVENTS L2, stage 2a).
7. [never-designed] Public comment as stage-7 testimony: the kind `public_comment` has no tier in the profile, and the U4 design must feed this.
8. [defect] Older canon still says escalation "activates" and lists six stages (working page §4.4). This is BOB's to correct.

### 6. Candidate questions for Bob

**Q-U9a · When can a group say the harm has been "addressed"?**
*Background.* Your escalation ends only when the body complies again and the consequences are addressed. Some harm cannot be undone: a year in portable classrooms, a missed election, money already spent. Today a member records "addressed" with evidence, but nothing says what counts.

*Options.*
- (a) **A member's reasoned judgment, as built,** with the evidence cited, and shown publicly where the group publishes its outcome.
  - Example: "Addressed: the district now reports monthly to the committee; the lost year cannot be restored; the Board adopted a recovery plan (cited)."
- (b) **A fixed set of ways a harm can be addressed,** each with its evidence: remedied, mitigated, compensated, acknowledged, cannot be addressed.
  - Example: "Mitigated: recovery plan (cited)."
- (c) **Allow ending an escalation on compliance alone,** with consequences left open and shown.

*Recommendation:* **(a), with (b)'s words offered as optional labels.** It keeps judgment with members (no score), gives readers a consistent vocabulary, and never lets a group claim a harm "fixed" when it was only acknowledged.

**Q-U9b · How does a group record that it stopped without winning?**
*Background.* Groups stop: the people move on, the effort outweighs the matter, or another group takes it up. Today an escalation can only be paused, never ended without compliance, so the record shows a pause forever.

*Options.*
- (a) **"Stopped pursuing", with a reason.**
  - It is separate from "ended" (compliance) and from "paused".
  - It is never read as success, and it can be reopened by a new escalation.
  - Example: "Stopped 2027-03: the oversight committee took the matter over; we follow its reports."
- (b) **Keep only pause.** A long pause speaks for itself.
- (c) **Stopping closes the plan only** (plans already close with a reason, action-plans R20); the escalation stays paused.

*Recommendation:* **(a).** It is honest and readable, and it supports a hand-off to another group. Your rule that a stopped escalation is never read as finished holds, because "stopped" is a third, explicit state.

## U10 · Working with a reporter: a tip, an embargo, the story, the city's response

### 1. Situation

Maria's Grandview group (U9) plans to publish an updated case edition with the district's change orders. A reporter at **The Oaklandside** (a nonprofit newsroom) covers OUSD.

1. On 2026-10-13 a member sends her a tip: a one-paragraph summary and an offer of the case before publication.
2. They agree an **embargo**: the reporter may see the draft case now; the group publishes at 06:00 on the day the story runs; the reporter will not publish before then.
3. Before publishing, the group must send the district a **request for comment** on its specific claims, with a response window (DEC-13).
4. The reporter seeks the district's own comment through its communications office.
5. The story runs 2026-10-22 at 06:00. It quotes the district spokesperson: "The district has kept the oversight committee informed throughout." The group's edition goes live at the same minute.
6. A week later the district issues a statement disputing one finding.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Tips.**
- Newsrooms take tips by email, signed or anonymous, and through tip forms. Some use secure channels such as SecureDrop or Signal.
- The best tips are short, verifiable, and point to documents. Reporters verify independently, so a group's published case with citations is the ideal tip.
- Reporters treat a source with a stake as a source with a stake. Disclosing the group's interest up front (D6) helps credibility.

**Attribution terms** (newsroom norms; definitions vary by outlet, so agree them explicitly):
- on the record;
- on background (use, but don't name the source);
- off the record (don't use).
- They are agreed **before** the statement, not after.

**Embargoes.**
- An embargo is an agreement, not law. Reporters may break it if the information becomes public elsewhere.
- Organisations give embargoed copies to reporters they trust, with a stated time and time zone.
- The organisation itself must not publish early.
- A statutory cousin: grand jury reports are given to subject agencies two working days early under §933.05(f) (U6).

**Right of reply.**
- Newsrooms ask the subject for comment before publishing (SPJ Code of Ethics: "seek sources whose conduct is criticized … an opportunity to respond").
- A group that publishes findings does the same, and Civicsmith's DEC-13 already requires a stated window.

**The city's response.**
- It arrives through the reporter: a quote in the story, a statement on the district's site, a letter to the editor, or a correction request to the outlet.
- Groups record it, answer it on their own channel if needed, and correct their own case if the response shows an error.

**Legal backdrop** (information, not advice).
- Statements about public officials' official conduct are protected unless made with actual malice (*New York Times v. Sullivan*).
- California's anti-SLAPP statute (CCP §425.16) protects statements made in connection with a public issue.
- California's shield law (Cal. Const. art. I §2(b); Evid. Code §1070) protects journalists from contempt for refusing to disclose sources. It does not protect the group.

**Common failure points.**
- An embargo with no agreed time zone.
- The group publishing early.
- A member speaking "for the group" without authority.
- Off-the-record comments made after the fact.
- Sharing the group's plan or counsel's advice with the reporter (strategy leaks; privilege waived, U8).
- Not recording the official's quote at publication, so a later "we never said that" cannot be answered.
- Treating the story as the outcome.

### 3. Today in Civicsmith

1. **Request for comment to the district.** Kind `request_for_comment` (product kind; actions R10). It must name the inquiries it puts (an `advances` leg) and carry a clock entry, the window (action-grammar R6; DEC-13). It is evidence-seeking, so never gated (actions R8). **[usable]** in the add flow (kind, legs, clock); text via `communicationPrepare` **[built, no screen]**.
   - The window's length is never enforced (K603).
   - Non-response is a dated fact (DEC-13, DEC-14).
2. **The tip as an action.** Kind `media` (profile, Tier 1). Addressee arm `{kind: press, role, organisation}`, e.g. "education reporter, The Oaklandside" (actions R9). **[usable]**
   - The arm has no field for the reporter's name, though the tip goes to a named journalist (see Q-U10a).
   - Text: `communicationPrepare` (filings R23), approved (R6), sent by hand and recorded (R7), with the in-band stamp (R22). **[built, no screen]**; the sent entry via `actioncorrespond` **[usable]**.
3. **Embargoed early copy.** The **review copy** (review R1–R19; Publication §6A): a draft case shown to a named recipient through a revocable read-and-comment grant, marked "not a publication", the secret never in the address (publication R73). **[usable]**: the old interface reaches `casedraft`, `reviewgrant` and `reviewcopy`.
   - The grant carries no embargo terms: no time, no zone, no agreed attribution.
   - It dies when the edition is published (review R8). That is right.
   - The reporter can comment (R18). Comments stay inside the instance.
4. **Publish at the embargo time.** `scheduleEdition` publishes a signed edition at a local date and time in the group's zone (publication R66). At its time it re-runs every check, including "a hold", and stops once if anything changed (R67). Move or cancel by an owner (R68). The queue shows "Signed · publishes <date, time>" (queue-producers R37). **[built, no screen]**
   - The old interface publishes only now.
5. **The district's quote in the story.** Capture the story (a captured page). The quote is a statement by an office in its official role and is information, not correspondence: it was not addressed to the group. **[usable]** as capture.
   - Whether it is the district's answer to the group's request for comment is a member's linking act. `responds_to` works only for a `received` entry with a capture (actions R17).
   - A statement made to the press is not a `received` entry on the group's action. **[not designed]**
6. **The district's later statement.** On a published case it belongs on the **docket** as a response from the subject, which may `contest` a finding (docket R1). The docket offers re-evaluation and the offer of a plan checkpoint (R3). Public entries register as "what we did" (R26). **[built, no screen]**
7. **The outcome.** The media action resolves `completed` (K590 D3; actions R13–R14). **[usable]** via `actionmove`.
   - Nothing records what the story did: whether the district acted after it. DEC-14 says the group's own action's outcome is never evidence of impact without a leg on evidence that is not the group's own action.
8. **Pressure.** If the district's counsel writes to the group threatening suit over the story, the letter is marked `legal` pressure (actions R48) and can carry a hold (R52; U8). **[built, no screen]**

### 4. What Civicsmith should do

**The member who uses the assistant.**
- *The tip:* drafted from the published case or the review copy. It states the group's stake (D6), the findings with links, and what the group will and will not say. It is a labelled proposal.
- *The embargo:* the assistant explains embargo norms as information, and helps write the terms in words for the member to send: time, zone, scope, attribution.
- *The request for comment to the district:* drafted with each specific claim and the window. The answer (or the non-answer) is laid out claim by claim when it arrives.
- *After the story:* offer to capture the story at publication time (one act), and to propose links:
  - the spokesperson's quote → the request for comment it appears to answer;
  - a contested finding → the docket.
  The member links.
- *Where it must stop:*
  - It never tells the member what to say to the press about the merits beyond the published case.
  - It never shares the plan, counsel matters or unpublished findings.
  - It never writes the member's reason for an act (K1841).

**The member who never uses the assistant** gets:
- a "Talk to a reporter" kind (the profile's `media`) with a template tip from the library;
- an "early copy for a reporter" step: the review copy grant plus a terms note;
- "publish at the agreed time" (scheduleEdition) in the same flow, with the time zone shown in words;
- a "record what was published" step: capture the story's URL.

**Rules the design must keep.**
- **Nothing leaves by a system path.** The review copy is reached by the reporter through a revocable grant to a draft. That is reading inside the instance, not sending, which is consistent with DEC-31's "pre-publish version". Sending the tip is the member's own hand.
- **The plan is never in a review copy** (DEC-25), and counsel matters never are (Q-U8a).
- **People.** Reporters are addressed by role and outlet. Officials quoted are named in their official role (K1483). The group's own members are never named to the press by the system.
- **A press quote is a captured statement, not the group's finding.** Contesting it is the docket's work, and a correction to the group's own case is a new edition.

### 5. Gaps

1. [doctrine-question] A reporter is a person in a professional role. The press arm (actions R9) holds role and outlet only, yet tips and embargo terms go to a named journalist. See Q-U10a.
2. [capability-unbuilt] Review copy grants carry no embargo terms (time, zone, attribution) and no "release at" link to the scheduled edition (review R6; publication R66). The two exist separately.
3. [reach] Scheduled publication, docket filing, pressure and communications have no screen. The old interface publishes only now.
4. [never-designed] A body's statement made to a third party (press, website) as a response to the group's request for comment. `responds_to` (actions R17) covers only a received entry with a capture.
5. [never-designed] "What did the story do?" No design exists for following a story's effect without breaking DEC-14 (the group's own act is never evidence of impact). This joins U16's "did it work".
6. [never-designed] Attribution terms (on record, background, off record) for what members say to a reporter, and who may speak for the group, have no record. See Q-U10b.
7. [capability-unbuilt] The publish-at-a-set-time check for "a hold" (publication R67) does not read actions' litigation hold, because of layering (R6 §4 item 4). A story that draws a legal threat before the scheduled time would not stop the publication.

### 6. Candidate questions for Bob

**Q-U10a · May an action addressed to the press name the reporter?**
*Background.* Your rule: actions are addressed to roles, never private individuals. A reporter is a person, but working in a public, professional role, like an official. Tips and embargo terms go to a specific journalist, and the group needs to know whom it told.

*Options.*
- (a) **Role and outlet only, as now.** The member keeps the name outside Civicsmith.
  - Example: "education reporter, The Oaklandside".
- (b) **Role and outlet, plus the journalist's byline name as "who holds the role"** (like an office's holder), shown inside the group and never published by the system.
  - Example: "education reporter, The Oaklandside · [byline name]".
- (c) **Name the reporter as the addressee.**

*Recommendation:* **(b).** It mirrors your office-holder rule (K1484 row 5), so the action stays addressed to the role. It records what the group actually did. It never publishes a journalist's name from the group's record.

**Q-U10b · Should Civicsmith record who may speak for the group, and on what terms?**
*Background.* When members talk to reporters, newsrooms ask whether they speak for the group and on what terms (on the record, on background, off the record). Groups get hurt when a member speaks "for the group" without authority, or when terms are disputed afterwards.

*Options.*
- (a) **A group setting naming its spokespeople, plus an "attribution terms" note on each media action.**
  - Example: "Spokesperson: the chair; terms agreed 13 Oct: on the record, embargo 22 Oct 06:00 Pacific."
- (b) **The terms note only.** Who speaks is the group's internal matter.
- (c) **Neither.** Outside Civicsmith.

*Recommendation:* **(b).** It records the agreement that matters, with no new role or gate on members (your rule that no member attribute gates anything). A group that wants spokespeople can say so in its own terms note.

## Cross-cutting patterns

1. **Clocks from the law that the profile does not yet hold.** Every case here turns on a statutory period the Oakland profile lacks: Penal Code §933(c) 60/90 days, §933.05's statuses and six months, Gov. Code §54960.1's 30/90, 30 and 15 days, §54960.2's 9 months, 30 and 60 days, claim bars for counsel. The machinery exists (action-clocks R2, R13; jurisdictions R26, R58; duties R2–R5). The data, with primary sources (K1445), is the work, together with an explicit **receipt** fact as the start event.
2. **A duty owed by the body, watched by the group.** The grand jury reply, the cure-or-not answer, a promise in a reply: each is a body's duty (duties R4, R5), not the group's action clock. They need one design that reads "what is owed to whom by when" without double-telling (R6 §4 item 6: an action clock and a duty occurrence for one date).
3. **Confidential, privileged and never-published scopes.** A grand jury referral (U6), counsel matters (U8), the plan (DEC-25) and embargoed copies (U10) all need a stated scope. The "what we did" lane (actions R66, K1494) is the leak path in each. One rule is needed: which acts may ever enter a published timeline, and who inside the group may see each.
4. **The breach gate against the outside world's timing.** A statutory window (U7), a referral meant to investigate (U6) and a request for comment before publishing (U10) each meet Action §4 rule 2 differently. They need a clear line between "asks someone to find out" (never gated) and "asserts our finding" (gated), and a fast route to a real determination.
5. **Replies that are not addressed to the group.** A grand jury report, a press quote, a district statement on its own site: all respond to the group's acts but are not `received` correspondence. They need a "response found elsewhere" link alongside `responds_to`.
6. **Following after sending.** Proceedings (entities R45; actions R65), portals (following R10), reports pages and dockets are built. Profile `proceeding_kinds` and the member's door are missing. Every use case here ends in "and then we watched for months".
7. **Office registry beyond the City.** County (grand jury), school district (OUSD, its committees) and press outlets need registry entries and lines. Without them, escalation's stage 1 and stage 7 (R4, R12) and addressee suggestions (actions R62) read "undetermined".
8. **Ending honestly.** These cases need a resolution vocabulary that tells apart: no reply owed (grand jury), deemed refusal (Brown Act), stopped without winning (escalation), compliance plus consequences (escalation R14), and "the story ran" (media). Today's resolutions (complied, denied, escalated, withdrawn, completed) and `suspend` cover these only roughly.
9. **Assistant boundaries are the same in all five.**
   - *Yes:* explain the law with citations; lay out conditions as met, unmet or undetermined; draft labelled proposals that follow the member's choice; read replies against the statute's outcomes.
   - *No:* whether to file, which lawyer, evaluating a response, a member's reason.
   - Each has a screen-and-template equivalent for the member who never uses it (D42).
10. **Reach first.** In every use case the old interface reaches create, correspond, move, laws, tier, the review copy and publishing now. Determinations, filings, packets, escalation, plans, duties, holds, scheduled publishing, the docket and following have no screen. The queue shows their to-dos without their doors.
