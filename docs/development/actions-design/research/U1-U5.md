## U1 · A records request: from an investigation's gap to the reply, a fee quote, a partial denial, and a challenge

### 1. Situation

*Lake Merritt Budget Watch* is investigating whether the City of Oakland's Department of Transportation (OakDOT) spent Measure U bond money on the projects the bond report listed. The project objective: for each FY 2024–25 Measure U disbursement, the council resolution that authorised it and the invoice that paid it, at grade B or better. The project page lists a gap: "invoices for 14 disbursements: none held."

Member Dana (who uses the assistant) and member Luis (who never does) decide to request:
- the invoices and payment records;
- the contract change orders;
- email between OakDOT's Measure U program manager's office and the Finance Department about re-allocations, 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025.

The rest of the record runs as follows:
- **Thu 15 Oct 2026.** Dana submits through the City's NextRequest portal, under the CPRA and the Sunshine Ordinance.
- **Day 10.** The City sends an extension notice under §7922.535(b), citing "need to search for and collect records from a field facility" and "voluminous records".
- **Day 24.** The City produces the invoices. It quotes **$412.50** for "staff time to redact and convert 1,650 pages of email to PDF". It withholds part of the emails under §7927.500 (preliminary drafts), §7927.705 with Evidence Code §954 (attorney–client), and the "catchall" §7922.000.
- The group thinks the fee is unlawful and the denial overbroad. It challenges both: first by asking the City to reconsider, then through the Public Ethics Commission's records mediation under the Sunshine Ordinance, and finally by weighing a petition in Alameda County Superior Court under Gov. Code §7923.000 et seq.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Before the request** (newsrooms, the First Amendment Coalition, the Reporters Committee's guides, MuckRock practice):
- Check whether the record is already public (the portal's published responses, Legistar attachments, the bond oversight committee's reports).
- Describe identifiable records, not questions. Name the date range, custodian offices and formats ("in native electronic format, Gov. Code §7922.570").
- Ask for a rolling production.
- Cite every law that applies. The CPRA (Gov. Code §7920.000 et seq.) and Oakland's Sunshine Ordinance (OMC ch. 2.20) both apply to the City.
- Use an **Immediate Disclosure Request** (OMC §2.20.230) for simple, routine records. It is 3 business days in the profile; verify its scope limits before relying on it.
- Keep the portal's receipt. The 10 days run from receipt, and the City Attorney's staff guide treats a request submitted on a non-business day as received on the next business day (profile `receipt`; K1504 (3)).

**The deadlines and their basis:**
- **10 calendar days** to *determine* whether disclosable records exist and to notify the requester (§7922.535(a)). This is a deadline to determine, not to produce.
- **Production** must be "promptly available" (§7922.530(a)). There is no fixed date. Practitioners ask for an estimated date, which §7922.535(c) requires the agency to give when it determines records will be produced. **Verify** the subsection; the duty to state an estimated date is long-standing in the former §6253(c).
- **Extension**: up to 14 more days, by written notice, only for the listed "unusual circumstances" (§7922.535(b), (c)(1)–(6)). An extension that names no listed circumstance is a common failure, and practitioners object to it in writing.
- **A denial** must be in writing when the request was made in writing, and must name each person responsible (§7922.540). The agency must justify withholding by an express exemption or the public-interest balance (§7922.000). Reasonably segregable non-exempt parts must be released (§7922.525(b)).
- **A duty to assist** in identifying records (§7922.600). Practitioners use it when the agency says a request is "overbroad".
- **Fees**: only "direct costs of duplication" (§7922.530(a)). For electronic records, programming or extraction costs are allowed only in the cases §7922.575 lists. *National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward* (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488 held that an agency **may not charge for redaction** of electronic records. A $412.50 charge for redaction staff time is the classic failure. Inspection is free. The CPRA has **no fee-waiver procedure** (FOIA has one).

**Challenging** (this is where groups most often lose time):
- **There is no administrative appeal under the CPRA.** Practitioners send a short "meet and confer" letter citing the sections and asking the agency to reconsider (to withdraw the fee, give the segregable portions, justify each exemption by record, or provide a log). Courts weigh such letters when they award fees.
- **Oakland's route:** the Public Ethics Commission (PEC) runs a **public records mediation program** under OMC §2.20.270. The requester files a "Request for Mediation" form once a request has been denied (or not answered). The form and its attachments become public records, and a copy goes to the persons named. After mediation the requester may file a complaint with the PEC, or seek a court order. The PEC "has no authority to impose fines for Sunshine violations" and reported a severe complaint backlog in 2026 (PEC pages and its 16 Sept 2026 agenda; web check). **Verify** §2.20.270's exact wording and any precondition such as how long the requester must wait after the request.
- **The petition** (Gov. Code §7923.000 et seq.):
  - Any person may sue for injunctive or declaratory relief or a writ of mandate (§7923.000), by verified petition in the superior court of the county where the records are (§7923.100). That is Alameda County Superior Court, in the René C. Davidson Courthouse civil clerk's office, as the profile states.
  - The court sets the times for responsive pleadings and hearings "with the object of securing a decision … at the earliest possible time" (§7923.005).
  - A prevailing requester recovers costs and reasonable attorney fees (§7923.115). This is why counsel take these cases.
  - Review of the trial court's order is only by extraordinary writ, petitioned within 20 days of service of notice of entry (§7923.500). **Verify** the day count and the extension available.
  - No exhaustion of a mediation step is required before the petition.
  - Limitations: no CPRA-specific period. Practitioners treat CCP §343's four years as the outside bound, but act fast because records age. **Verify.**
  - It is Tier 2 in the profile ("file with caution … legal review before filing is recommended").

**Documents a careful requester keeps:** the request as sent (bytes), the portal receipt, every notice with its date, each production with a page count, the fee quote verbatim, the denial with the exemptions as cited, a log of what was asked and when, and a running chronology. Newsrooms keep a "request tracker" per agency, and a body's pattern of extensions and late responses becomes a story in its own right.

**Common failure points:**
- the clock counted from the sending, not from receipt;
- an extension accepted without checking it names a listed circumstance;
- a fee paid that the law did not allow;
- a partial denial not challenged record by record;
- the group losing the thread between the portal, email and the PEC;
- a petition filed without the record of what was asked and refused;
- the group's own follow-up not dated.

### 4. What Civicsmith should do

**The request as one thread.** One records-request action holds the whole round trip as staged correspondence (D-147): request → acknowledgement → extension notice → production(s) → fee estimate → denial (partial) → reconsideration request → reply → mediation → petition. Each stage carries its date and what it follows. The member sees it as a timeline: "Asked 15 Oct · determination due 26 Oct (CPRA §7922.535(a), counted from receipt on 16 Oct) · extended to 9 Nov, reason given 'field facility; voluminous' · produced in part 9 Nov · fee quoted $412.50 'staff time to redact' · withheld in part: §7927.500, §7927.705/Evid. §954, §7922.000". Every date shows its basis kind, and only a `rule` date is called a deadline the law sets (K1431).

**Start from the gap.** On the project's gap "invoices for 14 disbursements: none held", the member is offered "Ask the office for them". This opens the request with:
- the step linked;
- the addressee suggested from the registry (R62) and adopted by her;
- the records described in her words.

The step's "by when" *is* the action's clock entry: one date, one reminder, shown in both places (§0 item 7; see question U1-Q3).

**Deadlines computed, shown and adopted.** The law's dates come from the profile: `clockPropose`'s trace in plain words ("10 calendar days after receipt; receipt was the next business day because you submitted on a Saturday; the 10th day fell on a court holiday, so it rolls to …"). The member adopts each date in one act (R13). An uncertain date shows both candidates (K1444). The production date stays "not fixed by law: ask for the estimate the law requires" (legal information, cited).

**The reply, read against the law, without judging.** When a reply is recorded, Civicsmith may lay beside it, labelled as legal information from held text (K1474 (ii)), never as a conclusion:
- for an extension notice: §7922.535(c)'s six listed circumstances, and the notice's stated reason quoted;
- for a fee quote: §7922.530(a)'s "direct costs of duplication" and §7922.575's cases, with the quote verbatim and the group's earlier quotes from the same office side by side (D-148);
- for a denial: each exemption as cited, the text of the cited section, §7922.540's naming requirement, and §7922.525(b)'s segregability rule.

It never says "this fee is unlawful" or "you should petition" (K1474; Action rule 1). Whether the quote exceeds the law is the member's claim in an inquiry (D-148). A case-law reading (*Hayward*) belongs in a guide (Investigation §6 reading guides), not in a generated answer.

**What she may do next, as information.** At a denial, the action page lists the routes the profile holds, each with its own cited facts:
- ask the agency to reconsider (no statutory step);
- PEC mediation (OMC §2.20.270; the form becomes public);
- PEC complaint;
- a petition (tier 2, venue, advisory).

The routes are listed in the profile's order, with no ranking or "recommended" mark. This is a procedural fact list (K1474 (iii)), not advice.

**Help for the member who uses the assistant:**
- drafting the request, the reconsideration letter and the mediation request from her chosen route, as proposals she adopts (D2; DEC-153), never in a reason field (K1841);
- reading a 40-page production and listing what it holds against her request ("asked for: change orders; found: none in this production"), labelled as machine work;
- noting that the denial names no responsible person, as a "Noticed" fact, not a violation.

**For the member who never uses it** (D42): approved templates for the request, the reconsideration letter and the PEC mediation request (K903 (6), K921), offered with their notes; a checklist of the law's parts shown with each reply; the same timeline; the same profile routes.

**Reminders.** Only those she sets, offered with visible defaults when she records the sending (DEC-94 (1); K613). Overdue is told once. A nearing date changes display only.

**Where the AI stops:**
- it never chooses the route, the exemption to contest or the amount to pay;
- it never sends or files;
- it never states that the City broke the law;
- a petition draft is prepared only after the member has chosen to petition;
- the petition is tier 2: the advisory shows, and the counsel packet is offered.

**The gate.** Requesting, following up and reconsidering are evidence-seeking and never gated. Whether a PEC complaint or a petition about the group's own request needs a published determination is question U1-Q1.

**Never published.** The plan and the counsel packet never are. The group's request, the City's replies and a non-response are dated facts ("We asked on 15 Oct; no determination by 26 Oct") and may become evidence (DEC-14; Case Making §8). Our own missed checkpoint is never a finding about the City (K1466).

### 6. Candidate questions for Bob

**U1-Q1 · Is challenging the group's own unanswered or refused records request "asserting a breach"?**

*Background.* Your rule (Action §4 rule 2, option (c)) says an action asserting a breach is refused unless it rests on a published finding and a member's determination, though a member may override it openly with a reason. Evidence-seeking actions (requests) are never held back. A PEC complaint or a court petition about our own request says "the City did not comply with the records law". Read literally, the group would first have to publish a case about its own request and determine it, before complaining to the PEC or petitioning.

*Options:*
- **(a) Treat it as any breach (today's rule).** Example: Dana must publish "The City withheld Measure U emails" as a case, determine it noncompliant, then file. Or she overrides with a reason, and the complaint carries the disclosure.
- **(b) Treat the whole enforcement of the group's own request as evidence-seeking.** It is never gated. Example: Dana files the PEC mediation request straight from the denial entry.
- **(c) A narrow class, "enforcing the group's own request", ungated but resting on the request's own dated record.** The complaint or petition may assert only what the action's ledger holds: sent on a date, the due date with its citation, the reply or silence, the exemptions as cited. Anything wider (for example "the City routinely hides records") still needs a determination. Example: the PEC complaint is prepared with the ledger as its exhibits, and a sentence about a pattern is refused unless a determination backs it.

*Trade-offs.* (a) is faithful to the letter but makes the most ordinary act of a records requester slow and public before the fact. (b) is fast but lets a group file broad accusations unchecked under cover of a request. (c) keeps the group's standard (the claim rests on what the record holds) without forcing publication, at the cost of a new rule for BOB to define.

*Recommendation: (c).* The claim is about the group's own correspondence, which the record holds at full strength as dated facts (DEC-14; Case Making §8). Requiring a published case would defeat the speed the law intends (§7923.005), and the narrow scope keeps rule 2's protection for every wider claim.

## U2 · A request for comment to an official before the group publishes

### 1. Situation

The Measure U investigation (U1) is ready to publish. Its findings include:
- three FY 2024–25 disbursements of **$1.86M** were paid on change orders the Council never approved, although the City's contracting rules require Council approval above a threshold (a determination to be made after publication);
- OakDOT reported the projects "complete" in the bond's annual report while invoices show work continuing.

Before signing, the group wants the City to answer. Dana puts four specific claims, with the documents, to **the Director of Transportation (OakDOT)** and **the City Administrator's Office**. Media inquiries go through the City's communications office, so the group copies it. The group allows **10 calendar days**.
- OakDOT acknowledges on day 2.
- On day 9 it replies to two claims, saying the change orders were "administratively approved under the City Administrator's delegated authority", and asks that its reply be published "in full".
- The City Administrator's Office does not reply.
- After publication, OakDOT sends a statement disputing a figure.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**The practice.**
- Newsrooms, investigative nonprofits and auditors all give the subject a chance to respond before publishing. The SPJ Code of Ethics: "Diligently seek subjects of news coverage to allow them to respond to criticism or allegations of wrongdoing."
- Government auditors give the audited agency a draft and publish its written response with the report. GAO's agency-comment protocol uses 7–30 days. The California State Auditor and Oakland's City Auditor publish the department's response, and the auditor's comments on it, in the report.
- The Columbia Journalism School review of *Rolling Stone*'s 2014 story found that withholding specifics from the subject was a central failure. A request must state each claim, not "we're writing about you".

**The steps.**
1. List each claim the piece will make about the subject, with its evidence.
2. Send them in writing to the office (the department head plus the public information officer). State a reasonable deadline and how the response will be used (quoted in full, summarised, or linked).
3. Log every contact attempt: date, channel and person.
4. Hold publication until the deadline, or publish with "did not respond by [date]".
5. Read the reply against the evidence. New facts may change a finding.
6. Quote fairly, in context, and in substance. Many outlets publish the full statement.
7. After publication, append later responses or corrections.

**Deadlines.** None is set by law. Newsroom norms for a complex investigation run from 48 hours to two weeks; auditors use about 30 days. A deadline that is unreasonably short is itself criticised and can bear on fault in a defamation claim.

**Legal frame (legal information, verify each point).** Statements about public officials' official conduct are protected unless made with actual malice (*New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964)). A documented, specific request for comment, and a fair account of the reply, are classic evidence against reckless disregard (*St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968)). California's anti-SLAPP statute (CCP §425.16) protects statements on public issues. Civil Code §48a's correction-demand procedure applies to newspapers and broadcasters, and whether it reaches a civic group's online case is uncertain (verify). The fair-report privilege (Civil Code §47(d)) protects fair reports of official proceedings.

**Failure points:**
- vague questions;
- a deadline over a holiday or weekend;
- sending to a person's private email instead of the office;
- not logging attempts;
- ignoring a reply that undercuts a finding;
- quoting selectively;
- treating "no comment" as an admission;
- publishing a reply marked "not for attribution" as attributed.

## U3 · A complaint to an oversight office, and following what it did

### 1. Situation

During the Measure U work, a city employee on the parents' committee (a member of the group, Rosa) tells the group that OakDOT staff split a contract into pieces under the competitive-bid threshold. The group has two documents: purchase orders with consecutive numbers, the same vendor, and amounts each just under the threshold. It has not concluded or published anything.

Two oversight routes exist:
- **The Oakland City Auditor's Whistleblower Program** (OMC ch. 2.38; hotline run on EthicsPoint, open 24/7 to employees, contractors, vendors and residents, anonymous allowed). The Auditor investigates fraud, waste and abuse and publishes substantiated investigations (for example the 2022 civilian fire inspectors report) and an annual whistleblower report (FY 2024–25, Nov 2025). A separate retaliation complaint must be filed within 180 days of the retaliation.
- **The Public Ethics Commission**, if an official's conduct falls under a law it enforces: the Government Ethics Act, the Campaign Reform Act, lobbyist registration, the Sunshine Ordinance. **Verify** each OMC chapter: GEA ch. 2.25, OCRA ch. 3.12 and lobbyist registration ch. 3.20 are the worker's recollection. The PEC takes complaints from anyone, does intake and preliminary review, and may investigate, settle or hold a hearing.

The group files with the Auditor in the group's name, and Rosa wants her own role kept out of every record that could leave. Eight months later, the group wants to know what happened.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Choosing the office** comes before anything else. Each office's jurisdiction is narrow:
- the Auditor takes fraud, waste and abuse involving City operations;
- the PEC takes violations of the laws it enforces;
- the Civil Grand Jury takes any local government matter (U6);
- the District Attorney takes crimes;
- the State Auditor takes state agencies, plus local audits the Legislature's Joint Legislative Audit Committee requests;
- the Fair Political Practices Commission takes state Political Reform Act matters such as Form 700.

Practitioners pick the office whose powers fit, sometimes several in parallel, and say so in each complaint.

**Writing the complaint** (auditors' intake guidance and newsroom tip practice):
- State facts, not conclusions: who (by office), what, when, where, how the group knows, and which documents support it.
- Attach copies, and keep the originals.
- Identify the rule the conduct appears to break, without asserting guilt.
- Ask that the matter be examined.
- Say whether the complainant may be contacted.
- Keep a dated copy of what was sent, plus the hotline's report number and password (EthicsPoint issues both for anonymous follow-up).

**Protections:**
- Employees are protected against retaliation by OMC ch. 2.38, Labor Code §1102.5, and Gov. Code §53296 et seq. for local agency employees who report to an investigating agency. **Verify** the scope of §53296–53299.
- Complainant identity is kept confidential by the Auditor.
- Practitioners advise a whistleblower to file in her own name only after advice, or to file anonymously and preserve evidence of the report's date.

**Deadlines.** Filing an Auditor or PEC complaint has no statutory deadline; PEC enforcement may face limitation periods (verify the five-year enforcement limit under the City's ordinances). Retaliation has 180 days (OMC 2.38). A claim for damages against the City runs 6 months or 1 year (Gov. Code §911.2, in the profile).

**Following what it did.** This is the hard part: oversight investigations are confidential while open. Practitioners:
- use the hotline's report number to check status;
- watch the Auditor's published reports and annual whistleblower report, and the PEC's agendas, where complaint dismissals, settlements and probable-cause findings appear;
- after closure, request the outcome under the CPRA (personnel and investigative exemptions often apply: §7927.705 with Penal Code / Gov. Code provisions, §7923.600 for investigatory files; verify);
- note when nothing is reported and ask again.

**Failure points:**
- the wrong office;
- conclusions instead of facts;
- a complaint that names a whistleblower;
- no copy kept;
- losing the report number;
- treating "no public report" as "nothing happened";
- publishing before the office acts, which can tip off subjects and spoil evidence.

- **(b) Her account stays in her hidden project.** The group's complaint rests on the documents she chooses to share, and the record names no source. Example: the group sees five purchase orders "provided by a member", nothing more.

## U4 · Public comment at a council meeting: preparing, the time limit, getting on the record, and what the minutes show

### 1. Situation

The Oakland City Council will hear an item adopting the **Measure U FY 2026–27 expenditure plan** at its regular meeting on **Tuesday 3 November 2026**. The group has published its case: three disbursements paid on change orders the Council never approved. It wants to say so at public comment and ask the Council to require Council approval of those change orders as the City's rules already say. It is not asking for a different spending policy.

- Luis will speak in person.
- Dana will submit a written comment through the City's eComment system before the meeting.
- A Spanish-speaking parent, Marisol, will speak through an interpreter.

Afterwards the group wants its comments in the record, and to see what the minutes say.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**The law** (legal information; verify the Oakland specifics):
- **Brown Act §54954.3(a):** every regular meeting agenda gives the public an opportunity to address the body directly on any agenda item, before or during the body's consideration of it, and on items of interest within its jurisdiction. At a special meeting, only on noticed items.
- **§54954.3(b)(1):** the body may adopt reasonable regulations, including time limits per item and per speaker. The Attorney General has said limits must be reasonable (for example, five minutes or less depending on the number of speakers; 75 Ops.Cal.Atty.Gen. 89 (1992), opinion 92-212; verify).
- **§54954.3(b)(2):** a speaker using a translator gets at least twice the time, unless simultaneous translation is provided. **Verify** the subsection.
- **§54954.3(c):** the body may not prohibit public criticism of its policies, programs, services, acts or omissions.
- **§54954.2(a)(1):** the agenda is posted 72 hours before a regular meeting (in the profile). **§54957.5:** agenda writings distributed to the body are public, and those distributed less than 72 hours before are available when distributed.
- **Oakland:** the Sunshine Ordinance (OMC ch. 2.20) adds longer notice for some meetings (the profile holds only §2.20.070's special-meeting rules; **verify** the regular-agenda posting rule, recalled as 10 days for Council meetings). The Council's Rules of Procedure set public comment practice: speaker cards or online sign-up, eComment, a per-speaker limit the chair sets by the number of speakers (historically 1–2 minutes; **verify** on the current agenda), and Open Forum for non-agenda items.
- **Minutes:** the City Clerk keeps them (Gov. Code §40801). Oakland's draft minutes are due within ten business days (in the profile's `practice.minutes_due_days`; time-law §2, K1504). Action minutes usually record the number of speakers, sometimes names, and not what they said. Video (KTOP/Granicus) is the fuller record. Written eComments are typically attached to the item in Legistar. **Verify** current Oakland practice.
- **If the body acts unlawfully** (for example on an item not properly noticed): a Brown Act cure-and-correct demand under §54960.1 (U7, not here).

**What advocates do:**
- watch the agenda as soon as it posts (Legistar), and read the staff report and attachments;
- write the comment to the time: about 130 spoken words a minute, so a two-minute limit is about 260 words;
- lead with the ask, give one or two facts with a source, and end with the ask;
- submit a longer written version to the Clerk or by eComment, so it is attached to the item;
- coordinate speakers so points are not repeated;
- arrange an interpreter in advance (the City provides on request with notice; verify the lead time);
- record the speaker's name, item number and time;
- after the meeting, check the video timestamp, the minutes and the attached written comments;
- if the minutes are wrong, ask the Clerk for a correction, or comment when the minutes come up for approval.

**Failure points:**
- missing the sign-up cut-off;
- reading more than the time allows;
- speaking on the wrong item;
- a written comment submitted after eComment closes;
- an interpreter not arranged;
- assuming the minutes record what was said;
- the group's position voiced as policy advocacy instead of asking the body to follow its own rule.

## U5 · Testimony before a state body, with written comments by a deadline

### 1. Situation

PG&E has applied to the **California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC)** to recover costs that include a **streetlight conversion program in Oakland**. In the City's own Measure U records (U1), the group found that the City paid part of the same work. The group's question is factual: are the same Oakland projects being charged to ratepayers and to bond funds? It has published that finding.

The proceeding has:
- a docket card (proceeding number as on the card; the profile's court-citation forms recognise CPUC numbers such as `A.YY-MM-NNN`);
- an assigned Administrative Law Judge;
- a **Public Participation Hearing** in Oakland;
- later, a **Proposed Decision**, which opens a comment period before the Commission votes at a **voting meeting**, a Bagley-Keene meeting.

The group wants to:
- speak at the Public Participation Hearing;
- file a written public comment;
- decide whether to become a **party** (so its comments on the Proposed Decision count formally);
- and then see whether the final decision addresses what it raised.

A variant uses a Bagley-Keene state board, such as the State Water Resources Control Board, holding a hearing with a written-comment deadline ("comments due by 12:00 noon on [date]"). The same steps apply without party status.

### 2. What real practitioners do

**Two tracks at the CPUC** (legal information; verify the rule numbers against the current Rules of Practice and Procedure, Cal. Code Regs. tit. 20):

1. **Public comment (no party status).**
   - Written comments go to the Public Advisor's Office (online form, email or mail) and are posted to the proceeding's docket card under "Public Comment".
   - Spoken comment is given at Public Participation Hearings, and at the voting meeting's public comment period (Bagley-Keene §11125.7; sign-up required, a minute or two each; verify).
   - Public comments inform the Commission but are not evidence in the record, and the decision need not respond to each one. That is the main limit.
2. **Party participation.**
   - Become a party by a protest or response to the application, by appearance at the prehearing conference, or by **motion for party status** (Rule 1.4; verify).
   - A party is served, may file testimony and briefs, may cross-examine at evidentiary hearings, and may file **comments on the Proposed Decision**: within **20 days** of service, replies within 5 days after (Rule 14.3); limited to factual, legal or technical errors, with page limits.
   - The Proposed Decision must be served at least **30 days** before the vote (Pub. Util. Code §311(d), (g)).
   - Party status brings obligations: **ex parte rules** (Rule 8.1–8.5; a reportable communication must be reported within three working days, Rule 8.4), service lists and deadlines.
   - **Intervenor compensation** (Pub. Util. Code §1801 et seq.) can reimburse eligible customer groups that substantially contribute. A Notice of Intent is due within 30 days of the prehearing conference (§1804(a)(1)). That is money the group receives, followed like any money (K1463).

**Bagley-Keene** (Gov. Code §11120 et seq.) governs state bodies' meetings:
- **§11125(a):** notice and agenda **10 days** before the meeting;
- **§11125.7(a):** an opportunity for public comment on each agenda item before or during the body's discussion, with reasonable time limits (§11125.7(b)); a translator rule like the Brown Act's (verify the subsection);
- **§11125.1:** writings distributed to the body are public.

**When a state agency adopts a regulation** (the APA, Gov. Code §11346 et seq.):
- a 45-day written comment period (§11346.4(a));
- a public hearing on request (§11346.8(a));
- the agency's **Final Statement of Reasons must summarise and respond to each objection or recommendation** (§11346.9(a)(3)).

That last point is the strongest "did they address what we said" tool a commenter has.

**What advocates do** (TURN, the Utility Reform Network; local groups; city attorneys):
- read the scoping memo to see which issues are in scope, and comment only on those;
- tie each point to the record (the exhibit and page);
- meet the deadline exactly (noon or 5 p.m. as stated; e-filing with the Docket Office for parties);
- keep the filing receipt;
- serve the service list;
- after the decision, search it for the issue (party comments are usually discussed; public comments are often summarised in one paragraph);
- consider an application for rehearing within 30 days (Pub. Util. Code §1731(b); parties only; verify) before any court review.

**Failure points:**
- commenting outside the scoping memo;
- missing the deadline by its time of day;
- mistaking a public comment for party comments;
- ex parte contacts not reported;
- not reading the Proposed Decision's revision;
- advocating a policy rather than supplying facts or asking that an existing requirement be applied.

## Cross-cutting: what U1–U5 have in common

1. **One engagement with many round trips.** Every case is a thread of dated sendings and replies: request → extension → production → denial → mediation → petition; claims → acknowledgement → reply → docket response; complaint → report number → status → outcome; comment → minutes; written comment → Proposed Decision → decision. The records-request lifecycle grammar (D-147: `stage`, `follows`, `outcome`, `due_by`/`due_cite`) is the right shape, but it is built for records requests and unreachable. **Need:** the staged ledger generalised by kind, with each kind's stages and outcomes taken from the profile (complaint statuses, the minutes, decisions), and one timeline view for both kinds of member.
2. **Venue facts as data.** Each case needs venue facts the profile does not hold:
   - how to submit;
   - limits (speaker time, pages);
   - time-of-day deadlines and deadlines counted from service;
   - what the office publishes;
   - anonymity;
   - what a submission becomes (public, confidential);
   - the ways to take part (public or party).

   The Oakland profile holds good deadline rules but almost none of these, and holds no PEC, City Clerk, department, CPUC or state-body entries. **Need:** a venue section in the jurisdiction profile, sourced (K1445), plus a state-level layer.
3. **Addressing a real office.** All five fail in the old interface at the addressee (R63 against the bare-name form). Copies and several addressees on one engagement are undesigned. **Need:** registry-linked addressees (Ladders ORG L2) and copies as a role.
4. **The breach gate at its edges.** Three cases (U1's enforcement of the group's own request, U3's oversight referral, U4's spoken comment) test rule 2 in ways the 30 September ruling did not consider. The same reading would answer all three: the claim may rest on what the record holds as dated facts, and a conclusion beyond those facts needs a determination. This is U1-Q1, U3-Q1 and U4-Q1; Bob may want them as one question.
5. **Information about the law, beside the event, never advice.** Each case needs a labelled reading of held text at the moment it matters (an extension's listed circumstances, the fee section, Brown Act §54954.3, Rule 14.3, APA §11346.9) and a list of routes in profile order (K1474 (ii)–(iii)). It never says "file", "unlawful" or a likely outcome. Ladders LAW L4's explain-a-rule reading is unbuilt.
6. **Following what the other side did.** Every case ends with "did they act on it?" (the production, the reply, the Auditor's report, the minutes, the decision), and none has a link from what following or monitoring captures to the action it answers, except `responds_to` for a direct reply. **Need:** "this document is the office's answer to / decision on our action", the group's and the body's lanes side by side (K1494), and outcomes recorded in the office's own terms.
7. **Templates and drafts for both kinds of member.** Each case needs a starting text. The wizard already names a template that does not exist. **Need:** a small Civicsmith library of approved Tier 1 templates per profile (U1-Q2), with assistant drafting as proposals (DEC-153) that never touch a reason field (K1841) and never draft before the member chooses what to send (K1474).
8. **One date, one reminder.** Steps, clock entries, duty occurrences and the group's own venue duties overlap. **Need:** one display rule so a fact is told once (DEC-70, DEC-94), with reminders only at the member's request, and the `.ics` download as the only bridge outside.
9. **Protecting people.** A whistleblower member (U3), an interpreter-assisted parent (U4) and an official replying (U2) each need the people fences applied at the action. **Need:** a hidden-project default for a member's own role, source protection, offices never persons, and quoting replies under a stated rule.
10. **Screens and doors.** Everything above sits on built-no-screen machinery. The fastest gains are the queue's per-kind doors, the correspondence stages, and the addressee arm.
