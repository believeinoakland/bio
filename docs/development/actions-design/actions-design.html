1 · Your direction

"we need to dive every bit as deeply into the requirements, current capabilities, additional capabilities needed, design, and use cases of Actions"

Bob, 9 October 2026, starting this lane

Your earlier rulings on actions still stand, and the design builds on them. Those from 29–30 September (the action plan) and later include:

  • The plan is the member's. The system suggests options. You first asked for "the best first 5", with a way to see the next five. On 9 October you widened that: "all action options should [be] listed, not just the top 5" (Actions D17). There is no catalogue of response options and no budgets. A plan holds up to three scenarios. An option left unticked is undecided, and declining one is an explicit act with a reason.
  • An action may start from success as well as a breach, and a plan may open as soon as an inquiry begins.
  • Never addressed to a private individual. The assistant drafts only proposals that a member adopts. Lobbying is allowed only to enforce or restore an existing requirement.
  • A breach claim needs a determination, unless a member openly overrides it with a reason. Evidence-seeking actions (records requests, requests for comment) are never held back.
  • The venue sets the standard of evidence. No action is refused for its evidence grade.
  • Reminders come only at the member's request, with no nagging and no outside channel. A missed date notifies once.
  • Due dates drive the plan. You said: "If the response isn't received by the due date, then [it may] cause other actions in an action plan to activate."
  • The litigation hold. You said: "we HAVE to [block] purging of all material named in the hold."
  • Filing templates go through a formal authoring, approval and attribution process. You asked for wording that is "optimally accurate, appropriate and compelling".
  • The investigation lane's rulings carry over. Both audiences must be served, members who work with the assistant and members who don't (D42). AI use is set per group, per project and per member account (D34–D39).

4 · The gaps

There are five kinds of gap. Each item says whose it is: yours (policy, doctrine, requirements, UX), BOB's (build order and record-keeping), UX (the design stream's screens), or this lane (to design and bring to you).

4.1 · Reach: built, but a member can't use it

No screens for most of the Action layer.

There are no screens for plans, determinations, consequences, filings, templates, escalation, reminders, holds or following. On an action itself, the old interface offers only four acts.

UX, then BOB's build order (next.md N821)

To-dos you can see but not answer.

The queue shows action to-dos (an overdue clock, a plan checkpoint, a proposed escalation stage) but offers only "Forward" and "Mark resolved". The system then refuses "Mark resolved" on these items. The reply each to-do needs is built but is never drawn on screen.

UX; BOB

Nothing proposes the next act on an action.

When a member looks at an action, nothing offers filing, escalation, the plan or a reminder, so the member has to know these exist.

this lane (behaviour), then UX

4.2 · Capabilities canon names that are not built

Jurisdiction facts the use cases depend on.

The records request that works under any jurisdiction's law (Case Making D-149) is built. Oakland's profile defines it under both the Public Records Act and the Sunshine Ordinance. The profile still lacks much of what other actions depend on:

  • the Brown Act demand and its windows;
  • the deadlines for answering a grand jury report;
  • the City Clerk, the Public Ethics Commission and state bodies;
  • speaker time limits;
  • any approved filing template. The "Get a record" wizard names a template that doesn't exist.
Deadlines computed from the law.

Counting days, rolling past weekends and holidays, and citing the rule are only partly built and can't be reached. (The capability ladders also record "overdue" as being marked on the UTC day, 7–8 hours early in California. A later fix (K1657) moved actions to the office's local day, so the ladder's note is probably stale. BOB will re-check it.)

Addressing a real office.

An action's addressee is free text, not linked to the registry of bodies and offices. Escalation can't follow real oversight lines ("who can make the City Administrator act?"). The old interface's "add" flow names a person, which conflicts with your rule that actions are never addressed to a private individual.

Following what happens after filing.

A group can't follow a petition's hearing date, a referral to the grand jury, or a court order as a proceeding linked to its action.

"Tell me if the Clerk misses this date."

Standing questions about a body's deadlines aren't built.

The "what we did" timeline.

The group's own acts, shown beside what happened in the world, aren't built.

The litigation hold on the member's device.

The device half is unbuilt and waits for a device-storage module.

Help drafting messages.

The assistant's help drafting messages and translations isn't built.

BOB's to schedule; this lane folds each into the design where a use case needs it

4.3 · Things never designed

These are for this lane, and the use cases will test each one.

Only one shape of group action.

What exists serves a group acting against a government office. Canon and your rulings also name:

  • awareness work, journalism and grassroots campaigns;
  • public comment and testimony at meetings;
  • recognising success;
  • negotiation and settlement;
  • coalitions with other groups (joint action, which you deferred);
  • "working on" notices;
  • help for a group that becomes a target (a SLAPP threat, a subpoena).

Most of these stop at "prepare" today. Nothing designs what happens after the message is sent.

Did it work?

An action records how it ended (complied, denied, escalated, withdrawn, completed), but nothing records what came of the plan as a whole. It is not defined what "consequences addressed" means, or whether the government actually fixed what was found. Consequences exist only for breaches, so the effect of a success is never recorded.

Support at every stage, for both kinds of member.

The assistant today suggests options and drafts communications. Your D43 asks for support "throughout", so this lane must find where help belongs: choosing whom to address, preparing a filing, getting ready for a meeting, reading a reply, deciding whether to escalate. It must also find where help must stop: legal information is allowed, legal advice is not, and the machine never says "what to file".

The question you deferred.

"If this finding holds, what could we do, and what else would have to be true?" covers standing, filing windows and exhausting remedies (D-165). Canon lays out how it would be shown but holds it back.

No capability ladder for actions.

The capability ladders cover every other construct. Actions, filings, escalation and plans have none, so there is no agreed path from what exists today to the fullest version.

A records request started from an investigation.

A records request started from an investigation step, and the step's wait set beside the action's clock, need one design so a member doesn't see two reminders for one date.

4.4 · Defects in the record

These are BOB's to correct. The list goes to BOB in the hand-off, not to you.

  • Records-request rules without a home. The rules for records requests are built: one round trip, the outcomes of a reply, and a due date that must cite its law. You ruled them in Case Making, whose action sections the Action canon then made "history". They now have no clear home in canon.
  • Stale texts on escalation and outcomes. Some older canon still says escalation "activates" on its own. Some still lists six stages, or the old seven state-specific action kinds. Some still says overdue is "marked" rather than worked out when read. Publication's quote matching still uses the old free-text counterparty.
  • Stale status lines and an unrecorded start. Two requirements files have stale status lines. No ruling records this lane's start or your 9 October words. N817 still reads "Not today".
  • Older rulings never marked as replaced. One example is the first litigation-hold release.

4.5 · Contradictions the design must resolve

The research found 18 across the core canon and 15 more across the rest. Most are wording, and this lane or BOB settles them as detail (section 5). Those that touch doctrine will come to you with the use cases. Three examples:

  • The shape of a plan. Case Making's plan is a decision tree of steps. The Action canon's plan is options laid out in up to three scenarios.
  • Resources. Case Making gives each step a "resources" list (money, hours, expertise). You ruled "no budgets", and canon forbids measuring members.
  • Three meanings of "action". Canon uses the word for any step toward an objective, for one outward engagement, and for a pursued course with a clock.

5 · Readings settled here

These follow from canon and your rulings, so they need no question to you. They are recorded for BOB to confirm, and they are named here so you can see them.

What "activate" means.

Your 5 October words say a missed due date may cause later steps of a plan to "activate". This is read as: the phase that waits on that date opens, and its options are offered to the member as ready to start. A member still starts each one. This is how the built plan already works, and it keeps your rule that the member chooses.

The pre-assembled escalation reason is not writing help.

You agreed that writing help is never offered in a field where a member states the reason for an act. Escalation's pre-assembled reason is a list of record facts with their sources (the standard breached, dates passed without reply, consequences recorded), labelled as machine work. Read that way, it is a summary of the record, not words written for the member, so both rulings hold.

Addressee and the person who owes the duty are two things.

A law may bind a named person, such as a lobbyist's registration or an official's financial statement. An action about that person's missed duty is addressed to the office that enforces it, never to the person.

Case Making's plan sections are history.

Where Case Making and the Action canon differ (the decision tree, resources, the gate on unestablished steps), the Action canon governs, as you approved. Case Making stays as the reasoning behind it.

7 · Decisions for you

The use cases raised 46 questions. Many overlapped, so they are merged here into sixteen. D1–D4 unblock the most design: between them they touch fourteen of the twenty use cases. D5–D8 shape how actions end and what members are given. D9–D16 are narrower. Each has a recommendation, and every recommendation was checked against your rulings. Where a worker's first recommendation conflicted with one of them, the conflict is noted and the recommendation corrected.

You can answer briefly, for example "D1 c, D2 c, D3 as recommended". Questions you'd rather take later can wait; the design proceeds on the others.

D1 · Asking or asserting: which outward acts your breach rule holds back

Background. You ruled on 30 September that an action claiming a breach is held back until the group has published a finding and a member has determined the breach. A member may go ahead anyway by an open act with a reason, and that disclosure then goes on everything prepared from it. Records requests and requests for comment are never held back. The rule doesn't say where three common acts fall:

  • A complaint to an oversight office (the Auditor's hotline, the grand jury) that asks the office to look, often before the group has published anything. These offices often ask complainants not to go public first.
  • Enforcing the group's own records request, through mediation or a court petition. Here the only "breach" is what the group's own dated letters show.
  • A joint letter or public comment that repeats the group's case.

Today a single switch the member sets decides it. If she sets it, a confidential complaint goes out with "rests on an unestablished premise" at its head. If she doesn't, nothing checks the text.

Options.

  • (a) Every such act is a breach claim. The Auditor complaint carries the override disclosure. To ask for mediation, the group must first publish "the City withheld the emails" as a case.
  • (b) All of them are evidence-seeking and never held back, whatever they say.
  • (c) Draw the line by what the text does. Asking an office to examine something, or enforcing the group's own request from its own dated record, is never held back. A sentence that asserts a breach as the group's finding still needs a finding and a determination, or your open override. Before approval, sentences that read as assertions are pointed out. The member either rewrites each one, or confirms in her own recorded words that the act only asks, or treats the act as a breach claim.
    For example, this goes out freely: "five purchase orders, consecutive numbers, same vendor, each just below the bid threshold; we ask the Auditor to examine them." But "the City routinely hides records" would be pointed out.

Trade-offs. (a) makes the most ordinary first moves of real groups slow and public, and turns your override into a routine click. (b) lets a group file accusations under the label "please examine". (c) is how auditors and inspectors want complaints written, and it keeps your standard exactly where the group makes its own claim. It adds one thing for the member to read before approving.

Recommendation: (c). One rule answers all four use cases, and it works for a member without the assistant: the pointing-out can come from a fixed list of phrasings as well as from AI. Checked: a worker proposed that flagged sentences be "flagged, never blocked". That would let assertions through with neither a determination nor your override, so the member must resolve each one, and her confirmation is recorded.

D2 · Checking what the group sends against the record

Background. You ruled that a published case's account is checked sentence by sentence. A sentence the record doesn't support is pointed out; one the record contradicts is refused ("lying is not bias"). Nothing the group sends is checked: letters, complaints, flyers, testimony. Yet that is where a group is most exposed to a defamation claim, especially before anything is published.

Options.

  • (a) No check. The sender is responsible.
  • (b) Point out only. Nothing is refused.
  • (c) The case check, with your open-override pattern. Unsupported sentences are pointed out. A sentence the record contradicts is held back by default, and the member may still send it only by an open act with a reason. Questions are never pointed out, because asking is not asserting. Claims about what a body did rest on the published case or on the group's own dated letters. Statements about what the group itself is doing need neither.
    For example, each of a flyer's three facts links to a finding. "They lost track of $X" is pointed out, because the finding says "reports not filed for two years".

Trade-offs. (a) leaves the group exposed where it is most at risk. (b) lets a sentence the group's own record contradicts go out under its name. (c) applies the standard you already set for cases to everything that carries the group's name, and the member still decides.

Recommendation: (c). Checked: a worker proposed refusing contradicted sentences outright. Because a tool must never have the final word on an action, the recommendation uses your hold-by-default, override-openly pattern instead. A member without the assistant still gets the check against the record: each claim is linked to its finding, and a claim with no link is pointed out.

D3 · When the law gives fewer days than it takes to publish

Background. A Brown Act demand to cure and correct must be sent within 30 days of the improper action. Under your rule, a demand that claims a breach needs a published finding and a determination, or the open override. Today the override's disclosure is printed first on the letter the City reads. Your 30 September ruling also says an overridden action can never join escalation, even after the group later makes the determination. Nothing shows the member how many days remain.

Options.

  • (a) Keep the override as the only path, and show the window beside the refusal. For example: "Due 5 Nov (30 days, Gov. Code §54960.1). Publish and determine first, or proceed with a stated reason, disclosed on the letter."
  • (b) As (a), plus a quick route. Civicsmith helps the group publish a short one-finding case (agenda, video, minutes) and determine it within days. No publication check is waived. For example: published on day 4, determined on day 5, demand sent on day 6, no override.
  • (c) Keep the disclosure on the group's own copy only, not on the letter.
  • (d) Let an action sent under override join escalation once a determination is later made, with the override kept in its history.

Trade-offs. (a) is honest, but it forces a choice between a weakened letter and a missed window. (b) keeps your standard and fits the work, since these cases are usually small and documentary. (c) breaks your rule that a departure is visible to every reader, and the City is the reader that matters. (d) removes a trap, but it changes part of your 30 September ruling.

Recommendation: (b) together with (d). Not (c).

D4 · What stays inside the group

Background. You ruled that nothing non-public leaves by a system path and that a plan is never published. But the group's list of "what we did" includes every letter on every action, and it can be frozen into a published case's timeline. So today a confidential grand jury complaint, or a lawyer's advice recorded on an action, could reach a published timeline. Counsel's advice is also visible to every member who can see the action, which can waive privilege. Nothing says what the record may hold about a member who is herself the whistleblower. Promises the group made to people (an embargo time, a source's anonymity) have no place to be recorded, so a newcomer can break one without knowing.

Options.

  • (a) Three scopes, each set by a member's act with a reason.
    • A confidential act never appears in anything published, but is seen inside the group like any action. For example, the case timeline shows the letters to the Director but not the grand jury complaint.
    • A counsel matter is its own project, which members join. Only its members see counsel's correspondence and dates, it is never published or used as evidence, and its packets carry a privilege marking.
    • A member's own role as a source stays in her hidden project. The group's complaint rests on the documents she chooses to share.
    Promises made to people are recorded as notes on the action, which every participant sees.
  • (b) Redact instead of excluding. The timeline shows "12 Oct · confidential referral", which still reveals that a referral exists.
  • (c) No special rules. Members leave things out by hand and keep counsel outside Civicsmith.

Trade-offs. (a) costs three small marks and closes the one leak path that four use cases share. (b) is weaker for confidential referrals. (c) relies on memory, against a timeline the system assembles automatically.

Recommendation: (a). Checked: a worker proposed "members the group names" for counsel matters. That would filter sight by a member attribute, which your rules forbid. Making the counsel matter a project its members join uses the same mechanism hidden projects already use.

D5 · When a breach is over

Background. Your escalation ends when compliance is restored and the consequences are addressed. As built, "restored" requires a compliant determination of the same act. But most breaches are past acts (a transfer in 2025) cured by a later act (a restoring transfer in 2028), so most escalations can never end; they stay "paused". Nothing says what "addressed" means when harm can't be undone, such as a year in portable classrooms. And because a determination rests on a published finding, recording that the City fixed it means publishing again.

Options.

  • Restoring compliance.
    • (a) Keep "the same act".
    • (b) A curing act counts. A later act of the same office, determined compliant against the same standard and linked by a member as the cure. For example: "Resolution 2028-031 restoring $X".
    • (c) A member closes the escalation with a reason, without any determination.
  • Harm addressed.
    • (a) A member's reasoned statement with evidence for each part, with optional plain words offered: remedied, mitigated, compensated, acknowledged, cannot be undone. For example: "Mitigated: monthly reporting adopted; the lost year cannot be restored."
    • (b) A fixed list only.
    • (c) End on compliance alone.
  • Publishing again.
    • (a) Always.
    • (b) A dated outcome entry on the published case at once, and the determination with the next edition.
    • (c) Allow a compliant determination on unpublished findings.

Trade-offs. Keeping "the same act" makes your "until compliance is restored" unreachable for most breaches. Closing without a determination lowers your rule that compliance is recorded as carefully as noncompliance. A fixed list forces a label where judgement is needed. Allowing a determination on unpublished findings breaks the layer's contract.

Recommendation: the curing act (b); a reasoned statement with optional words (a); an outcome entry first, then the determination with the next edition (b). None of these is a score.

D6 · How a plan and a pursuit end, and how watching stops

Background. You ruled that compliance counts and that success stories are actions. Today a plan closes with a reason only. An escalation can be paused, but not honestly stopped without winning. A success's effect has nowhere to go, because consequences exist only for breaches. And what a group set running (following a body, watching a page, a promised date) runs until someone remembers it.

Options and the recommendation for each:

  • Stopping without winning. Recommended: "stopped pursuing", with a reason, a state never read as success and open to reopening. For example: "Stopped March 2027: the oversight committee took the matter over." The alternatives are pause only, or close the plan and leave the escalation paused.
  • What a closed plan records. Recommended: the reason, plus a closing summary computed from the record for each matter. It covers determinations, whether compliance was restored, consequences addressed, how each action and escalation ended, promises kept, and what is still watched. It is never published, and the member may add her own note. Rejected: letting the member rate each matter "achieved / partly / not", because that is the significance score your rules keep out.
  • A success's effect. Recommended: a success record for each matter, the member's statement on evidence, with optional figures from cited sources. It never claims the group caused the fix unless evidence beyond the group's own act shows it. The alternatives are the action's ending only, or widening consequences to include benefits.
  • Stopping watching. Recommended: at plan close, the plan offers once to keep each watch until a date the member picks, or to stop it, and every watch shows its end date. Rejected: an automatic sunset, which would be the machine deciding; and silent watching, which hides what is still running.

D7 · When an official's spoken promise binds the body

Background. Standards include an office's public commitments, and a later breach claim can rest on one. A deputy director saying "the remaining streets will be done by June" in a private meeting is narrative until it is in the record. The investigation design already draws the line between an account and evidence.

Options.

  • (a) Only once the body confirms it in writing or states it publicly. Until then it is kept "as heard" in each member's own account. The group's confirming letter asks the office to correct the summary, and silence is recorded as a dated fact. For example, the Director's reply confirms "by June", and a member adopts it as a duty to watch.
  • (b) The group's confirming letter suffices if the office doesn't correct it within 14 days.
  • (c) Any member's account may serve, labelled as testimony.

Trade-offs. (b) is how lawyers write, but silence is not agreement, and a breach claim could then rest on a sentence nobody can check. (c) invites weak claims.

Recommendation: (a), with the office's silence recorded as a dated fact, which may be published as such: "we wrote on 3 October asking the Department to correct our summary; no reply by 17 October".

D8 · Starting texts, procedure guides and legal facts for every member

Background. You set a formal authoring, approval and attribution process for filing templates. You ruled legal information yes, legal advice no, and never "what to file". You also ruled that both audiences must be optimally served. Today no template exists; the approved "Get a record" path names one that was never written. So a member without the assistant writes every request, demand and comment from a blank page. Oakland's is the only real profile, and elsewhere every date reads "undetermined".

Options and the recommendation for each:

  • Templates. Recommended: Civicsmith ships a small Tier 1 set for each profile, under your process: written by a named person from public sources, reviewed by a professional, approved and attributed. The set is a records request, a follow-up when a date passes, a reconsideration letter, a mediation request, written public comment, a grand jury complaint and a Brown Act demand. A template is offered only after the member has chosen the act, so it never says what to file. The alternatives are groups writing their own over time (which fails your D42), or court-petition templates as well (better left until your deferred licensed certification).
  • Guides. Recommended: a sourced guide for each kind of action and each profile, labelled "legal information, not legal advice". It says what replies to expect and what each route is, in the profile's order and unranked. Members read it, and the assistant reads it as its source.
  • Facts at the moment they matter. Recommended: shown with citations only when relevant. Examples: the lobbyist threshold when a paid attendee joins a meeting; the all-party consent law before recording; the anti-SLAPP window after a threat. Whether a protection actually applies waits for the question you deferred ("if this finding holds, what could we do").
  • Other states. Recommended: support a state when a group there asks, and extend the profile's design now so the first group outside California isn't blocked. Rejected: a generic "any state" fallback, which breaks "a missing fact reads undetermined, never a default".
  • Who writes a jurisdiction's rules. Recommended: a member drafts a rule with its primary source, a second member confirms it (a professional for Tier 2 and 3 matters), and it applies in that group at once. It may then be offered to the shared profile.

D9 · Briefing a lawyer before anything is determined, and for the group's defence

Background. Civicsmith assembles a counsel packet: facts, chronology, exhibits with their grades, standards, candidate theories and deadlines. It is never published and never fileable. As built, it needs a determination or the open override. But lawyers want to be consulted early, and a group that is threatened or sued needs the same packet for its defence, where no determination applies. Today a defence packet would be labelled "a breach claim on an unestablished premise".

Options. (a) Keep it as built. (b) A briefing at any stage without the override, showing each matter's support ("1 suspected, hypothetical"), plus a defence purpose. A defence packet holds each challenged statement with its finding and sources, the pressure received, and the defence deadlines with citations. (c) Early briefings for Tier 3 only, and no defence packet.

Recommendation: (b). Your breach rule sits at the outward act. A packet goes only to the group's own lawyer by a member's hand and asserts nothing outward, so the rule does not apply. The packet never says "you have a claim".

D10 · The litigation hold: who places it, and what it reaches

Background. You ruled that nothing named in a hold may be purged. Today a hold can be placed only from a threatening letter on an action. A letter about the published case raises none, and a group planning to sue has no way to place one. A real hold also reaches members' own email, texts and chats, which Civicsmith cannot and must not watch.

Options.

  • Who may place it. (a) Any member who can see the action or case, with a reason such as "we anticipate litigation". (b) Only from a counsel matter. (c) No change.
  • What it reaches.
    • (a) A hold notice to members, prepared when the hold is placed and sent by a member's own hand. It lists in plain words what to keep: email, texts, chats with disappearing messages turned off, notes, photos. There is no tracking of who read or complied.
    • (b) As (a), and each member records "I have read it".
    • (c) Civicsmith's own material only.

Recommendation: (a) and (a). Option (b) on reach is rejected, because a record per member is the measuring of members your rules exclude.

D11 · Where the no-policy line falls: testimony and elections

Background. Your rule: Civicsmith takes no position on what policy should be. Groups may testify, and may support legislation that restores or enforces an existing requirement. Policy advocacy and candidate support are not actions. Two places test this:

  • Testimony at the utilities commission almost always touches policy, though the group's finding is factual.
  • Near an election, a group telling voters what the record shows about the last bond can become a campaign committee under state law if it spends money urging a vote.

Options.

  • Testimony.
    • (a) Only enforcement testimony that rests on a determination.
    • (b) Factual testimony on any matter, plus asking a body to examine or apply an existing requirement, without asking for a policy outcome. For example: "The City paid $X for these 14 projects (exhibits A–D); we ask the Commission to examine whether the same projects are in the utility's request."
    • (c) Any position, disclosed as the group's own view.
  • Elections.
    • (A) Inform, and fence the assistant. When a message names a pending measure or candidate, a short notice says Civicsmith takes no position on how to vote and that spending on vote-urging material may be campaign activity (with the citation). The assistant never drafts vote advice. The approving member states that the message urges no vote; if she says it does, it is not recorded or stamped as the group's action.
    • (B) Refuse any message containing vote words.
    • (C) Nothing special.

Recommendation: testimony (b); elections (A). Checked: a worker proposed that nothing be refused. Your rule says vote advocacy is not an action, so Civicsmith should not stamp one as the group's. The member's own statement keeps this her decision rather than a word filter.

D12 · Who speaks and who is named

Background. An action commits the group. But a member speaking at a council meeting is also a resident exercising her own right, often at two minutes' notice. Actions are addressed to roles, never private individuals, yet a tip goes to a named journalist the group needs to remember. And thanking a department may mean naming the staff who did the work.

Options and the recommendation for each:

  • Public comment: the member chooses when preparing. "For the group" means approved, stamped, and held to your rules. "As myself" means kept in her own notes, with no group stamp. The alternatives are always the group's act, which is too heavy for live speech, or always her own, which lets the group's name be used without its standard.
  • The reporter: addressed by role and outlet, with the journalist's name kept inside the group as "who holds the role", the same way an office shows its holder. It is never published by the system.
  • Terms with the press: a terms note on each press action, for example "on the record; embargo 22 Oct 06:00". Rejected: a named-spokesperson setting, which would be a member attribute deciding who may act.
  • Thanks: the text may name staff acting in their official roles, with a reminder that public thanks names them publicly. Requiring each person's consent would treat public employees as private individuals.

D13 · The other side's words

Background. Canon says third parties' words leave Civicsmith only by their own act. Newsrooms and auditors routinely publish an office's reply to a request for comment, because fairness requires it. Separately, your right-of-reply rule is a declaration, not a gate, so a group could sign its case on day 9 of a 10-day window without noticing.

Options.

  • Quoting a reply. (a) Only with the office's express consent. (b) On the record unless the reply says otherwise, with that rule stated in the request itself, so the office answers knowing it. (c) Always attached in full.
  • At signing. (a) Nothing. (b) A plain fact line for each open request. For example: "You asked the Director of Transportation on 15 Oct; the window closes 25 Oct; the reply received 23 Oct answers 2 of 4 claims." (c) As (b), and signing while a window is open asks for a reason.

Recommendation: (b) and (b). The same pattern, a fact comparison shown once and never a judgement, also serves the minutes beside the group's comment.

D14 · When a member leaves

Background. Reminders belong to each member. To-dos about an action or plan go to whoever created it, so when she leaves they go to her or to no one clearly. You ruled out assignees in a plan. A law firm never lets a deadline live in one person's calendar.

Options.

  • Her dates. (a) Nothing changes. (b) The owners are told once which dated items went to her, and any member may "take up" an item by one recorded act. Taking up changes where its to-dos go, and nobody assigns anybody. (c) Automatic reassignment to the owners.
  • Learning where things stand. (a) The plan page and history. (b) A computed "where it stands" page: waits with their basis, next dates, holds, overrides, and declined options with their reasons. (c) As (b), plus plan status reports drafted from the record and edited by a member, never required and never published.

Recommendation: (b) and (c). Taking up is a member choosing for herself where her own to-dos arrive, so it isn't an assignee.

D15 · Becoming a formal party before a venue

Background. At the utilities commission, public comments are read but are not evidence; a party's comments are formally considered. Party status brings legal duties: service, deadlines, and reporting contacts with decision-makers within three working days. It sometimes brings compensation from the utility. Records laws in other states also put deadlines on the requester.

Options. (a) Public comment only; party status stays outside Civicsmith. (b) Support party participation as a Tier 2 action with its advisory and the counsel packet offered. The group's own duties are held as "our deadlines", with reminders only as asked. (c) As (b), plus intervenor compensation claims.

Recommendation: (b) now, (c) when a group asks. This is the area you work in. Your judgement on how groups really take part in commission proceedings would sharpen this one.

D16 · Coalitions: lift part of your deferral now?

Background. On 30 September you deferred joint action with another group until a coalition asks. The use cases show that sign-on letters and joint testimony are ordinary. The first level needs no machinery between groups: a partners field, one text approved in each group's copy with a matching fingerprint, and each signatory added by its own organisation's act. But no group can ask while it has no screen to ask through.

Options. (a) Design and build the first level with the first action screens. (b) Record partners now, and design joint approval now to build with the screens. (c) Keep everything deferred.

A second question. May a group make a breach claim about a body using another group's findings it has accepted? Recommended: those findings may support the group's own, but its determination rests on its own published findings about a body it examined.

Recommendation: (b).

D18 · When may a member claim a breach without a determination?

Background. You asked this on the page. Your 30 September rule lets a member override openly, with a reason, and the disclosure goes on everything sent. The legitimate cases are nearly all about time: the facts are in the record, but publishing and determining can't finish before the moment passes.

  • A legal deadline shorter than the time to publish. A Brown Act demand within 30 days; a CEQA challenge within 30–35 days; a government claim for damages within 6 months.
  • An irreversible act about to happen. A vote tonight on a contract awarded without the required bid, or a demolition next week.
  • Counsel advising a demand to preserve rights before the group's case is ready.

Two other cases that look similar don't need a breach claim at all, if you accept D1 (asking an office to examine, or enforcing the group's own records request) and D3 (a quick route to a small published case).

Options.

  • (a) Keep the override as it is: any reason the member writes.
  • (b) Narrow it to stated grounds. The member names a legal deadline (cited, with its date) or an imminent act (what, and when), plus her reason. Both are shown on the letter. For example: "Sent before determination: the 30-day window under Gov. Code §54960.1 closes 5 Nov; the group's case is in preparation."
  • (c) Remove the override. The group then acts outside Civicsmith, and the record holds nothing.

Trade-offs. (a) can become a routine way past your standard. (b) keeps your choice, since the member decides and every reader sees why the group could not wait, but a real case outside the two grounds would have to wait or act outside Civicsmith. (c) is the worst outcome for the record: the group still acts, with no trace and no disclosure.

Recommendation: (b). If a real case outside the two grounds turns up, the grounds can be widened.

D19 · How many scenarios a plan may hold

Background. You asked why there are at most three. It is your own ruling of 29 September ("a plan holds up to three scenarios, alternative ways of laying out the chosen options, to compare"), and no reason is recorded. It seems to have served comparison. Since then, each scenario can branch inside itself, on a checkpoint the member judges or on a body's deadline being met or missed. So many contingencies that once needed their own scenario are now branches within one. You have also widened suggested options to all (D17).

Options.

  • (a) Keep at most three.
  • (b) No fixed limit. A scenario no longer in use is set aside with a short reason and kept, as a declined option is. The screen compares any two or three side by side. For example, the group keeps "negotiate first", "go to the grand jury" and "litigate with the coalition", and sets aside "media first" with the reason "the reporter left".
  • (c) A higher fixed number, such as five.

Trade-offs. (a) is simple, but it forces the group to delete an alternative it may want later. (b) keeps the history of the roads not taken and leaves comparison to the screen, but a long list needs the set-aside state to stay readable. (c) only moves the line.

Recommendation: (b).
