## Ground it in the real world
The product must work for real civic watchdog groups, against real governments, with real data. Before proposing anything, research with WebSearch and WebFetch (several searches, sent together):
- **How the work is actually done:** how investigative journalists, watchdog groups, auditors, inspectors general, ethics commissions, legal-aid and civic-tech groups do this in practice; the methods and the failure modes.
- **Established standards and data models** to adopt or adapt rather than invent; say which and why. Starting points: PEOPLE: Popolo (person, membership, post), FollowTheMoney (OCCRP Aleph's model: Person, Ownership, Directorship, Membership, Family, Associate), Wikidata, OpenSanctions and its entity resolution, record linkage practice (Fellegi–Sunter; Splink), statements of economic interests (California FPPC Form 700 and other states), lobbying registers, professional licensing look-ups, campaign-finance filings (FEC, state systems), and the legal limits on holding personal data (privacy law and its journalism exemptions, defamation, safety). EVENTS: event models (CIDOC CRM, schema.org Event, W3C PROV, FollowTheMoney's Event and Interval), EDTF, investigative timeline practice (Aleph, Hunchly, timeline tools), legislative event models (Legistar matter history, Akoma Ntoso lifecycle), process mining, and how causation claims are handled in audit and journalism. MONEY: governmental fund accounting (GASB 34 and 54 fund types, the ACFR's schedules), the budget cycle and its stages (adopted, amended, appropriated, encumbered, expended), USAspending and the DATA Act (obligations and outlays), OpenSpending and the Fiscal Data Package, OCDS (planning, tender, award, contract, implementation), open checkbooks and ledger exports, performance and unit-cost measurement (GFOA, GASB service efforts and accomplishments), campaign finance, follow-the-money method (OCCRP, GIJN), and public-finance rules (fee limits such as California Proposition 218, restricted funds, procurement thresholds).
- **Real availability and cost:** which sources a group can actually get, in which formats, at what cost, with what legal limits. Check them; don't assume.
- **The runtime:** Cloudflare Workers and Durable Objects (per-request limits, one SQLite per object of at most 10 GB, one scheduler alarm, no cron). A proposal that won't fit must say how it would run.
Cite each external fact with its URL.

## Write `studies/<CONSTRUCT>.md`, in exactly these sections
1. **Anticipated needs.** The concrete work, from the digest (cite the note's source and §) and from the real-world research (cite the URL). Group it; give each item a one-line example in a member's words, and mark how central it is: core, regular or occasional.
2. **Levels of support.** A ladder, L0 to L5, specific to this construct, each level defined by what a member can do. Map each need from §1 to the lowest level that serves it.
3. **What exists now.** Per module: what it provides (R ids), whether it is built, reachable by a member (op and screen) and touched by an AI; its layer. Place the system on the ladder, separately for "built" and "a member can actually use it today".
4. **Gaps.** Each need → the missing capability, with severity: blocks core work, degrades it, or nice to have.
5. **Proposed architecture.** Why L5 and how it is staged; what to adopt from the real world; the data model (objects, fields, relations, versions and as-of); module changes (extend X; a new module Y with purpose, layer and uses); how it fits the first study's architecture (`lines`, `duties`, `chronology`, `calculations`, `standards`, `answers` and the rest), what that architecture must change for it, and any change to the total order (check `build/modules.json`); the AI's role (what the machine proposes, what the member decides); the doctrine kept, each rule cited, and any rule the construct strains; what it needs at runtime and in deployment.

7. **Interfaces with the other constructs** (all eight others), specifically what yours needs from each and supplies to each.
