## 0 · Who brings an investigation, and what the mission makes of it

**Who the members are.** The mission is written for ordinary residents acting together, not for professionals: "citizens" whose apathy is "rational learned helplessness" and who need "a credible path to results" (`BIO_Complete_Roadmap_v5.md` §2). The unit of work is the group, from "one person to a federation of groups, same framework" (§6, DR2), groups that "form and dissolve freely" (DR3); some groups are professional ("legal, accounting, technical", with credentials and engagement basis, §12 "Key UX design principles"). Bob's scenario is the typical member: a parent with a grievance, a few remembered facts, a promise, and no method (`RESUME.md`).

**What the mission makes of a grievance.** BIO "hold[s] government to the law and its stated policies" and "take[s] no position on what the policies should be" (§5, OP1). So "what's going on?" has to be turned into a comparison: *what was required or promised* (a statute, a contract term, a board-adopted schedule, a bond's project list, a stated policy) against *what was done*. That is exactly Skill 6, Government Compliance Analysis: "what the standard requires, what the city did, where they align, where they diverge, and what additional information would resolve ambiguous cases ... for human evaluation rather than rendering a determination" (§9). A parent's "we were promised one school year" is a stated-policy claim whose source must be found before it can be held against anyone; if the promise was only campaign talk, the investigation's honest answer may be that the binding standard was different. *(inference)*

**The workflow the mission already names.** Orient → Investigate → Document → Escalate → Monitor, with "new evidence triggers re-investigation" (§10), served by eight skills: Context/Landscape, Data Archive (baseline, monitoring, change detection "preserving both versions as evidence"), Data Extraction, Legal/Policy Lookup, Monitoring/Watchdog, Government Compliance Analysis, Compliance Evaluation, Escalation Protocol (§9). The UX already has Search as "persistent requests with lifecycle" whose accepted results carry provenance into Context, and Monitoring that "feeds New Developments" (§12). Bob's request adds what the roadmap does not name: an **engine** that plans toward an objective, **reads each document knowing why it is in the file**, and keeps an **investigation file** over weeks, proposing next steps a member accepts (`RESUME.md`). The scenarios below test that addition. (Note: the roadmap's Focus/Project vocabulary is superseded by the INQUIRY, DEC-72, per the header of §§9–10; and "case" means the signed, published case, so this note says *investigation file* for Bob's "case file", `RESUME.md`.)

**The origin case is itself the template.** The sewer-fund work (§1) shows every element at once: an auditor's finding with a deadline (October 2023) and zero of four recommendations implemented; money continuing under a possibly new label ("relabeled as cost allocations or overhead charges"); a CPRA request (26-3028) with a named custodian, a statutory clock (Gov. Code 7922.535), silence, a follow-up deadline, and three escalation paths (CPRA petition, grand jury, State Controller). It appears as S2 below.

### S1 · Grandview Elementary: the rebuild that will not end (schools; public works; procurement)

**Opening words** (Bob's, `RESUME.md`): "My kids' school, Grandview Elementary, is finally being rebuilt. The process of designing the new campus, putting the construction out for bid, choosing the winning bid, and construction starting took 3 years. We were promised that reconstruction would take no longer than 1 school year. But now it's been 2 years, and the project still isn't close to being done. What's going on?"

**Clarifying questions** (the few an investigator asks before digging; each changes the plan):
1. Which district, and is Grandview a full rebuild on the same site, or modernization? (Decides the document set: new construction vs modernization funding and DSA scope.)
2. Who made the "one school year" promise, when, and in what form: a board presentation, a bond campaign mailer, a principal's letter, a community meeting? Do you have it? (Turns a memory into a standard to test.)
3. Where are the children now: portables on site, another campus, split? (Interim housing has its own cost and contract; its lease end is a hidden deadline.)
4. How is it paid for: a local bond measure (which one, which year), state matching funds, both? (Decides which oversight body and audits exist.)
5. What do you see on site: crews working, idle, a fence and nothing? Any notices posted? (Distinguishes slow work from stopped work.)
6. Have you or other parents asked the district already, and what were you told? (The district's own explanation is the first competing hypothesis, and a refusal is a record.)

**Objectives.** (O1) Establish the binding schedule: what completion date the contract and board actually adopted, versus the promise. (O2) Establish actual progress and the current forecast. (O3) Explain the gap: which causes, attributable to whom, in which amounts of time and money. (O4) Determine whether any law or stated policy was broken (bidding, change-order limits, bond-fund use, Brown Act disclosure, CBOC duties). (O5) Identify what the member can do and what would change the outcome.

**Documents that would answer it** *(general knowledge unless cited)*:
| kind | publisher | where found | what matters in it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bond measure ballot text and project list | district / county elections | county voter guide; district bond page | Grandview named? scope promised; Prop 39 accountability terms |
| Facilities master plan; board item approving the project | district board | board portal (BoardDocs, Granicus, etc.) | scope, budget, any stated schedule ("one school year" may live here) |
| Architect selection RFQ/RFP and design contract | district | board agenda attachments | design fee, phases, design schedule |
| DSA application, approval, project tracker | Division of the State Architect | DSA online project tracker | approval date; inspector of record; change documents; closeout status. | Notice inviting bids / bid documents | district | district purchasing page; newspaper notice | contract time (calendar days), liquidated damages per day, phasing, alternates |

| Stop-payment notices, claims, lawsuits | subcontractors; courts | district; superior court docket | unpaid subs, contractor distress, disputes over delay |
| State funding applications and reports | Office of Public School Construction | OPSC project tracking | state share, expenditure reports |
| Contractor history elsewhere | other districts; courts; news | board minutes of other districts; case law | prior defaults, delay claims, terminations (e.g. a district declaring a contractor in default and a surety completing, [LAUSD/Lewis Jorge opinion](https://courts.ca.gov/opinions/revpub/B189133.PDF)) |

**Actors and relations.** District board (awards, ratifies), superintendent and facilities director (manage), program/construction manager (consultant, reports), architect of record (design, responds to RFIs), DSA and its project inspector (code approval, inspection), general contractor and its surety, subcontractors, CBOC (oversight), auditors, OPSC/State Allocation Board (state funds), parents, PTA, teachers' union (interim housing), board members and their campaign donors (construction firms commonly fund bond campaigns: DR6 ties, *general knowledge*). Relations that matter: who paid whom, who approved what, who reported what to whom and when.

**Time structure.** Three clocks run side by side: the *promise* (one school year, source unknown); the *contract* (contract time from notice to proceed, extended by approved change orders, with liquidated damages after); the *reported forecast* (each monthly report's completion date). The investigation's spine is a chronology: board approval → design contract → DSA submission → DSA approval → bid opening → award → NTP → each change order → each forecast slip → today. The question "when did the district first know?" is answered by the first report whose forecast moved. Hidden deadlines: interim-housing lease expiry; DSA closeout (DSA may open closeout within six months of occupancy and issues 90-day letters for missing documents, [DGS PL 09-04](https://www.dgs.ca.gov/-/media/Divisions/DSA/Publications/policies/PL_09-04.pdf), via search summary); bond series issuance dates.

**Money.** Budget at approval vs award amount vs current contract sum (award + change orders) vs spent to date; contingency drawn; liquidated damages assessed or waived; interim-housing cost per year of delay; program-management fees that grow with time. Change-order totals against the statutory limits on change orders without rebidding *(general knowledge: PCC 20118.4 limits school change orders; the exact limit to be checked when the study needs it)*.

**Competing explanations** (each with what would confirm it): unforeseen site conditions (hazardous materials, soils, utilities) → early COs coded "unforeseen", geotechnical reports; design errors or incomplete drawings → many RFIs, architect-caused COs, DSA change documents; DSA re-review of changes → DSA tracker dates; contractor capacity or financial distress → falling certified-payroll headcount, stop-payment notices, other districts' disputes; owner-caused delay (slow decisions, late payments, scope added) → owner-requested COs, board delays; funding gap → work slowed before a bond series sale; supply chain or labour market → industry-wide, other districts' similar slips; termination and surety takeover → board default resolution. Most real delays are several of these at once, and both sides' accounts are self-serving (contractor delay claims vs district "contractor performance" statements). *(general knowledge)*

**Dead ends and refusals.** Schedules, CM reports and correspondence are usually not posted and need a CPRA request: the agency must determine disclosability within 10 days, extendable by up to 14 for unusual circumstances, and the 10 days is a deadline to respond, not to produce ([Gov. Code 7922.535, FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/government-code/gov-sect-7922-535/)); open-ended "rolling production" is common ([First Amendment Coalition](https://firstamendmentcoalition.org/asked-and-answered/is-it-normal-to-receive-multiple-cpra-extensions-for-months/)). Expect claims of exemption for documents tied to pending claims or litigation, and for deliberative material. Board minutes record that a CO passed on consent, not why. CBOC reports may be thin. Scanned PDFs without text. The contractor is private and owes no answers.

**How it iterates.** Week 1: public record sweep (board items, bond page, DSA tracker, CSLB, DIR), first chronology with gaps marked. Weeks 1–2: CPRA request for the contract, schedules, CO log, CM reports; member speaks at a board meeting or writes the CBOC. Weeks 3–8: production arrives in batches; each batch re-plans (a CO coded "differing site condition" opens a soils line; a stop-payment notice opens a contractor-distress line). Months 2–6: monitoring each board agenda and monthly report for new slips; follow-up requests; collaboration with other parents. The investigation stays open until occupancy and DSA certification (OP6).

**What an answer looks like.** A dated chronology of promised vs contracted vs forecast completion; each slip attributed to documented causes with the evidence and its grade; money table; what remains UNDETERMINED and which record would settle it; any departure from law or stated policy (e.g. COs ratified late, CBOC not reviewing the audit, a schedule promise in an adopted board item not kept). **Action**: a published finding; a request to the CBOC to review; public comment; Tier 1 filings (CPRA follow-ups, grand jury complaint if warranted); monitors on the board agenda and DSA tracker until completion.

### S2 · The sewer fees that went to the general fund (budgets; utilities) — BIO's origin case

**Opening words.** "The auditor said the city moved $52 million of our sewer fees into the general fund. They promised to fix it by 2023. Did they? Are they still doing it?" (paraphrasing `BIO_Complete_Roadmap_v5.md` §1).
**Clarifying.** Which recommendations do you mean (the 2022 audit's four)? Is the concern restitution, the ongoing transfers, or the missed deadline? Do you pay the sewer fee (standing for a Prop 218 claim lies with ratepayers)?
**Objectives.** Did the transfers stop or change label; were the auditor's recommendations implemented; is any current transfer authorised and Prop 218-compliant; what was the response to CPRA 26-3028.
**Documents.** City Auditor report and follow-ups; ACFR fund statements (Sewer Service Fund, "Statement of Revenues, Expenses, and Changes in Fund Net Position"); adopted budgets and OpenGov transfer data; cost-allocation plan; council resolutions; Municipal Code; CPRA production (§1). Prop 218 limits fee revenue to the service and requires 45-day notice and a majority-protest hearing for new or increased fees ([sample Prop 218 notice, West Sacramento](https://www.cityofwestsacramento.org/government/departments/finance-department/water-and-sewer-rate-study/notice-of-public-hearing)).
**Actors.** Controller (named CPRA contact), Finance, City Auditor, City Attorney, Council, external auditor, ratepayers; State Controller; grand jury (§1).
**Time.** Audit Feb 2022; implementation deadline Oct 2023; CPRA filed 19 Mar 2026, statutory determination due 30 Mar, follow-up deadline 7 Apr (§1). Fiscal-year series FY2012–FY2027.
**Money.** $52.6M over nine years; $1.6–2.8M annually since, $2.77M budgeted FY2026-27 (§1); the question is what each dollar is now called.
**Competing explanations.** Legitimate cost allocation for services the general fund provides to the sewer utility (lawful if cost-justified); relabelled franchise fee (unlawful under Prop 218 if not cost-based); accounting reclassification without change in substance.
**Dead ends.** Total nonresponse to CPRA (§1); cost-allocation plans that are opaque spreadsheets; labels changing between budget years so a series breaks.
**Iteration.** Years, not weeks: each budget cycle and ACFR is a new data point; escalation through three parallel paths (§1).
**Answer and action.** A fund-flow series with labels normalised and the label change dated; the legal comparison; Tier 1 grand jury complaint and State Controller referral; Tier 2 CPRA petition; Tier 3 Prop 218 challenge via counsel (§8).

### S3 · The emergency contract nobody bid (procurement; homelessness services)

**Opening words.** "The county gave a nonprofit $14 million to run a shelter without any bidding because of an 'emergency'. The shelter opened late and has half the beds they said. Who decided this and where did the money go?"
**Clarifying.** Which agency signed (city, county, housing authority)? Is the shelter open; how do you know the bed count? Was it a new contract or an amendment to an existing one?
**Objectives.** Was the emergency exemption from bidding properly invoked and renewed; what did the contract require (beds, dates, outcomes); what was delivered and paid.
**Documents.** Emergency declaration and its periodic renewals (board items); the contract, its scope of work, deliverables and budget; amendments; invoices and payment records (CPRA); program performance reports to the board; nonprofit's IRS Form 990 (revenue, executive pay, related-party transactions) via public 990 databases; state grant source documents (e.g. state homelessness grant reporting) *(general knowledge)*.
**Actors.** Board of supervisors / council, department head, the nonprofit and its executives and board, subcontractors, state grantor, possible personal ties between officials and the nonprofit (DR6).
**Time.** Emergency declared; contract signed days later; opening date promised vs actual; renewals of the emergency finding.
**Money.** Contract ceiling vs paid; cost per bed-night promised vs actual; administrative overhead share.
**Competing explanations.** Genuine emergency and a provider market with one capable bidder; site-acquisition or permitting delays outside the provider's control; favouritism; weak contract management (no deliverable-based payment).
**Dead ends.** Invoices withheld as "containing client information" (redaction rather than refusal is the right outcome); nonprofit not subject to CPRA; Form 990 lags by one to two years.
**Iteration.** Contract amendments come to the board every few months; each is a checkpoint.
**Answer and action.** Deliverables vs payments table, exemption chronology; public comment; request to the county auditor; grand jury complaint if public funds were paid for undelivered services.

### S4 · The officer still on patrol (policing)

**Opening words.** "An officer who has had complaint after complaint, and was in a shooting, is still working my neighbourhood. Has anything been done?"
**Clarifying.** Do you know the officer's name or badge number, the dates of incidents, the agency? Was there a lawsuit or settlement? Do you want the individual's record or the department's pattern?
**Objectives.** What records of force and sustained findings exist; how the department and oversight body handled them; whether discipline policy was followed.
**Documents.** Records disclosable under SB 1421/SB 16 (Penal Code 832.7(b)): firearm discharges, force causing death or great bodily injury, sustained findings of sexual assault and of dishonesty, and (SB 16) more categories, to be produced no later than 45 days from request; agencies keep transparency portals ([First Amendment Coalition handbook](https://firstamendmentcoalition.org/handbook/police-transparency-handbook/); [Santa Clara Sheriff portal](https://sheriff.santaclaracounty.gov/transparency-portal/reports/officer-misconduct-and-certain-use-force-records)); oversight commission/inspector-general reports; civil suits and settlements (court dockets; council settlement approvals); use-of-force policy and discipline matrix; for Oakland, consent-decree monitor reports (§1 names the 23-year OPD consent decree).
**Actors.** Officer, chain of command, internal affairs, police commission / CPRB, chief, city attorney (settlements), DA (charging decisions), plaintiffs' lawyers, union (arbitration).
**Time.** Incident dates; investigation deadlines (the one-year limit for discipline under the Peace Officer Bill of Rights, *general knowledge*); 45-day production; arbitration reversal dates.
**Money.** Settlements paid; overtime; litigation cost.
**Competing explanations.** Allegations not sustained after investigation; sustained but discipline overturned in arbitration; investigation missed its deadline; record not covered by SB 1421 categories.
**Dead ends.** Most complaint records (not sustained, non-covered categories) stay confidential; heavy redaction of witness identities; agencies citing ongoing investigation to delay.
**Iteration.** Requests per incident; portal releases arrive irregularly; arbitration and lawsuits run years.
**Answer and action.** A per-incident table of what is public, its outcome, and what is legally unknowable; pattern questions to the oversight body; this is DR6 territory (a named person) with heightened care for accuracy and SLAPP risk (§2).

### S5 · The landlord who never fixes anything (housing; code enforcement)

**Opening words.** "Our building has had no heat, mold, and broken locks for two years. We've called the city again and again. Inspectors came, but nothing changes. Why isn't the city making the owner fix it?"
**Clarifying.** Address and unit count? Case numbers from the city? Who owns the building (individual, LLC)? Is the rent-control/rent board involved? Are tenants organised; any retaliation (OP8)?
**Objectives.** What the enforcement record shows; whether the city used the powers it has (notices, penalties, receivership); who the real owner is.
**Documents.** Code-enforcement case files and inspection reports (city permit/enforcement portal; CPRA); notices to repair or abate under Health & Safety Code 17980.6 and the escalation path to penalties and receivership under 17980.7 ([17980.6](https://california.public.law/codes/health_and_safety_code_section_17980.6); [17980.7, FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/health-and-safety-code/hsc-sect-17980-7/)); recorded notices of substandard conditions and liens (county recorder); assessor's parcel ownership; Secretary of State LLC filings (owner behind the LLC); rent-board petitions; small-claims or habitability suits.
**Actors.** Tenants, owner and LLC members, property manager, inspectors, code-enforcement manager, city attorney, rent board, lenders.
**Time.** Each complaint date, inspection date, compliance deadline in each notice, re-inspection; the city's own response-time standards.
**Money.** Fines assessed vs collected; liens; rent paid during violations.
**Competing explanations.** Owner partially complies before each re-inspection (case closed, reopened); understaffed enforcement; city policy favours voluntary compliance; owner insolvency.
**Dead ends.** Opaque LLC ownership; case files closed and archived; tenants fearing retaliation will not sign statements.
**Iteration.** Inspection cycles of 30–90 days; organising builds the record; each re-inspection is a monitored event.
**Answer and action.** Enforcement chronology vs statutory tools; request for escalation; rent-board petitions; a pattern across the owner's other buildings.

### S6 · The promised affordable units that never came (land use and zoning)

**Opening words.** "When they approved the tower on our corner, the developer promised affordable units and a park. The tower's open. Where's the park? Are the units really affordable?"
**Clarifying.** Project address and approval year? Was the promise in the approval conditions, a development agreement, a community-benefits agreement, or a press release?
**Objectives.** Find the binding conditions; test each against what exists; identify who enforces each.
**Documents.** Planning approval resolution and conditions of approval; development agreement; CEQA document and its Mitigation Monitoring and Reporting Program, which the lead agency must adopt and which is "designed to ensure compliance during project implementation" ([MMRP summary, CPUC decision citing PRC 21081.6](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/published/FINAL_DECISION/65276-07.htm)); affordable-housing regulatory agreement recorded against title; annual affordability compliance reports to the housing department; certificate of occupancy; park dedication or in-lieu fee records.
**Actors.** Developer, planning commission, council, planning and housing staff, the recorded regulatory agreement's monitor, community groups that negotiated benefits.
**Time.** Conditions often key to milestones ("prior to certificate of occupancy"); affordability term (e.g. 55 years) *(general knowledge)*.
**Money.** In-lieu fees paid instead of units; park fees; subsidy received.
**Competing explanations.** Conditions amended later (look for later approvals); fee paid instead (lawful); compliance pending a later phase; condition never enforced.
**Dead ends.** Conditions spread across many approvals and amendments; MMRP compliance never reported publicly; income-certification files confidential.
**Iteration.** Find all approvals first (the record is cumulative); then condition-by-condition testing.
**Answer and action.** A conditions register, each with status and evidence; a request to planning enforcement; public comment at the next related approval.

### S7 · The water bill that doubled (utilities and energy)

**Opening words.** "Our water rates went up 40% over three years for 'pipe replacement'. I don't see any pipes being replaced. Is the money being spent on what they said?"
**Clarifying.** Which utility (city department, special district, investor-owned)? Do you have the rate notice? (For an investor-owned utility the venue is the CPUC; this changes everything.)
**Objectives.** What the rate increase was justified by; whether the capital programme it funded is being delivered; whether the Prop 218 process was followed.
**Documents.** Prop 218 notice and hearing record (45-day notice, protest count) ([sample notice](https://www.cityofwestsacramento.org/government/departments/finance-department/water-and-sewer-rate-study/notice-of-public-hearing)); the cost-of-service rate study; capital improvement programme with annual targets (miles of pipe); annual reports and audited financials of the enterprise fund; reserve balances. For an investor-owned utility, the CPUC general rate case docket: application, intervenor testimony, public participation hearings, proposed and final decision ([CPUC GRC](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/generalratecase); [PG&E 2027 GRC forums](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/news-and-updates/all-news/cpuc-to-hold-public-forums-on-pacific-gas-and-electric-2027-rate-case)).
**Actors.** Utility board/council, general manager, rate consultant, ratepayers; CPUC, intervenors (e.g. TURN, whose intervenor compensation shows the formal participation path, [CPUC decision](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/published/FINAL_DECISION/95496.htm)).
**Time.** Rate steps by year; capital targets by year; GRC cycle (multi-year).
**Money.** Revenue raised vs capital spent vs reserves accumulated; transfers out (links to S2).
**Competing explanations.** Capital work delayed by contracting capacity (money sits in reserves); costs rose (fewer miles per dollar); revenue diverted.
**Dead ends.** Capital reports by project code, not by miles; rate studies long and technical; CPUC process formal and slow.
**Iteration.** Annual: each budget and capital report; rate cycles of 3–5 years.
**Answer and action.** Promised vs delivered capital by year; reserve trend; comment at the next rate hearing or protest; for CPUC, filing comments in the docket.

### S8 · The council vote and the donor (elections and campaign finance)

**Opening words.** "Our council member voted to give a towing contract to a company whose owners gave to her campaign. Is that legal?"
**Clarifying.** Which vote (date, item)? Which company? Did she disclose anything or recuse? Do you suspect a financial interest of her own, or only donations?
**Objectives.** Map contributions to the decision; test against conflict-of-interest and contribution rules; check disclosure.
**Documents.** Campaign statements (Form 460) and 24-hour late contribution reports (Form 497), filed with the city clerk, often on a NetFile portal ([FPPC where to file](https://www.fppc.ca.gov/learn/campaign-rules/where-and-when-to-file-campaign-statements/where-to-file-campaign-statements-form-460.html); [Stockton FPPC filings](https://www.stocktonca.gov/government/city_clerk/fair_political_practices_commission_(fppc).php)); the official's Statement of Economic Interests (Form 700); lobbyist registrations; the agenda item, staff report, minutes and video of the vote; the procurement file for the towing contract; corporate filings linking donors to the company; FPPC enforcement decisions *(general knowledge)*; local ethics commission rules.
**Actors.** Council member, her committee and treasurer, the company, its owners and their family members, lobbyists, staff who recommended the award, ethics commission, FPPC.
**Time.** Contribution dates relative to the vote; filing deadlines (late-reporting windows near elections).
**Money.** Contributions (individually and bundled), contract value.
**Competing explanations.** Contributions lawful and below limits; staff recommended the company on merit after a competitive process; the official had no financial interest (donations alone are not a conflict under the Political Reform Act, *general knowledge*, though some local rules and Gov. Code 84308 for certain bodies restrict them).
**Dead ends.** Donors giving through spouses, employees or affiliated LLCs (entity resolution); scanned paper filings; ethics bodies that do not discuss open matters.
**Iteration.** Each filing period adds data; the next related vote is a watch point.
**Answer and action.** A sourced contribution–decision timeline, with the legal standard compared; a complaint to the ethics commission or FPPC (Tier 1 in kind); public comment. Heavy DR6 obligation: named people, precise claims, no insinuation.

### S9 · The smoke from the recycling yard (environmental permits)

**Opening words.** "There's a metal recycler next to our school. Fires, smoke, dust on our cars. Does it even have a permit? Has anyone ever fined them?"
**Clarifying.** Address; how often and when (log dates); any photos or air-monitor readings? Health effects in the neighbourhood?
**Objectives.** Which permits apply and what they require; violation and enforcement history; whether enforcement escalated as rules require.
**Documents.** Air district permit to operate (or Title V permit for large sources), which gathers all applicable requirements into one permit; deviation reports the facility must file (e.g. within 10 days of discovery, and a cause report within 30 days, in sample permits); notices of violation and penalties ([BAAQMD Title V in your community](https://www.baaqmd.gov/~/media/Files/Engineering/Title%20V/in_your_community.ashx?la=en&la=en); [sample BAAQMD Title V permit](https://www.baaqmd.gov/~/media/files/engineering/title-v-permits/a4022/a4022200902renew02.pdf?rev=c468f3f51e0f45888a814e0a72402d6d&sc_lang=en)); EPA ECHO compliance history for federally tracked facilities (summarised in the same search, [EPA Title V program evaluation](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-01/title-v-operating-permit-program-evaluation-report-baaqmd-2024-01-11.pdf)); stormwater permits (regional water board); DTSC hazardous-waste records; fire department incident reports; local land-use permit and conditions (S6).
**Actors.** Operator, air district, regional water board, DTSC, fire department, city planning, school district, neighbours.
**Time.** Permit renewal dates; incident dates; NOV-to-resolution intervals.
**Money.** Penalties assessed vs settled; whether penalties are smaller than the cost of compliance (*inference*: the roadmap's "cost of doing business", §2).
**Competing explanations.** Facility within permit limits and the nuisance is unregulated dust; violations found and penalised but recurring; agencies each assume another has jurisdiction.
**Dead ends.** Split jurisdiction across five agencies; complaint records anonymised; settlements negotiated privately.
**Iteration.** Complaint logging by residents over months becomes evidence (OP8 "document everything"); each permit renewal is a public-comment window.
**Answer and action.** A per-agency compliance table; complaints filed with the right agency each time; comment at permit renewal; request to the school district about exposure.

### S10 · The lead in the school water (public health)

**Opening words.** "I just learned the water fountains at my daughter's school tested high for lead years ago. Did they ever fix it? What about other schools?"
**Clarifying.** Which school; where did you learn it; do you want this school or the district-wide pattern?
**Objectives.** Test results by fixture and date; remediation and retesting; notification to parents as required.
**Documents.** State dataset of lead sampling results at California schools (results above 5 ppb at about 17% of 7,188 schools tested; 15 ppb was the action level) ([State Water Board program](https://www.waterboards.ca.gov/drinking_water/certlic/drinkingwater/leadsamplinginschools.html); [CA open data set](https://data.ca.gov/dataset/drinking-water-results-of-lead-sampling-of-drinking-water-in-california-schools); [Capital & Main](https://capitalandmain.com/lead-found-in-drinking-fountains-at-17-percent-of-california-public-schools-0827)); district notices to parents; work orders for fixture replacement; retest results; board items on remediation funding.
**Actors.** Water system, district facilities and health staff, State Water Board Division of Drinking Water, parents, county health.
**Time.** Sample dates; required follow-up actions after an exceedance; retest dates.
**Money.** Remediation budget vs spent.
**Competing explanations.** Fixtures removed or replaced but no record posted; retest below threshold; never acted on.
**Dead ends.** Data by sample point codes that do not map to fixtures; work orders not public; district says "resolved" without documents.
**Iteration.** Short: data pull, a CPRA request for work orders, a check visit; then a district-wide pattern query across all schools.
**Answer and action.** Fixture-level status; a request to the board; a pattern finding across schools; a watch on the dataset for updates.

### S11 · The bus line that was supposed to open in 2022 (transit)

**Opening words.** "The rapid bus line on our avenue was supposed to open years ago. The road has been torn up forever and businesses are closing. Why is it so late and how much over budget?"
**Clarifying.** Which agency and project; is it federally funded; which businesses and what losses (a separate line of inquiry)?
**Objectives.** Baseline vs current schedule and cost; causes; whether oversight bodies flagged it.
**Documents.** For federally funded capital projects, the Full Funding Grant Agreement, in which the grantee "commits to complete its new starts project on time, within budget" ([FTA FFGA guidance](https://www.transit.dot.gov/regulations-and-guidance/fta-circulars/full-funding-grant-agreements-guidance)); FTA risk and contingency reviews that "quantify uncertainties ... for project cost and schedule compared to the project baseline" ([FTA OP 40](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/2024-11/Oversight-Procedure-40-Risk-and-Contingency-Review.pdf?frame=oigpdbgohuhec)); monthly Project Management Oversight Contractor reports, some published ([example PMOC report](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/2021-07/esa-monthly-pmoc-report-september-2020.pdf)); GAO reviews of the programme ([GAO-23-105479](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-23-105479.pdf)); agency board reports, change orders, contractor claims; third-party utility agreements ([FTA OP 39](https://www.transit.dot.gov/sites/fta.dot.gov/files/2024-11/Oversight-Procedure-39-Review-of-Third-Party-Agreements-for-Major-Capital-Projects.pdf)).
**Actors.** Transit agency board and staff, construction contractors, utilities relocating lines, city public works, FTA and its PMOC, local businesses.
**Time.** Like S1 at a larger scale: baseline at FFGA, each re-baseline, each PMOC forecast.
**Money.** FFGA budget, contingency drawdown, local match, claims.
**Competing explanations.** Utility relocation conflicts (common); design changes; contractor disputes; agency management capacity.
**Dead ends.** Many PMOC reports are not routinely posted; claims under negotiation are withheld.
**Iteration.** Monthly oversight reports make this a monitoring investigation over years.
**Answer and action.** The S1 pattern: chronology and attribution. It shows S1's needs generalise to any capital project.

### S12 · The eviction court that defaults everyone (courts)

**Opening words.** "In our county, tenants who go to eviction court seem to lose by default because hearings are rescheduled without notice and there are no interpreters. Is the court following its own rules?"
**Clarifying.** Do you have specific case numbers? Which courthouse? Are you a tenant, an advocate, a legal-aid worker? (Case files of named parties raise privacy duties.)
**Objectives.** Measure default rates and continuances; test against court rules and the court's own language-access plan and budget.
**Documents.** Case dockets (register of actions) by case number; court statistics reported to the Judicial Council; the court's language-access plan; interpreter staffing and budget records. A superior court is not subject to the CPRA for its administrative records; Rule of Court 10.500 gives access to "judicial administrative records" (budget, management), not adjudicative files ([Rule 10.500](https://courts.ca.gov/cms/rules/index/ten/rule10_500); [Sacramento court page](https://www.saccourt.ca.gov/general-information/judicial-administrative-records)). Unlawful-detainer records are also masked from public access in California for a period (*general knowledge*, to be checked).
**Actors.** Presiding judge, court executive officer, clerk's office, interpreters, landlords' and tenants' lawyers, legal aid, Judicial Council.
**Time.** Statutory timelines for unlawful-detainer cases; hearing dates and continuances per case.
**Money.** Interpreter budget; fees.
**Competing explanations.** Default rates reflect tenants not appearing for reasons outside the court; staffing shortage; a policy or practice change at one courthouse.
**Dead ends.** Different access regime from agencies (S2's CPRA tools do not apply); masked case records; bulk docket data costly or unavailable.
**Iteration.** Court-watching (members attending hearings and logging) builds a dataset over months; administrative records requests to the court.
**Answer and action.** A measured pattern with method shown (OP2); a letter to the presiding judge; testimony to the Judicial Council. Shows the engine must know *which access regime* applies to which publisher.

### S13 · Library hours cut while the budget grew (budgets)

**Opening words.** "They cut our branch library to three days a week 'for budget reasons', but the city budget went up. Where did the money go?"
**Clarifying.** Which branch and when were hours cut? Is there a library parcel tax or measure that promised minimum hours? (Many do; that becomes the standard.)
**Objectives.** Test the "budget reasons" claim; test any measure's maintenance-of-effort or minimum-service terms.
**Documents.** Adopted budgets and mid-year revisions (department and fund level); actuals (ACFR, open-budget portal); position control and vacancy reports; the library measure's text and its annual oversight-committee reports; council staff reports on the cut.
**Actors.** Library director, budget office, council, measure oversight committee, library workers' union.
**Time.** Budget cycle; date of cut; measure's term and renewal date.
**Money.** Library appropriation vs actual spending (vacancy savings swept), measure revenue vs use, general-fund contribution vs maintenance-of-effort floor.
**Competing explanations.** Budget grew elsewhere (public safety, pensions); hiring freeze left funded positions vacant, so hours were cut although money was budgeted; measure revenue substituted for general-fund support.
**Dead ends.** Budget books with changing line structures across years; "actuals" published late; vacancy data held by HR.
**Iteration.** One budget cycle to establish; repeats yearly.
**Answer and action.** Budget vs actual vs vacancy series; the measure's terms compared; comment at budget hearings; request to the oversight committee.

### S14 · Schools closed by a decision nobody saw (open meetings; schools)

**Opening words.** "The school board voted to close four schools. Parents only found out the next morning. It feels like it was decided behind closed doors."
**Clarifying.** Which meeting; was the item on the agenda, and how was it worded? When were the supporting documents posted? Was there a closed session before it?
**Objectives.** Test the board's process against the Brown Act and its own closure policy (community engagement steps, facilities analysis).
**Documents.** Agendas (posted at least 72 hours before a regular meeting with a brief general description of each item); writings distributed to the board less than 72 hours before a meeting must be made public at the same time ([Brown Act, Attorney General guide](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/media/the-brown-act.pdf); [CSBA on late materials](https://publications.csba.org/california-school-news/july-2022/requirements-when-materials-are-provided-less-than-72-hours-prior-to-a-regular-meeting/)); closed-session agendas and reportable actions; meeting video; the district's closure policy and any state-required steps; consultant facilities studies; emails among board members (serial-meeting test).
**Actors.** Board members, superintendent, consultants, parents, the county office of education, the DA (Brown Act enforcement).
**Time.** Posting timestamps vs meeting time; statutory cure-and-correct windows for a Brown Act demand (*general knowledge*: short, measured in days).
**Money.** Projected savings vs costs; facility sale or lease plans (links to S6).
**Competing explanations.** Process was lawful but communication poor; agenda description legally adequate though vague; a real serial meeting.
**Dead ends.** Email requests delayed past the cure window; deliberations in closed session properly confidential.
**Iteration.** Short and deadline-driven: the cure-and-correct window forces action within days; then a longer policy compliance look.
**Answer and action.** A posting-timestamp chronology; a cure-and-correct demand letter (Tier 1 "Brown Act report", §8) within the window. Shows the engine must surface **deadlines that bind the member**, not only the government.

### Portability note (outside California)

Every scenario has an analogue elsewhere with different machinery. For example, under the Texas Public Information Act a body that wants to withhold must ask the Attorney General for a ruling within ten business days or the information is presumed open, and the Attorney General rules within 45 business days ([Texas AG PIA Handbook 2024](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/sites/default/files/files/divisions/open-government/publicinfo_hb.pdf)); federal FOIA runs on 20 working days with appeals (*general knowledge*). The engine must hold *jurisdiction-specific* rules as data, not as code (`BIO_Complete_Roadmap_v5.md` §3, §6 DR2, DR15). *(inference)*
