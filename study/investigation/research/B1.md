## 1. How practitioners work, step by step

### 1.1 Story-Based Inquiry (SBI): the hypothesis method

Source: Hunter et al., *Story-Based Inquiry: A Manual for Investigative Journalists*, UNESCO 2011 (SBI below), read whole: https://bird.tools/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/SBI_Manual.pdf (UNESCO record https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000193078_eng). Second edition announced https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/new-edition-story-based-inquiry-global-reference-investigative-journalists.

**The process (SBI ch. 1, repeated as the frame of every chapter):** discover a subject → create a hypothesis to verify → seek open-source data to verify it → seek human sources → organise the data as it is collected → put it in narrative order and compose → quality control → publish, promote and defend. Organising is not a phase after research: "You do not do research, then organise, then write. Instead, you organise as you research" (ch. 5).

**Subject vs story.** "Corruption … is a subject. It is not a story." A hypothesis is a specific, falsifiable story ("Corruption in the school system has destroyed parents' hopes…") (ch. 2). Pursuing a subject wastes time; pursuing a story focuses it.

**Hypothesis rules (ch. 2):**
| rule | content |
|---|---|
| Length | at most three sentences: "if it is longer than that you probably don't understand it yourself" |
| Shape | news (present problem) → cause (past) → what must change (future); "It contains news, a cause, and a solution" |
| Decomposition | split the hypothesis into its terms; each term generates specific questions; verify each term separately — "if the entire hypothesis can't be confirmed, its separate terms can nonetheless be verified" |
| Precision | "If you use the word 'house' … is it a villa, or a penthouse, or a shack?" — the more precise, the easier to verify |
| Objectivity | "If the facts say the hypothesis is wrong, we change the hypothesis. We do not try to make the facts disappear." "Try to disprove them as well as prove them." |
| Revision | neither cling to a mistaken hypothesis "nor leap in a new direction at the first contrary fact"; the warning sign is "a fair amount of information, but it doesn't make sense" |
| Subsidiary hypotheses | depth research yields new angles that "require new hypotheses that can be verified in turn"; unrelated ones may be parked |
| Unverifiable | "A hypothesis that can't be verified in whole or part is mere speculation" |

**Promise vs reality — the official version as hypothesis (ch. 2).** "Most investigations are about the difference between a promise and the reality of whether or not it was kept. Thus the official promise often serves as a hypothesis, and verification shows whether or not the promise has been kept." The Contaminated Blood case: the agency head's three-part explanation was taken as a hypothesis and each part tested against the literature; none held. This is directly Bob's school case: "reconstruction would take no longer than 1 school year" is the official promise to test *(inference)*.

**Outcome bracketing (ch. 2).** A hypothesis lets the investigator state in advance a worst case (quick disproof, project ends cheaply), a minimum (hypothesis verified), and a maximum (follow-on stories). The Baby Doe case shows the boss's hypothesis disproved and replaced by a better, provable one — a disproved hypothesis is a result, not a failure.

**Strategy and order (ch. 2–3).** Start with the easiest questions, answerable from documents without talking to anyone, because open-source work "makes no noise"; the Center for Public Integrity required "several weeks of research before they are allowed to call sources." Open sources (est. ~90% of what is called "secret") let the investigator ask "This is what happened, isn't it?" instead of "What happened?" — confirmation from a position of strength. If none of the hypothesis's elements are supported in open sources, "either your hypothesis is seriously mistaken, or someone is working very hard to conceal the story" (ch. 3).

**Managing an investigation as a project (ch. 2).** Once a hypothesis appears valid, set: deliverables (minimum and maximum), process milestones, costs and rewards, promotion; hold a weekly review of progress where "verification of the hypothesis and discovery of new information are the first concerns" but time and cost also count; "Delays which threaten the future of the project must not be tolerated."

**Backgrounding (ch. 3).** Map the subject: key actors (individuals and institutions), key issues, key dates and events. Follow references from document to document. When blocked, "take note of the obstacle, and seek information about something related to it instead"; never let one source become indispensable. Move from general sources (press) to expert sources (the original report). Ask professionals which sources they use: "Don't just collect facts: Collect the methods by which the facts are found." Obtaining a document is not understanding it: find an expert interpreter of its language with no conflict of interest (example: a municipal subsidies report read "line by line with a former municipal employee … who worked on budgetary matters"). Kinds of open source named: incident reports, inspection reports ("If there's no report, that's a story"), complaints, court records, cadaster/property records, company registers and filings, annual reports, international audits, government libraries.

**Human sources (ch. 4).** Source map: a graphic of all actors and their relations, built early, so that "if one source is blocked, you can go to another source who can see past the obstacle." Assess each source on three axes (SVT/British intelligence diagram): *motivation* (comprehensible and convincing?), *access* (is the source in a position to know? first-hand?), *quality* (documents? fits other data? something to hide? correct before?). Mid-rank operational staff are the best sources. Attribution states: off the record, not for attribution, on the record. Anonymous material only if documentary evidence exists elsewhere, it fits a pattern of verified information, or the source has been credible before; "demand the document" rather than accept a quoted extract. Every meeting ends by asking for the next source. Keep sources informed and involved over time; review notes immediately after each interview.

**Organisation (ch. 5)** — the case-file model, detailed in §2 below.

**Writing (ch. 6).** The verified hypothesis becomes the "nut graf": a few sentences stating the essence. Structure: present → past → future (why should I care; how did it come about; will it ever be over, how?). "The facts do not tell the story. The story tells the facts." Endings: journalists "do not have the right to invent" a resolution; compose a "closer" that marks where the narrative stops and points to who has responsibility to act.

**Quality control (ch. 7).** Four components: (1) the facts add up to a larger truth — "If an alternative explanation makes more sense than yours, something is wrong"; (2) a known source for every factual assertion; (3) correct the errors found; (4) remove emotional noise. Procedure: two people; the checker asks of every fact "How do you know that?"; "If there is no source, the author has to find one. If no source can be found, the passage must be cut." Interpretations of a target's motives are cut unless documented. Mark the gap-filling the mind does ("it must have been like this"): "Either tell the reader that you are speculating, or acknowledge what you do not know." Right of reply: every criticised party informed of every criticism, given reasonable time, the right person responding, and the reporter's efforts to get a response documented. Contact targets "early and often", because their explanation sometimes shows the hypothesis to be wrong. Deborah Nelson's "transparent approach": tell each actor what you are finding and ask for comment at every stage. A footnoted version of the story, each footnote rechecked against the source document; download web pages before publication because they get taken down.

**Publication (ch. 8).** Results depend on coalitions and allies (citing Protess et al., *The Journalism of Outrage*): notify friendly sources, legislators, NGOs, citizen groups; give colleagues the key documents; anticipate counter-attacks from the targets' official responses so far and prepare follow-ups.

### 1.2 Open-source investigation and verification (Bellingcat, Berkeley Protocol lineage)

Primary source: Bellingcat and Global Legal Action Network, *Justice and Accountability Unit Methodology for Online Open Source Investigations* (Dec 2022), read whole except two annexes outside scope: https://www.bellingcat.com/app/uploads/2022/12/JA-Manual-for-PUBLICATION.pdf. It is a set of standard operating procedures that implements the Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open Source Investigations (OHCHR/UC Berkeley, 2020) for court-grade work; it states that it "may not be compatible with organisations whose objective it is to get through a lot of data quite quickly" and that "a more light-touch methodology could be considered" for volunteer coalitions.

**The workflow (sections IV–VII):**
1. *Preparation:* dedicated device, VPN, passive research accounts; never contact a source or interact with an account without the lead investigator's authorisation.
2. *Investigation folder per incident*, named by incident ID, holding: an **Incident Assessment** (living document, from a fixed template), **Research Notes** per investigator (a search log: every search term, engine, variation and "if you search for something that is not obvious, make a note of why"), a **Links sheet**, captured web pages (Hunchly records every page visited), media, misc.
3. *Discovery:* work from a **Table of Factual Inquiries** (the questions that matter, derived from the legal elements); "actively pursue alternative possibilities"; pursue "all lines of reasonable enquiry including those leading towards evidence that may explain the attack or suggest alternative attribution".
4. *Preservation:* every relevant link goes to a preservation sheet (an external partner, Mnemonic, archives it immutably); the analysis database (Uwazi) "does not preserve content" — analysis and preservation are separate systems.
5. *Earliest source:* always find the "Original Online Source"; cite the earliest poster even if you found a later copy; if an earlier version turns up later, edit the report and note it.
6. *Verification & analysis:* geolocation, chronolocation, corroboration; verification notes "are not 'evidence' in themselves, rather they are a description of the significance of the evidence".
7. *Logging off:* save links and leads, hand links to preservation, upload content, so the next session "can pick back up where you left off".
8. *Review:* first-instance investigators, then reviewing investigators and a separate legal review.

**Two categories of material (section III):** *examinable* content (an item the investigator can interrogate: a video, a satellite image, a flight log) vs *descriptive* content (an account of events — a news article, an NGO's witness statements — "not necessarily more or less reliable", but not something the investigator's techniques can test). Descriptive content is summarised; examinable content is verified.

**Language of confidence, not scores:** "We will not attribute formal confidence gradings or degrees of likelihood to any findings"; use "appears to be", "suggests", "indicates", "strongly indicates" — but do not over-hedge where a fact is certain by logic ("must have taken place on or before 12 March" if posted on 12 March). Casualty figures from others are recorded as descriptive content with no comment on their veracity unless independently verifiable.

**Incident Assessment template (Annex VIII):** ID; location; date *prima facie* (claimed) vs date *assessed*; 2–4 key findings; background summary of descriptive content with the searches that found it; analysis of examinable content including a **timeline** ("Don't fill in gaps if you are unsure, simply report and cite the events … in the order that the evidence indicates"); "If you cannot explain something that happened, it is just as important to note it"; statements from each party; a short factual conclusion; **Further Action** (follow-ups, linked incidents).

**Database fields (Annex IX, Uwazi):** three entity types — *Incident* (ID, prima-facie date, assessed date, description, **level of investigation: detailed or stub**, cautions flag for content shown to be bogus, investigator responsible, incident group, stage of investigation), *Content* (title, original post text, generated ID, link(s) to incident(s), live link and archived link, posted date/time, filmed date, geolocation, source actor), *Actor* (name as displayed, description including "affiliations, credibility and length of presence online", multiple profiles merged into one actor). "Stub" incidents are logged for every related allegation "even if they remain allegations" so patterns can be grouped.

**Bias controls (Annex I):** begin "with no settled hypothesis as to what happened or what party was responsible"; search all sides' platforms; equal verification effort on content from either side; a list of questions so "no relevant lines of inquiry are skipped due to bias"; separate review. Algorithmic bias in search engines is mitigated by many varied queries in several languages, not by tricks.

**Evidence principles (Annex V):** challenges come on *impartiality*, *authenticity*, *reliability*; hearsay attached to a video ("school full of children bombed") is evidence that the statement was made, not that it is true; disclosure duty means records of the investigation itself (search logs, draft changes, a conversation agreeing to ignore contrary information) may have to be produced, so the process record is part of the evidence. Neutral language, "free from legal or moral commentary"; write-ups "comprehensive enough so that you or another person could retrace your steps".

**Corruption research by open sources** (Toler, Bellingcat, 2016, https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2016/09/05/how-to-uncover-corruption-using-open-source-research/): compare visible assets to declared income; follow relatives' social media; above all "check public records" — tax declarations, business registrations, land deeds, court documents — via aggregators such as OCCRP's Investigative Dashboard and national registries (UK Companies House); money-laundering indicators (companies with no clear commercial purpose, pass-through flows, abnormal prices, disproportionate agent fees, unnecessary complexity). Limits: open sources raise questions; discrepancies are not proof.

**Verification Handbook for Investigative Reporting** (ed. Silverman, EJC; https://datajournalism.com/read/handbook/verification-2):
- *Data quality* (Segnini, ch. 5): "data lies — just as much as people"; on receipt, check completeness (extremes and blanks — 53% of World Bank projects had zero cost listed), duplicates (count records per ID), accuracy (sample records against source documents), integrity (projects from 1986 still "active"), and decode the codes and acronyms (they can be the story); after analysis, ground-truth: "Not even the best data analysis can replace on-the-ground journalism and field verification".
- *Companies* (Sharife, ch. 4): "Everything has a paper trail"; registry filings (shareholders, directors, dates), court records, land documents, domain records; cross-reference; in-country verification.
- *Newsroom fact-checking* (Janssen on Der Spiegel's documentation department, ch. 10): every proof is marked fact by fact with standard symbols (correct / incorrect / unverifiable); priorities under time pressure: dates and times, internal contradictions, names and titles, quotations in context, currency of sources; prefer original documents over summaries. One studied issue produced 1,153 corrections.

### 1.3 Citizen and community investigation guides

Primary source: Tactical Tech, *Exposing the Invisible: The Kit* (https://kit.exposingtheinvisible.org/en/), written for citizen investigators without a newsroom behind them. Chapters read whole: "What Makes an Investigation", "Evaluating Evidence and Information Sources" (Rautner, 2020), "How to Manage Your Sources", "Crowdsourcing Evidence for Investigations" (Bohdanova, 2024), "Get Your Facts Straight: The Basics of Fact-Checking" (Adhikari, 2021).

- **Evidence defined generically:** "information that is material to the question … you are investigating"; "not every piece of information is, or can turn into, evidence."
- **A checklist for good evidence** (14 tests): first-hand; documented and preserved; timely; verifiable; origin confirmable by a third party; content confirmable by a third party; shareable; unbroken chain of custody; uncorrupted; carries metadata; connects to other evidence; shows where the information is weak; does not expose sources to unmanageable risk; may contradict your preconceptions. "Not every piece of evidence … will meet all these conditions … some … will meet a lot of them – those pieces are the ones you may want to prioritise."
- **"Without documentation, it didn't happen":** a log of every step (date, tool, outcome, including failures — "I tried calling … but I was diverted to voicemail"), contact records with how each contact was found, a rolling narrative outline that shows what is missing, and an evidence log with storage location, status, who collected it and who touched it. Documentation also lets someone else take over the investigation if the original investigator stops.
- **A ladder of evidence strength:** word of mouth → expert statement → second-hand accounts → research reports → official documents (strong, but "mind institutional bias") → photos/video/audio → first-hand observation.
- **"If it's not verified, it's not valid";** verify always and iteratively, because new evidence "may cast reasonable doubt on old evidence that you may have already verified"; three phases: verify the source, the content, its relevance.
- **Working hypothesis** (citing SBI): changes with the evidence; "it is extremely rare for any investigation to provide 100% proof"; the realistic result is "an approximation of proof where (after looking at all possible explanations) your hypothesis is the most likely explanation". At a dead end: give up, change the hypothesis, or "publish the information you have in a transparent way without making claims for which you do not have any evidence".
- **Direct vs indirect evidence is relative to the claim:** a spokesperson's denial is indirect evidence that the company did not pollute but direct evidence that the company denies it. Even direct evidence may prove only one component of the hypothesis (a lab report proves pollution, not who polluted).
- **Source strength depends on the claim, not the source alone:** the same company-commissioned study is "medium" evidence for "Company X is not polluting" but "strong" for "Company X is polluting" (an admission against interest). Official data alone "should rarely be considered strong enough to contradict your hypothesis on its own."
- **Bias:** confirmation bias, sunk-cost effect, availability, anchoring; especially dangerous for citizen investigators who "often work alone … have personal or emotional attachments to the outcome … and can lack review processes". Remedies: second opinions, revisit conclusions after time has passed, try to disprove. Competing hypotheses in a consistency matrix (ACH, "common sense is most likely sufficient" except for long, complex cases); the example resolves to a benign explanation (storm-driven oxygen loss) that ends the investigation.
- **Evidence sheet fields:** ID (typed prefix), date created, file name, storage link, type, latitude/longitude, location, source name, description, notes (concerns about accuracy or intentions).
- **Mitigation table:** for each piece of evidence, the risk to it and the action that reduces the risk (e.g. a council water test with no toxicity: "Samples may have been taken at a different time or at locations that would not capture pollution … Request the full tests over a few years").
- **Publishing:** describe methods; mark unverified material as unverified; publish data in reusable formats; protect confidential sources.
- **Crowdsourcing** (structured vs unstructured call-outs; "decide how much verification you deem 'enough'" before collecting; vet unverifiable reports for plausibility of time and place; "Clearly mark 'unverified' data"; close the feedback loop with contributors, who will not contribute if they see no result).
- **Fact-checking:** archive the claim first; quote the claim verbatim and check it part by part; give the process so anyone can replicate it; graded verdicts (false, misleading, half-true …) rather than a binary; "you cannot fact-check people's intentions, nor … predictions"; correct errors openly. OCCRP's fact-checking standard: "proof of everything … recorded interviews, official documents, written notes, links".

### 1.4 Following the money: procurement, companies and public records

GIJN's own pages (gijn.org) refused every automated fetch (HTTP 403 bot challenge), so GIJN material here is cited from search-result text only and marked so; the Open Contracting Partnership (OCP) and the sources above stand in for it. The deep procurement domain (OCDS, red-flag indicators, US construction contracting) belongs to unit B5 and is not repeated here.

- **The procurement cycle as a sequence of documents.** OCP's journalist page (https://www.open-contracting.org/implement/investigating-public-contracts/) frames the investigation around planning → tendering → delivery and around three core elements to track: **buyers, companies, projects**; procurement is "the single greatest corruption risk in the public sector". It points journalists to a red-flags guide, a data-driven tipsheet and the OCP Data Registry of OCDS data.
- **Red flags by stage** (GIJN's COVID-contracts guide, from search-result text, https://gijn.org/stories/researching-government-contracts-for-covid-19-spending/, not fetched): no-bid contracts justified as emergencies; narrowly written specifications; fast procedures and short notice; too many or too few bidders; unknown bidders winning; very low winning bids; rejected bids; then look at ties between winners and officials or their relatives, and the "credentials and past performance of winning companies and their officers". Findings in such work: collusion, high prices, unnecessary purchases, inexperienced contractors, secrecy.
- **Who the company is:** registry filings (shareholders, directors, incorporation dates), court records, property records, domain records, cross-referenced against each other and, where possible, verified on the ground (Sharife, Verification Handbook ch. 4). OCCRP's Aleph does this at scale by modelling every record as an entity and cross-referencing lists against registries, sanctions lists, procurement and court data (§2.4).
- **Document-driven reporting in practice — the CT Mirror school-construction investigation** (https://ctmirror.org/2024/01/14/ct-kosta-diamantis-school-construction-aais-bestech/): the backbone was "more than four years' of emails" obtained by public-records requests, read against school building committee minutes and meeting videos from several towns, the contracts actually awarded, the rejected competing bids, and state grant records (two firms received "more than 98% of the work" from a four-company state list). The narrative was built chronologically from those records, and the key evidence was the officials' own words in email ("ALL school construction abatement and demolition is going to be done by the state contract!") and in minutes ("It appears our hands are bound").
- **Public records requests** (Berkeley Graduate School of Journalism tutorial, https://multimedia.journalism.berkeley.edu/tutorials/public-records-act-requests/; MuckRock and GIJN via search text): cite the statute (California Public Records Act; agencies must respond within 10 days); describe records specifically, with a date range and a format; cap fees and ask for a public-interest waiver; expect full release, partial release with redactions, withholding, or "no responsive documents"; "Most FOIA experts will encourage journalists to always appeal denials"; track every request, response, fee and what was or was not received (the MuckRock model). SBI adds: find out from officials and archivists which records exist and where, before asking (ch. 3), and treat a missing record as a finding ("If there's no report, that's a story").

### 1.5 How journalists investigate a delayed or over-budget public construction project

Synthesised from the cases read (*inference* where marked) — together they show a consistent method:

| step | what reporters do | example |
|---|---|---|
| Fix the promise | find the baseline commitment in a dated document: funding agreement, bond measure text, board resolution, contract completion date, project budget | Honolulu rail: 2009 federal funding agreement, $5.12bn, against the 2021 financial update, $12.4bn (Civil Beat, https://www.civilbeat.org/2021/08/for-cost-overruns-honolulu-rail-is-in-a-league-of-its-own-new-data-shows/) |
| Measure the gap | current cost and date from the owner's own status reports and financial updates | same |
| Benchmark | compare with peer projects so "late" and "over" have a reference: 29 recent US transit projects came in at 98% of initial budget | same (FTA "predicted vs actual" data) |
| Read the auditors | state audits, comptroller and city-auditor reports, grand-jury reports, federal oversight reviews give documented causes | Honolulu: 2019 state audit found prematurely awarded contracts triggered delay claims ($108m, $50m); NYC: a comptroller audit found that "in 85 percent of the negotiated change orders" the School Construction Authority "made no effort to discover the reasonableness of the contractor's estimate" (City Journal, https://www.city-journal.org/article/why-new-york-cant-build-schools); Oakland: city auditor found 47-day invoice processing, half of construction-engineer posts vacant, unaudited bond funds, five firms taking 61% of capital dollars (Oaklandside, https://oaklandside.org/2026/09/09/oakland-contracting-problems-oakland-auditor-report/) |
| Follow the claims and lawsuits | contractor delay claims and suits give the contractor's version of who caused the delay | Honolulu: a contractor's $320m claim alleging the city's "gross mismanagement" (Civil Beat 2025) |
| Get the owner's explanation, then test it | record the official reason verbatim and check it against records | NYC: "delays … are often due to the discovery of additional repair needs or unforeseen field conditions" (SCA spokesman, Chalkbeat, https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2025/12/11/nyc-parents-want-more-oversight-over-school-construction-authority-delays-cost-overruns/) |
| Aggregate | turn one school into a pattern: 57% of NYC capital projects missed deadlines in 2024 | Chalkbeat, from public performance data and NYC OpenData |
| Ground it in the affected | parents, parent councils, site visits and photos of the unfinished work | Chalkbeat: seven elected parent boards; ramps blocking dismissal for two years |
| Follow the money trail of the procurement | how the contractor was chosen, competing bids, who directed the award | CT Mirror (above) |
| Name what would fix it | dashboards, hearings, oversight bodies — the "closer" SBI asks for | Chalkbeat: parents asked for a public project dashboard and listening sessions; a council hearing was promised |

The recurring competing explanations in these stories *(inference from the cases)*: unforeseen site conditions; design errors and ambiguous specifications driving change orders; scope added by the owner; owner capacity (vacancies, slow payment, weak change-order review); contractor performance or concentration among few contractors; funding gaps, inflation and reallocation of bond money; permitting; and, in the worst cases, steering of contracts. A reporter's job is to find which of these the documents support for *this* project, which is exactly SBI's "official version as hypothesis" applied to the owner's explanation.

### 1.6 How newsrooms track an investigation

- **SBI master file** and **Svith's segmented spreadsheet** (§2.1): document list, source list, chronology, contact log.
- **Bellingcat incident folder**: assessment, research notes with search log, links sheet, captures, media; plus a database (Uwazi) of incidents, content and actors, and a separate preservation archive (§1.2).
- **Tactical Tech logs**: process log, contact list with provenance, evidence sheet, rolling outline (§1.3).
- **OCCRP Aleph** (https://docs.aleph.occrp.org/users/getting-started/key-terms/): an "investigation" workspace holding documents (with OCR, extracted names, phone numbers, addresses), entities, network diagrams and timelines, shareable with groups; cross-referencing against hundreds of datasets.
- **Fact-check apparatus**: the footnoted draft (SBI ch. 7, Tactical Tech), Der Spiegel's per-fact marks, OCCRP's "proof of everything".
- **City Bureau Documenters** (https://www.citybureau.org/documenters; Knight Foundation, https://knightfoundation.org/articles/the-documenters-model-how-city-bureau-makes-information-accessible-to-all/; Nieman Lab via search text): trained, paid residents attend public meetings and file notes on a form that records decisions *and the questions they had*, which professional journalists use as leads; scrapers collect meeting times and agendas. Over 1,000 trained to cover 1,300+ meetings (Knight). This is the nearest existing model of members feeding a shared public record of government action.
- **Promise trackers** (PolitiFact, https://politifact.com/article/2018/feb/12/principles-truth-o-meter-politifacts-methodology-i/ and https://www.pressclubinstitute.org/2025/02/06/how-politifact-uses-the-maga-meter-to-monitor-presidential-campaign-promises/): a promise is "a prospective statement of an action or outcome that is verifiable"; interim ratings *In the Works* / *Stalled*, final ratings *Promise Kept* / *Compromise* / *Promise Broken*, plus *Not Yet Rated*; rated on "verifiable outcomes, not on intentions or effort"; for claims, "The burden of proof is on the speaker", a source list is published with every check, and a rating is set by three editors with two votes deciding.

## 2. Data models and tools: what the practitioners' artefacts are

### 2.1 The case file ("master file") — SBI ch. 5
| artefact | what it holds | rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Document archive** | every asset ("A source's business card is a document. So is an official report, a news clip, interview notes") | collect → review and mark key passages → title or number it (web pages: record the original URL; a page saved under its own title is "hidden in plain sight") → file by subject, newest first within subject → review monthly → copy across files when a document bears on two subjects (the FBI practice, to raise the chance of connections) → back up off-site |
| **Master file** | one sequence holding all facts, extracts, portraits and notes, **ordered chronologically**, actors' portraits inserted where they first appear | every item carries its source (full citation) and physical location; contacts with sources and promises made to them are logged; insights flagged with a keyword (NOTE/TN); dates and names in one consistent format so search works; no material that could expose a confidential source |
| **Document list** (Svith) | No, Date, From, To, Subject/keywords, Form (email, letter, phone…), link | "Give all documents a number and keep the paper documents in numeric order" |
| **Source list** | No, title, person, organisation, contact details | — |
| **Chronology** | Date, Source, Organisation, Event (act), Content/keywords — "including all contacts with sources" | sortable by actor |
| **Contact log** | Date, Time, Researcher, Person, Org, Contact mode, Answered?, Content/next step ("Call again 15.00") | — |

Purpose stated in three tests: you know what you have; you can put your hand on any asset "within 30 seconds"; you can make connections across assets. The structure "is telling you what data it needs to be completed" — gaps in the file are the next research tasks.

### 2.2 The hypothesis and its decomposition — SBI ch. 2
A hypothesis is a ≤3-sentence story (present problem, past cause, future remedy), broken into terms, each term into questions, each question into a verification task. Subsidiary hypotheses hang off the main one. Outcome bracket: worst / minimum / maximum. Project parameters: deliverables, milestones, costs, promotion; weekly review.

### 2.3 Source map and source assessment — SBI ch. 4
A graph of all actors and their relations (the "village"), with the people the story is about at the centre; used to route around a blocked source. Each source assessed on motivation, access (first-hand? who else knows?), quality (documents? consistent with other data? track record?). Attribution state per piece of information: on the record / not for attribution / off the record; "NFC" marks in notes.

### 2.4 Entity models — Bellingcat/Uwazi and OCCRP/FollowTheMoney
- **Uwazi (Bellingcat J&A):** *Incident* (ID, claimed vs assessed date, level of investigation: detailed or **stub**, cautions, investigator, incident group, stage), *Content* (original text, links live and archived, posted vs filmed date, geolocation, source actor, links to one or more incidents), *Actor* (profiles, description of affiliation and credibility). Analysis database separate from preservation archive.
- **FollowTheMoney (Aleph):** entity types including Person, Company, Public body, Legal entity (when you cannot tell), Contract, Call for tenders, Project, Court case, Real estate, Event, Note; relationship types including Ownership, Directorship, Employment, Family, Payment, **Contract award**, **Project participant**, Representation; both carry properties (e.g. ownership start/end dates). Datasets are categorised (company registries, procurement, court archives, gazettes, land registry…). Cross-referencing needs clean keys (one property per column, consistent formats, IDs where possible).

### 2.5 Logs and the evidence record — Bellingcat and Tactical Tech
- **Search log / research notes**: every query, engine, variation, and the reason for a non-obvious search; logged failures too.
- **Evidence sheet**: ID, date created, file, storage link, type, location (and coordinates), source name, description, notes on weaknesses.
- **Chain of custody**: who collected, who touched, where stored; earliest original source recorded; later better copies kept too.
- **Incident/assessment report**: key findings, descriptive-content summary with the searches behind it, verified analysis, timeline without filled gaps, unexplained items listed, each party's statement, conclusion, further action.

### 2.6 A "lead" in these practices *(inference, assembled from the sources)*
None of the sources defines a lead formally. In use it is: a name, document, reference or anomaly found in one asset that points to another asset or source to obtain (SBI: "follow references or allusions in that material"; every interview ends by asking for the next person; Documenters' questions; Bellingcat's "Further Action" and "stub" incidents). Leads are recorded in the log at the end of each session so work can resume (Bellingcat "Logging Off").

## 3. What fails, and what practitioners warn against

| failure | warning | source |
|---|---|---|
| Pursuing a subject, not a story | "you may become expert in the subject, but a lot of time, money and energy will be wasted" | SBI ch. 2 |
| Proving the hypothesis at any cost | leave out the disproving facts and "you will join the ranks of the world's professional liars"; "Bad journalists try to make the facts fit their hypothesis" | SBI ch. 2 |
| Not accepting what was found | "More investigations are sabotaged by reporters who can't accept the truth of what they've found than by targets"; self-sabotaging endings that give a well-placed source the last word | SBI ch. 2, 6 |
| Filling gaps with speculation | the mind fills holes ("it must have been like this"); say you are speculating or say you do not know | SBI ch. 7; Bellingcat timeline rule |
| Single indispensable source | defining your progress as dependent on one source "place[s] all power in the hands of the source" | SBI ch. 3 |
| Disorganisation | lost clips, a briefcase in a taxi, a year searching for proof already in the files; without a master file "you will need twice as much time" | SBI ch. 5 |
| Facts without a source | "If no source can be found, the passage must be cut"; checkers have "never checked a story that had no mistakes" | SBI ch. 7 |
| Attacking without right of reply | "Never, ever, ever attack someone in a story without offering them the chance to reply"; target's explanation sometimes shows the hypothesis wrong | SBI ch. 7 |
| Trusting data as given | blanks, duplicates, stale statuses, undecoded codes; no analysis replaces field verification | Segnini, Verification Handbook ch. 5 |
| Confirmation bias, sunk cost, anchoring, availability | worst for lone citizen investigators with emotional stakes and no review | Tactical Tech, Evaluating Evidence |
| Treating official data as decisive | government information alone "should rarely be considered strong enough to contradict your hypothesis on its own" | Tactical Tech |
| Mixing hearsay with fact | a caption is evidence the claim was made, not that it is true | Bellingcat Annex V |
| Unpreserved web evidence | sites are taken down on publication day; archive before you rely on it | SBI ch. 7; Tactical Tech fact-checking |
| False precision in confidence | no numeric confidence grades; use calibrated words, but state certainty where logic gives it | Bellingcat VII |
| Unverified crowd data presented as fact | decide in advance how much verification is "enough"; mark unverified; beware coordinated manipulation | Tactical Tech, Crowdsourcing |
| Heavy process where speed matters | the court-grade SOP "may not be compatible with organisations whose objective it is to get through a lot of data quite quickly"; lighter methods for volunteer coalitions | Bellingcat Foreword |
| Publishing into silence | "the isolated reporter is always going to lose"; impact needs allies and promotion | SBI ch. 8 |

## 4. Lessons for a member-run, AI-assisted, evidence-graded civic investigation system

Each lesson is tied to its source; how it maps onto the product is for the A units. Where a lesson goes beyond the source it is marked *(inference)*.

1. **Start from the promise, stated as a testable hypothesis.** "Most investigations are about the difference between a promise and the reality" (SBI ch. 2). The engine's first act on Bob's case should be to locate the dated documents that made the promise (bond measure, board resolution, contract completion date, schedule) and frame "completion within one school year" as the hypothesis to test *(inference)*.
2. **Treat each official explanation as a hypothesis too, and keep competing explanations side by side.** SBI's Contaminated Blood method; Tactical Tech's consistency matrix; Bellingcat's duty to pursue lines that "explain … or suggest alternative attribution". For construction delay the standing set is listed in §1.5. A benign explanation that the evidence supports is a legitimate answer (Tactical Tech's storm example) *(the list is inference from the cases)*.
3. **Decompose the hypothesis into terms → questions → document tasks, and do the quiet documentary work first.** SBI ch. 2–3. This is a natural planning structure for an AI planner: each question names the kind of document that would answer it *(inference)*.
4. **State outcomes in advance and review weekly.** SBI's worst/minimum/maximum bracket and weekly progress review map onto member-visible checkpoints in a long investigation *(inference)*.
5. **One chronological spine.** SBI's master file is ordered by time with actors placed where they first appear; Bellingcat's timeline forbids filling gaps; reporters on delayed projects build baseline-vs-current from dated documents. Time is the primary axis of a delay investigation *(inference)*.
6. **Every fact carries its source, its location and its attribution status; no source, no claim.** SBI ch. 5 and 7; OCCRP "proof of everything"; Der Spiegel per-fact marks. For an AI system this is the citation-grounding rule: an extracted fact without a pinpoint source is not admitted *(inference)*.
7. **Grade evidence relative to the claim, not by source alone.** Tactical Tech shows the same source is weak for one claim and strong for its opposite (admissions against interest), and direct/indirect depends on the claim. A grading scheme keyed only to source type will mis-grade *(inference from the source's tables)*.
8. **Separate what a document says from what is true.** Bellingcat's hearsay rule and examinable vs descriptive content: a status report proves that the district *reported* a date, not that the date was real *(inference applied to the school case)*.
9. **Use calibrated language, not numeric confidence, and say "not established" plainly.** Bellingcat's language of confidence; SBI "acknowledge what you do not know"; PolitiFact's interim ratings (*In the Works*, *Stalled*) show that "not yet determinable" is a first-class state for promises.
10. **Log the process, including failures and dead ends.** Bellingcat search logs and disclosure duty; Tactical Tech "Without documentation, it didn't happen". A refused records request or a missing report is itself a finding (SBI ch. 3) and a reason to try another route, not an end.
11. **Preserve before relying, and record the earliest original.** Bellingcat (preservation separate from analysis; Original Online Source); SBI and Tactical Tech on archiving web pages.
12. **Know the document before reading it.** SBI: "Obtaining a document is not the same thing as understanding it" — find an interpreter of its language; Segnini: decode the codes and acronyms. This is the practitioner basis for Bob's "why is this document in the case file?" reading *(inference)*.
13. **Data needs its own verification pass.** Completeness, duplicates, sample-check against source documents, integrity, codes; then ground-truth (Segnini). Applies to procurement portals, capital-project dashboards and bond reports.
14. **Map actors and relations early, and route around blocked sources.** SBI source map; FollowTheMoney's typed entities and relationships (contract award, project participant, directorship, ownership) give a ready vocabulary for buyer–contractor–official relations.
15. **Right of reply is part of the method, not a courtesy.** SBI ch. 7: inform each criticised party of each criticism, give reasonable time, document the attempt; contact targets early because their answer can overturn the hypothesis. A member-facing answer that blames a district or contractor should show that party's stated explanation *(inference)*.
16. **Bias controls matter most for lone, invested investigators — which members are.** Tactical Tech names citizen investigators as most exposed; remedies are second review, revisiting after time, and active disproof. An AI assistant can supply the disproof pass and the second opinion, but cannot replace a human decision to accept (Bellingcat's separate review) *(inference)*.
17. **Light-touch method for volunteers.** Bellingcat says its court-grade SOP is too heavy for volunteer coalitions and fast throughput; the system should keep provenance and logs automatic so members are not burdened *(inference)*.
18. **Members as documenters and crowd contributors need structure and feedback.** City Bureau's notes-with-questions form; Tactical Tech: structured call-outs, a verification threshold set in advance, unverified items marked, and closing the loop so contributors see results.
19. **An answer has a closer and a next step.** SBI: no invented endings; say who must act. Bellingcat: "Further Action". Chalkbeat's parents asked for a dashboard and hearings. The answer to a member should end with what is established, what is not, and the actions open to them *(inference)*.
20. **Impact needs allies.** SBI ch. 8 (*The Journalism of Outrage*): share findings with friendly sources, officials, citizen groups; give colleagues the key documents. A member's finished investigation should be easy to hand to others with its evidence *(inference)*.
