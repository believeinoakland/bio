## 1. How practitioners work, step by step

### 1.1 Story-Based Inquiry (SBI): the hypothesis method

Source: Hunter et al., *Story-Based Inquiry: A Manual for Investigative Journalists*, UNESCO 2011 (SBI below), read whole: https://bird.tools/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/SBI_Manual.pdf (UNESCO record https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000193078_eng). Second edition announced https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/new-edition-story-based-inquiry-global-reference-investigative-journalists.

**The process (SBI ch. 1, repeated as the frame of every chapter):** discover a subject → create a hypothesis to verify → seek open-source data to verify it → seek human sources → organise the data as it is collected → put it in narrative order and compose → quality control → publish, promote and defend. Organising is not a phase after research: "You do not do research, then organise, then write. Instead, you organise as you research" (ch. 5).

**Subject vs story.** "Corruption … is a subject. It is not a story." A hypothesis is a specific, falsifiable story ("Corruption in the school system has destroyed parents' hopes…") (ch. 2). Pursuing a subject wastes time; pursuing a story focuses it.

**Hypothesis rules (ch. 2):**
| rule | content |
|---|---|
| Length | at most three sentences: "if it is longer than that you probably don't understand it yourself" |
| Shape | news (present problem) → cause (past) → what must change (future); "It contains news, a cause, and a solution" |
| Decomposition | split the hypothesis into its terms; each term generates specific questions; verify each term separately — "if the entire hypothesis can't be confirmed, its separate terms can nonetheless be verified" |
| Precision | "If you use the word 'house' … is it a villa, or a penthouse, or a shack?" — the more precise, the easier to verify |
| Objectivity | "If the facts say the hypothesis is wrong, we change the hypothesis. We do not try to make the facts disappear." "Try to disprove them as well as prove them." |
| Revision | neither cling to a mistaken hypothesis "nor leap in a new direction at the first contrary fact"; the warning sign is "a fair amount of information, but it doesn't make sense" |
| Subsidiary hypotheses | depth research yields new angles that "require new hypotheses that can be verified in turn"; unrelated ones may be parked |
| Unverifiable | "A hypothesis that can't be verified in whole or part is mere speculation" |

**Promise vs reality — the official version as hypothesis (ch. 2).** "Most investigations are about the difference between a promise and the reality of whether or not it was kept. Thus the official promise often serves as a hypothesis, and verification shows whether or not the promise has been kept." The Contaminated Blood case: the agency head's three-part explanation was taken as a hypothesis and each part tested against the literature; none held. This is directly Bob's school case: "reconstruction would take no longer than 1 school year" is the official promise to test *(inference)*.

**Outcome bracketing (ch. 2).** A hypothesis lets the investigator state in advance a worst case (quick disproof, project ends cheaply), a minimum (hypothesis verified), and a maximum (follow-on stories). The Baby Doe case shows the boss's hypothesis disproved and replaced by a better, provable one — a disproved hypothesis is a result, not a failure.

**Strategy and order (ch. 2–3).** Start with the easiest questions, answerable from documents without talking to anyone, because open-source work "makes no noise"; the Center for Public Integrity required "several weeks of research before they are allowed to call sources." Open sources (est. ~90% of what is called "secret") let the investigator ask "This is what happened, isn't it?" instead of "What happened?" — confirmation from a position of strength. If none of the hypothesis's elements are supported in open sources, "either your hypothesis is seriously mistaken, or someone is working very hard to conceal the story" (ch. 3).

**Managing an investigation as a project (ch. 2).** Once a hypothesis appears valid, set: deliverables (minimum and maximum), process milestones, costs and rewards, promotion; hold a weekly review of progress where "verification of the hypothesis and discovery of new information are the first concerns" but time and cost also count; "Delays which threaten the future of the project must not be tolerated."

**Backgrounding (ch. 3).** Map the subject: key actors (individuals and institutions), key issues, key dates and events. Follow references from document to document. When blocked, "take note of the obstacle, and seek information about something related to it instead"; never let one source become indispensable. Move from general sources (press) to expert sources (the original report). Ask professionals which sources they use: "Don't just collect facts: Collect the methods by which the facts are found." Obtaining a document is not understanding it: find an expert interpreter of its language with no conflict of interest (example: a municipal subsidies report read "line by line with a former municipal employee … who worked on budgetary matters"). Kinds of open source named: incident reports, inspection reports ("If there's no report, that's a story"), complaints, court records, cadaster/property records, company registers and filings, annual reports, international audits, government libraries.

**Human sources (ch. 4).** Source map: a graphic of all actors and their relations, built early, so that "if one source is blocked, you can go to another source who can see past the obstacle." Assess each source on three axes (SVT/British intelligence diagram): *motivation* (comprehensible and convincing?), *access* (is the source in a position to know? first-hand?), *quality* (documents? fits other data? something to hide? correct before?). Mid-rank operational staff are the best sources. Attribution states: off the record, not for attribution, on the record. Anonymous material only if documentary evidence exists elsewhere, it fits a pattern of verified information, or the source has been credible before; "demand the document" rather than accept a quoted extract. Every meeting ends by asking for the next source. Keep sources informed and involved over time; review notes immediately after each interview.

**Organisation (ch. 5)** — the case-file model, detailed in §2 below.

**Writing (ch. 6).** The verified hypothesis becomes the "nut graf": a few sentences stating the essence. Structure: present → past → future (why should I care; how did it come about; will it ever be over, how?). "The facts do not tell the story. The story tells the facts." Endings: journalists "do not have the right to invent" a resolution; compose a "closer" that marks where the narrative stops and points to who has responsibility to act.

**Quality control (ch. 7).** Four components: (1) the facts add up to a larger truth — "If an alternative explanation makes more sense than yours, something is wrong"; (2) a known source for every factual assertion; (3) correct the errors found; (4) remove emotional noise. Procedure: two people; the checker asks of every fact "How do you know that?"; "If there is no source, the author has to find one. If no source can be found, the passage must be cut." Interpretations of a target's motives are cut unless documented. Mark the gap-filling the mind does ("it must have been like this"): "Either tell the reader that you are speculating, or acknowledge what you do not know." Right of reply: every criticised party informed of every criticism, given reasonable time, the right person responding, and the reporter's efforts to get a response documented. Contact targets "early and often", because their explanation sometimes shows the hypothesis to be wrong. Deborah Nelson's "transparent approach": tell each actor what you are finding and ask for comment at every stage. A footnoted version of the story, each footnote rechecked against the source document; download web pages before publication because they get taken down.

**Publication (ch. 8).** Results depend on coalitions and allies (citing Protess et al., *The Journalism of Outrage*): notify friendly sources, legislators, NGOs, citizen groups; give colleagues the key documents; anticipate counter-attacks from the targets' official responses so far and prepare follow-ups.

### 1.2 Open-source investigation and verification (Bellingcat, Berkeley Protocol lineage)

Primary source: Bellingcat and Global Legal Action Network, *Justice and Accountability Unit Methodology for Online Open Source Investigations* (Dec 2022), read whole except two annexes outside scope: https://www.bellingcat.com/app/uploads/2022/12/JA-Manual-for-PUBLICATION.pdf. It is a set of standard operating procedures that implements the Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open Source Investigations (OHCHR/UC Berkeley, 2020) for court-grade work; it states that it "may not be compatible with organisations whose objective it is to get through a lot of data quite quickly" and that "a more light-touch methodology could be considered" for volunteer coalitions.

**The workflow (sections IV–VII):**
1. *Preparation:* dedicated device, VPN, passive research accounts; never contact a source or interact with an account without the lead investigator's authorisation.
2. *Investigation folder per incident*, named by incident ID, holding: an **Incident Assessment** (living document, from a fixed template), **Research Notes** per investigator (a search log: every search term, engine, variation and "if you search for something that is not obvious, make a note of why"), a **Links sheet**, captured web pages (Hunchly records every page visited), media, misc.
3. *Discovery:* work from a **Table of Factual Inquiries** (the questions that matter, derived from the legal elements); "actively pursue alternative possibilities"; pursue "all lines of reasonable enquiry including those leading towards evidence that may explain the attack or suggest alternative attribution".
4. *Preservation:* every relevant link goes to a preservation sheet (an external partner, Mnemonic, archives it immutably); the analysis database (Uwazi) "does not preserve content" — analysis and preservation are separate systems.
5. *Earliest source:* always find the "Original Online Source"; cite the earliest poster even if you found a later copy; if an earlier version turns up later, edit the report and note it.
6. *Verification & analysis:* geolocation, chronolocation, corroboration; verification notes "are not 'evidence' in themselves, rather they are a description of the significance of the evidence".
7. *Logging off:* save links and leads, hand links to preservation, upload content, so the next session "can pick back up where you left off".
8. *Review:* first-instance investigators, then reviewing investigators and a separate legal review.

**Two categories of material (section III):** *examinable* content (an item the investigator can interrogate: a video, a satellite image, a flight log) vs *descriptive* content (an account of events — a news article, an NGO's witness statements — "not necessarily more or less reliable", but not something the investigator's techniques can test). Descriptive content is summarised; examinable content is verified.

**Language of confidence, not scores:** "We will not attribute formal confidence gradings or degrees of likelihood to any findings"; use "appears to be", "suggests", "indicates", "strongly indicates" — but do not over-hedge where a fact is certain by logic ("must have taken place on or before 12 March" if posted on 12 March). Casualty figures from others are recorded as descriptive content with no comment on their veracity unless independently verifiable.

**Incident Assessment template (Annex VIII):** ID; location; date *prima facie* (claimed) vs date *assessed*; 2–4 key findings; background summary of descriptive content with the searches that found it; analysis of examinable content including a **timeline** ("Don't fill in gaps if you are unsure, simply report and cite the events … in the order that the evidence indicates"); "If you cannot explain something that happened, it is just as important to note it"; statements from each party; a short factual conclusion; **Further Action** (follow-ups, linked incidents).

**Database fields (Annex IX, Uwazi):** three entity types — *Incident* (ID, prima-facie date, assessed date, description, **level of investigation: detailed or stub**, cautions flag for content shown to be bogus, investigator responsible, incident group, stage of investigation), *Content* (title, original post text, generated ID, link(s) to incident(s), live link and archived link, posted date/time, filmed date, geolocation, source actor), *Actor* (name as displayed, description including "affiliations, credibility and length of presence online", multiple profiles merged into one actor). "Stub" incidents are logged for every related allegation "even if they remain allegations" so patterns can be grouped.

**Bias controls (Annex I):** begin "with no settled hypothesis as to what happened or what party was responsible"; search all sides' platforms; equal verification effort on content from either side; a list of questions so "no relevant lines of inquiry are skipped due to bias"; separate review. Algorithmic bias in search engines is mitigated by many varied queries in several languages, not by tricks.

**Evidence principles (Annex V):** challenges come on *impartiality*, *authenticity*, *reliability*; hearsay attached to a video ("school full of children bombed") is evidence that the statement was made, not that it is true; disclosure duty means records of the investigation itself (search logs, draft changes, a conversation agreeing to ignore contrary information) may have to be produced, so the process record is part of the evidence. Neutral language, "free from legal or moral commentary"; write-ups "comprehensive enough so that you or another person could retrace your steps".

**Corruption research by open sources** (Toler, Bellingcat, 2016, https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2016/09/05/how-to-uncover-corruption-using-open-source-research/): compare visible assets to declared income; follow relatives' social media; above all "check public records" — tax declarations, business registrations, land deeds, court documents — via aggregators such as OCCRP's Investigative Dashboard and national registries (UK Companies House); money-laundering indicators (companies with no clear commercial purpose, pass-through flows, abnormal prices, disproportionate agent fees, unnecessary complexity). Limits: open sources raise questions; discrepancies are not proof.

**Verification Handbook for Investigative Reporting** (ed. Silverman, EJC; https://datajournalism.com/read/handbook/verification-2):
- *Data quality* (Segnini, ch. 5): "data lies — just as much as people"; on receipt, check completeness (extremes and blanks — 53% of World Bank projects had zero cost listed), duplicates (count records per ID), accuracy (sample records against source documents), integrity (projects from 1986 still "active"), and decode the codes and acronyms (they can be the story); after analysis, ground-truth: "Not even the best data analysis can replace on-the-ground journalism and field verification".
- *Companies* (Sharife, ch. 4): "Everything has a paper trail"; registry filings (shareholders, directors, dates), court records, land documents, domain records; cross-reference; in-country verification.
- *Newsroom fact-checking* (Janssen on Der Spiegel's documentation department, ch. 10): every proof is marked fact by fact with standard symbols (correct / incorrect / unverifiable); priorities under time pressure: dates and times, internal contradictions, names and titles, quotations in context, currency of sources; prefer original documents over summaries. One studied issue produced 1,153 corrections.

### 1.3 Citizen and community investigation guides

Primary source: Tactical Tech, *Exposing the Invisible: The Kit* (https://kit.exposingtheinvisible.org/en/), written for citizen investigators without a newsroom behind them. Chapters read whole: "What Makes an Investigation", "Evaluating Evidence and Information Sources" (Rautner, 2020), "How to Manage Your Sources", "Crowdsourcing Evidence for Investigations" (Bohdanova, 2024), "Get Your Facts Straight: The Basics of Fact-Checking" (Adhikari, 2021).

- **Evidence defined generically:** "information that is material to the question … you are investigating"; "not every piece of information is, or can turn into, evidence."
- **A checklist for good evidence** (14 tests): first-hand; documented and preserved; timely; verifiable; origin confirmable by a third party; content confirmable by a third party; shareable; unbroken chain of custody; uncorrupted; carries metadata; connects to other evidence; shows where the information is weak; does not expose sources to unmanageable risk; may contradict your preconceptions. "Not every piece of evidence … will meet all these conditions … some … will meet a lot of them – those pieces are the ones you may want to prioritise."
- **"Without documentation, it didn't happen":** a log of every step (date, tool, outcome, including failures — "I tried calling … but I was diverted to voicemail"), contact records with how each contact was found, a rolling narrative outline that shows what is missing, and an evidence log with storage location, status, who collected it and who touched it. Documentation also lets someone else take over the investigation if the original investigator stops.
- **A ladder of evidence strength:** word of mouth → expert statement → second-hand accounts → research reports → official documents (strong, but "mind institutional bias") → photos/video/audio → first-hand observation.
- **"If it's not verified, it's not valid";** verify always and iteratively, because new evidence "may cast reasonable doubt on old evidence that you may have already verified"; three phases: verify the source, the content, its relevance.
- **Working hypothesis** (citing SBI): changes with the evidence; "it is extremely rare for any investigation to provide 100% proof"; the realistic result is "an approximation of proof where (after looking at all possible explanations) your hypothesis is the most likely explanation". At a dead end: give up, change the hypothesis, or "publish the information you have in a transparent way without making claims for which you do not have any evidence".
- **Direct vs indirect evidence is relative to the claim:** a spokesperson's denial is indirect evidence that the company did not pollute but direct evidence that the company denies it. Even direct evidence may prove only one component of the hypothesis (a lab report proves pollution, not who polluted).
- **Source strength depends on the claim, not the source alone:** the same company-commissioned study is "medium" evidence for "Company X is not polluting" but "strong" for "Company X is polluting" (an admission against interest). Official data alone "should rarely be considered strong enough to contradict your hypothesis on its own."
- **Bias:** confirmation bias, sunk-cost effect, availability, anchoring; especially dangerous for citizen investigators who "often work alone … have personal or emotional attachments to the outcome … and can lack review processes". Remedies: second opinions, revisit conclusions after time has passed, try to disprove. Competing hypotheses in a consistency matrix (ACH, "common sense is most likely sufficient" except for long, complex cases); the example resolves to a benign explanation (storm-driven oxygen loss) that ends the investigation.
- **Evidence sheet fields:** ID (typed prefix), date created, file name, storage link, type, latitude/longitude, location, source name, description, notes (concerns about accuracy or intentions).
- **Mitigation table:** for each piece of evidence, the risk to it and the action that reduces the risk (e.g. a council water test with no toxicity: "Samples may have been taken at a different time or at locations that would not capture pollution … Request the full tests over a few years").
- **Publishing:** describe methods; mark unverified material as unverified; publish data in reusable formats; protect confidential sources.
- **Crowdsourcing** (structured vs unstructured call-outs; "decide how much verification you deem 'enough'" before collecting; vet unverifiable reports for plausibility of time and place; "Clearly mark 'unverified' data"; close the feedback loop with contributors, who will not contribute if they see no result).
- **Fact-checking:** archive the claim first; quote the claim verbatim and check it part by part; give the process so anyone can replicate it; graded verdicts (false, misleading, half-true …) rather than a binary; "you cannot fact-check people's intentions, nor … predictions"; correct errors openly. OCCRP's fact-checking standard: "proof of everything … recorded interviews, official documents, written notes, links".
