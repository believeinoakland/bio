## 1. How practitioners work, step by step

### 1.1 Police major-case investigation (UK MIRSAP/HOLMES; Canadian MCM; Australian AFP)

**The core loop.** Every police doctrine read describes the same cycle; the clearest statement is Oldfield's "idealised model of the investigative decision-making process" reproduced in the *Murder Investigation Manual* (MIM 2006 §2.3.3.6, Fig. 12):

| step | the question the investigator asks |
|---|---|
| 1 | What does the investigation now know? (assess available information with specialist advisers) |
| 2 | What does this tell you about what (might have) happened? "If it is not absolutely clear what occurred, develop scenarios as to what happened, why it happened and who committed the crime. Evaluate scenarios against available information ... to identify the most plausible explanation(s)." |
| 3 | What else does the investigation need to know? |
| 4 | Where is the required information most likely to reside? |
| 5 | How can the investigation best get hold of it? (strategic and tactical approaches) |
| 6 | Initiate and monitor actions; how is the investigation getting on? then back to 1 |
| exit | "If no viable lines of enquiry are left: enter *Investigative Maintenance*" (dormant, re-opened when a new viable line appears) |

The IIO file-review checklist restates it as questions a panel asks at each review: "What facts are now established? What does the investigation know? What does the investigation need to know? What resources are necessary to achieve the missing information? Where is the evidence known to be or potentially? How can the investigation achieve corroboration of the evidence?" (IIO MOI ch. 5, *Key Issues at File Reviews*).

**Named stages.** The AFP doctrine gives the lifecycle as *Initiation → Evaluation → Planning → Investigative activity → Outcome → Finalisation*, with reviews throughout (AFP Investigations Doctrine ch. 4). Initiation paths are response, report, intelligence-led, or spun off another investigation. **Evaluation** is an explicit go/no-go: continue, refer to a partner, record without further investigation, or reject, and "regardless of the result of the evaluation, decisions should be recorded ... the method of initiation, the decision taken and the context and reasons". **Finalisation** expressly allows re-opening "on the basis of new information"; "the investigative records will be the starting point for the new investigation".

**The investigative mindset.** UK doctrine (ACPO Core Investigative Doctrine 2005) names five principles: understanding the source of material; planning and preparation; examination; recording and collation; evaluation *(search snippet of the 2005 doctrine; the PDF itself was unreachable)*. The College's 2023 guidelines define the mindset as "a disciplined approach to investigations that ensures the decisions made are appropriate to the case, are reasonable and can be explained to others" and extend it to: knowing one's role, open-mindedness and *professional curiosity*, following "all reasonable lines of enquiry", proportionality, awareness of bias (College, *Conducting effective investigations* 2023, Introduction). Professional curiosity is operationalised as **5WH** (who, where, what, when, why, how) and **ABC** ("Assume nothing, Believe nothing, Challenge everything"), and includes "looking for, and considering, alternative explanations", "identifying gaps in knowledge or understanding and seeking to fill them", "seeking corroboration and testing material" (ibid., Guideline 1).

**Investigate towards and away.** The legal duty (UK CPIA 1996) is to "identify and follow all reasonable lines of enquiry ... and, where a suspect is identified, investigate towards and away from the suspect" (College 2023, Guideline 1). The AFP says the same as "explore possible sources of inculpatory and exculpatory evidence to challenge the strength of any investigative hypotheses" (AFP ch. 4, *Investigative Activity*).

**Hypotheses.** MIM §2.3.3.5: hypotheses bridge gaps the material cannot fill; build them only after "a thorough understanding of the relevance and reliability of all material", with "a clear objective for the hypothesis", "best fit" to known material, consulting colleagues and experts, proportionate to seriousness; "Hypotheses must not be based on 'flights of fancy' ... Where assumptions are used to develop hypotheses this should be made explicit." The College adds a bias countermeasure with evidence behind it: instruct investigators to "'consider the opposite' during the identification and formulation of hypotheses", which "may increase the numbers of hypotheses generated" (College 2023, Guideline 2).

**Strategy → lines of enquiry → actions.** This is the planning hierarchy of a UK major investigation (MIM §§2.3.4–2.3.5):
- **Investigative strategy**: a set of named strategy *elements* (scene, forensic, witness, suspect, elimination, media, community, passive data, house-to-house, search, covert, ...). Each element is developed the same way: *advice* (the right experts), *material* (what is known about it), *objectives* ("a clear statement of the objectives"; vague orders like "carry out house-to-house" are "meaningless" and mean "the team is setting the investigative strategy, not the SIO"), *methods and resources*, *responsibility* (a named owner), *review* (continuous by the owner, formal by the SIO at intervals; daily early on).
- **Line of enquiry**: "a series of actions that have a particular focus, for example, tracing a vehicle described as leaving the scene". A **main line of enquiry** "appears to have the potential to lead to the suspect and ... is, therefore, given a higher priority".
- **Action**: "any activity which, if pursued, is likely to establish significant facts, preserve material or lead to the resolution of the investigation". Before raising one weigh: likelihood it finds the material, achievability (break "locate the offender" into small achievable actions), probable size (including the volume of material it will generate for the incident room to process), resources, and cost including downstream cost. "No activity should be undertaken by investigators unless tasked by an action." The SIO quality-assures by dip-sampling actions.
- **Fast-track actions**: actions which "if pursued immediately, are likely to establish important facts, preserve evidence or lead to the early resolution"; the "golden hour" principle: material perishes (CCTV is overwritten, memories fade). Balanced by "creating slow time ... putting their foot on the ball" to bring order after the initial rush (MIM §§2.2.4–2.2.5).

**Parameters.** The SIO sets explicit search parameters so everyone knows what material is sought: scenes, vicinity, suspect description, time window ("alibi times": "err on the side of caution and ... choose wider rather than narrower"), and **TIE** (trace / interview / eliminate) categories, the groups in which the offender may be found, each with elimination criteria (MIM §2.3.1; MIM §19 index).

**The incident room (MIRSAP + HOLMES).** MIRSAP was introduced after the Byford report on the Yorkshire Ripper investigation (1981), whose card-index system failed to connect material it already held; HOLMES (1986; HOLMES 2 from 2000–04) computerised it *(search snippets of the College MIRSAP page; Wikipedia, HOLMES 2)*. MIM §2.3.1: SIOs "must" use MIRSAP with HOLMES or the identical-convention Paper System; this "provides a nationally approved ... management system which serves the needs of the investigation and enables independent audit". The discipline: "Ideally, no investigative activity should take place until the material giving rise to it has been processed by the MIR and a formal action has been raised." Document flow and roles (MIRSAP, via search snippets of the 2005/2021 text and job profiles):
- **Receiver**: reads all documents entering the room, checks completion, decides whether fast-track actions are needed, marks content to be indexed; "should be the most up to date on the current state of the enquiry".
- **Action allocator / action manager**: raises, prioritises (action priorities and queues, MIRSAP §4.3.1), allocates actions, monitors high-priority ones, and ensures resulted actions are "finalised satisfactorily".
- **Statement / document reader**: an experienced investigator who reads every statement and document in detail, summarises, marks text for indexing, and raises further actions from what it says.
- **Indexer**: indexes marked content into HOLMES entities (nominals, locations, vehicles, telephones, events, categories) and cross-references them, so the same person mentioned in two documents is one record.
- **Office manager**: runs the room; **analyst**: timelines, network charts, gap analysis.
- Document types: messages (incoming information), actions (with results), statements, officer's reports, other documents, exhibits *(general knowledge of MIRSAP; consistent with the snippets)*.

The resulting cycle, document in → read for meaning → index entities → raise actions → allocate → result returns as a new document, is the incident-room's engine. Every action has a source document and every document is either the result of an action or an incoming message, so the record is closed and auditable.

**Canadian Major Case Management.** Ontario's MCM arose from the Campbell review of the Bernardo investigations (1996), where several forces held linked information without connecting it; it became mandatory under the Police Services Act in 2005 with provincially supplied case-management software (PowerCase) "to organize, manage, retrieve and analyze large volumes of investigative data" (Public Safety Canada synopsis). The **command triangle** is its core: a case manager (in the IIO: *Team Director*: governance, resources, accountability), a **Primary Investigator** ("responsible for the speed, flow and direction of the investigation": strategy, identifying, prioritising and delegating tasks, briefings, documenting decisions) and a **File Coordinator** ("responsible for managing all the materials and information gathered, located and generated": information management, "assigning, tracking and managing tasks", documenting briefings, disclosure packages, quality control). In a small case one person holds several roles "but the principles are still maintained" (IIO MOI chs. 2–3). Nine principles: command triangle, communication, leadership and teams, management considerations, investigative strategies (including "identifying and addressing evidentiary gaps" and "gap analysis"), accountability mechanisms, ethics, legal considerations, partnerships (IIO MOI ch. 2 and App. 1).

**Briefings.** "The heart and soul of any investigation": tasking happens at briefings; each member accounts for actions under "where we have been, where we are, where we are going"; minutes are taken, reviewed and adopted by those present, and filed (IIO MOI ch. 7; MIM §2.3.5.5, which also asks the SIO to display the Policy File and analytical charts at briefings).

**Decisions recorded with rationale.** UK: the SIO's **Policy File**, "sequentially numbered bound books", each entry signed and dated, recording "strategic policy decisions", "not an action book"; "the definitive record upon which they will rely when subsequently asked to account for decisions"; "always attempt to look forward and anticipate likely future challenges" (MIM §2.5.1). Canada: the **Decision Log** must record "the circumstances surrounding the decision (what was known? when was it known?)", what was decided, when, who (all involved), and the rationale; decisions to log include "the focus of the investigation" and "why an avenue of potential investigation was/was not followed". Kept separately from the **Investigational Log** (a "concise chronological record" of significant events, used to brief newcomers and to draft the final report) (IIO MOI ch. 8). AFP: "A decision should be judged on the quality of the decision-making, not on the outcome" and every decision is recorded "in a manner that allows later review" (AFP ch. 3 and ch. 5).

**Reviews.** Self, peer, supervisory, formal internal, formal external (AFP ch. 4). The UK **major crime review**: commissioned with written terms of reference, by an experienced officer independent of the enquiry, normally from 28 days if undetected and at least every two years thereafter; the reviewer gets the Policy File, sequence of events, main statements, exhibit schedules and situation reports; aims: "no investigative opportunities have been overlooked"; "all-embracing" audits of the whole account "should generally be avoided"; the SIO sees and may answer the report (MIM §4). For low-information cases MIM warns: "avoid issuing low-quality actions simply as a way of doing something" and ask *why* information is low (planned to minimise traces? witnesses afraid? material missed or misinterpreted?) (MIM §2.6.1).

**Handover.** A new SIO must "fully assimilate all the material": circumstances, how it began, initial actions, the previous SIO's evaluation and strategy, what the strategy gathered, the people, the problems, by reading policy files and "interrogating the HOLMES account"; allow ample time (MIM §2.6.4).

**Investigative evaluation (College APP, *Investigation process*, Dec 2022, §5).** The College's own APP text (consultation PDF, read pp. 1–38) gives the standard evaluation model, to be repeated "several times throughout an investigation":
1. *Set objectives* for this evaluation (broad early: has a crime been committed, who, where, what material can be gathered; narrow later: does this person fall within the parameters? available in the time window? physical, forensic, passive-data, identification, intelligence, behavioural links?).
2. *Filter* each item for **relevance** ("a wide view should be taken of relevance"; exclude only after careful consideration, because "a decision to exclude as irrelevant may later be called into question"), **reliability** (independent corroboration raises it; corroboration only by a partner or associate lowers it; uncorroborated and conflicting lowers it; "should not rely solely on a single account"; never judge reliability by the victim's or suspect's lifestyle) and **admissibility**.
3. *Organise* the knowledge in a **gap-analysis matrix**: rows who / what / when / where / why / how; columns *what is known*, *what is not known*, *conflicts*, *consistencies*.
4. *Build hypotheses*: "a supposition or proposed explanation based on limited evidence as a starting point for further investigation"; first ask whether enough material has been gathered and understood, and whether unpursued lines remain. "Developing alternative hypotheses from the same material may identify further lines of enquiry [and] test alternative interpretations." Worked example: stolen jewellery not found at the fingerprinted suspect's home gives six hypotheses (sold, hidden, destroyed, held by accomplice, victim exaggerating, suspect innocent and print legitimate), each generating lines of enquiry until one is confirmed and the rest discounted (§5.4).
5. *Decision support*: self-review, peer review, expert review, formal review.

The same APP states the record-keeping and disclosure discipline as **record, retain, review, reveal** (§3.4) and closes with the standard for a finished investigation: investigators "should expect to be able to explain all the circumstances of the case, what they did – and did not do – and why ... A fair investigation does not mean an endless investigation. Investigators must be able to define and explain the scope of their investigation", with "no unresolved lines of enquiry within the case file" (§9). It also states that the MIRSAP principles of case and document management "should be applied to all case development" (§9). Victims and, since Operation Kentia, suspects get an expected finish date and **28-day progress updates** (§8.4).

### 1.2 Litigation case files, matter management and eDiscovery

**Matter management** *(general knowledge)*. A law firm opens a *matter* at intake (client, adverse parties, conflict check, engagement terms, matter number); the matter is the container for everything after: pleadings, correspondence, the document collection, the docket of deadlines computed from court rules, tasks, time and budget. The "case file" is thus two things: the administrative matter record and the evidential collection, which in modern practice lives in a review platform.

**The EDRM.** The Electronic Discovery Reference Model is the profession's standard map of handling a document collection (EDRM 2.0, edrm.net):

| stage | EDRM definition (quoted) |
|---|---|
| Information governance | the foundation: "effective discovery begins with strong information governance practices" |
| Identification | "Determining the scope of the matter and relevant ESI and document sources" |
| Preservation | "Safeguarding relevant ESI and documents against alteration or destruction" |
| Collection | "Retrieving potentially relevant ESI and documents" |
| Processing | "Normalizing and converting ESI/documents for review and analysis" |
| Review | "Evaluating ESI/documents for relevance, responsiveness, privilege, confidentiality, privacy, and/or issue significance" |
| Analysis | "Assessing ESI/documents throughout the lifecycle using human insight and/or technology" |
| Production | "Delivering ESI/documents in agreed, defensible, or appropriate formats" |
| Presentation | "Strategically displaying ESI, documents, and demonstratives in legal proceedings" |
| Disposition | "A systematic, defensible process to retain, delete, transfer or return data after use" |

EDRM stresses it "is a conceptual view ... not a literal or linear workflow"; practitioners "repeat them, or circle back to earlier phases as their understanding of the data improves".

**The governing standard is reasonableness, not perfection.** US Federal Rule 26(b)(1) limits discovery to matter "relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case", weighing importance of the issues, amount in controversy, relative access to information, resources, importance of the discovery, and whether burden outweighs benefit; Rule 26(g) requires certification after a "reasonable inquiry" (Cornell LII, FRCP 26). Sedona Principle 6: "Responding parties are best situated to evaluate the procedures, methodologies, and technologies appropriate for preserving and producing their own electronically stored information"; start with the primary, most accessible sources and move to less accessible ones only while reasonable and proportionate *(search snippet of The Sedona Principles, 3rd ed.)*.

**Review in practice** *(general knowledge, consistent with the EDRM TAR Guidelines)*. A review protocol (written by the lead attorney) defines relevance, issue tags, privilege rules, and coding fields; a first-pass team codes each document (responsive / not; issue codes; privilege; confidentiality; "hot"); a second-pass team checks responsive and privileged calls; QC samples the work; key documents are escalated to the trial team. Every coding decision is attributed and time-stamped on the platform.

**Technology-assisted review (EDRM/Duke TAR Guidelines 2019).** TAR is "a review process in which humans work with software ... to train it to identify relevant documents": a human with knowledge of the case codes examples; the software builds a model and ranks or classifies the rest; repeat; then validate. Points that matter here:
- Roles: a **lead attorney** who "fully understands the scope of relevancy" and must make the reviewers and the software review accurately; a **case manager** who tracks what was collected, what survived culling, what was included and *excluded* from the TAR set, and the predicted relevant and non-relevant sets.
- The scope of relevance must be defined before training and the reviewers trained on it; it often shifts later, which costs rework.
- Documents the software cannot read (images, audio, very short or very long text, foreign language) "should be tracked, and if necessary, sent through an alternate review workflow": the exclusions are themselves recorded.
- TAR 1.0 trains then applies a model with a cut-off; TAR 2.0 (continuous active learning) melds review and training, always serving the next most-likely-relevant document. Training seeds can come from human judgment, random sampling or the machine's own feedback; human-selected seeds risk covering only the issues the team already thought of ("if the team's scope of relevancy spans ten ... requests, but the team only finds training-set examples that cover five ... the computer may not identify documents relevant to the other five").
- Stopping and validation: recall (share of truly relevant documents found) and precision, estimated by sampling; "validation of TAR is based on reasonableness and on proportionality considerations: How much could the result be improved by further review and at what cost?" There is "no standard measurement".
- **Privilege review** is where TAR is weakest: privilege turns on legal context, not topic; "the same exact content may be privileged in one document and not in another"; the software "cannot account for the events surrounding the creation or dissemination of a document". Hence human review plus clawback agreements (FRCP 26(b)(5)(B)).
- TAR's other uses map closely onto an investigator's: **early case assessment** (clustering to see what a collection contains and what is *missing* from it), **prioritisation**, **categorisation by issue**, **review of incoming productions** ("prepare key evidence ... developing timelines, assessing case strengths and weaknesses, and understanding witness knowledge"; also to "demonstrate gaps" and challenge a "data dump"), and **witness preparation** (assemble the documents bearing on one person).

**Privilege logs.** A party withholding material must "expressly make the claim" and "describe the nature of the documents ... in a manner that, without revealing information itself privileged or protected, will enable other parties to assess the claim" (FRCP 26(b)(5)(A)). The analogue for public records is the redaction or exemption log a records officer gives with a partial release *(inference)*.

### 1.3 Legal fact investigation (chronologies, cast of characters, issues; CaseMap)

Litigators organise what they learn in three linked lists, a practice that CaseMap (LexisNexis) productised and that its manual recommends starting "when you have the first conversation with a prospective client ... Long before the first box of case documents arrives" (*Using CaseMap* v14, Getting Started):
- **Facts**: "where you build a fact chronology for the case, including rumors, potential facts, and known facts. Each fact is entered as an individual record" and linked to issues, objects and supporting documents. Fields (CaseMap Facts field list): *Date and Time*; *Fact Text*; *Source(s)* ("the definitive sources of the fact and their location"); *Potential Source(s)* (where you hope to get it, e.g. a witness not yet deposed); *Source Quote*; *Status* (undisputed, disputed and by which party, or prospective) with a *Status Description* of why it is disputed; *Key*; *Linked Issues*; counts of linked issues, objects and **open questions**; and authorship (*Creation Author/Scribe*, timestamps, last update).
- **Objects** ("cast of characters"): "the people, organizations, documents, and other things that are important to know about in the case", each with a unique **short name**; typing a short name in any text field links the record, which "is what allows you to search" across the case.
- **Issues**: "an outline of claims and arguments", hierarchical, linked to facts; per-issue counts such as "# Undisputed Facts" linked.
- **Questions**: "brainstorm all questions regarding the case and build an overall 'to do' list"; assign each to a person, track unanswered ones, rank by **Criticality**.
- **Evaluation fields**: each author may keep their own evaluation of a fact or issue (on a weight scale) and an *Evaluation Comparison* shows where team members' judgments diverge.
- Outputs: fact chronology, cast-of-characters, document index, privilege log, and summary-judgment "statements of undisputed material facts" built from the undisputed facts linked to an issue.

Two practice points are built into the structure *(inference from the fields; consistent with practitioner teaching, general knowledge)*: a fact is a *proposition* distinct from the *evidence* that supports it (one fact may have several sources; a source may support many facts), and a fact carries its *status* (who disputes it) rather than being silently accepted.

**Evidence charting.** For hard cases the academic method is Wigmore's chart, revived by Anderson, Schum and Twining (*Analysis of Evidence*, 2nd ed. 2005): a numbered **key list** of propositions and a diagram linking each item of evidence through intermediate inferences to the *penultimate probanda* (the elements to be proved) and the *ultimate probandum*, with contradicting evidence drawn explicitly (search results: Cambridge UP book page; arXiv 2403.16628 compares Wigmore charts, Bayesian networks and chain event graphs and notes they serve lawyers, forensic scientists and police respectively). It is the formal ancestor of "claim → supporting and opposing evidence" structures.

### 1.4 Inspector-general and audit standards

**CIGIE Quality Standards for Investigations (QSI, July 2025; supersedes Nov 2011).** Three *general* standards: **Qualifications** (the team "collectively possess professional proficiency"), **Independence** ("independent and free, both in fact and appearance"; personal, external and organisational impairments listed, including "preconceived opinions", "authority to overrule or influence the extent and thoroughness of the investigative scope", and "denial of access to sources"), and **Professional judgment** (thoroughness, legal compliance, appropriate techniques, impartiality, objectivity, ethics, **timeliness** "defined in objective and measurable ways", and "accurate and complete documentation ... maintained in the case file"). Four *qualitative* standards:
- **Planning**: every complaint is evaluated to one of three decisions ("initiate investigative activity, refer to another appropriate authority, or take no further specific investigative action"); case management considers the nature of the allegation, "planned focus and objectives", the violations and their **elements of proof**, coordination, venue, "pursuing leads in a timely manner", and causative factors to report as control weaknesses. (2025 change: the formal written investigative plan is no longer required.)
- **Executing**: "The investigator is a fact-gatherer and has a duty to be receptive to evidence, including exculpatory and incriminating evidence"; review known information before an interview; record or document every interview; verify the validity of information; assess specialists' impartiality; document activities "accurately and completely" in the case file; **supervisory case reviews at least quarterly**.
- **Reporting**: state facts and the rule allegedly violated; "logically organized, accurate, complete, concise, impartial"; "must contain exculpatory evidence and relevant mitigating information"; "supported by evidence and documentation in the investigative case file"; report systemic weaknesses to management.
- **Managing investigative information**: secure, retrievable, analysable; complaints "tracked to final resolution"; "a decision not to investigate ... must be documented"; the **official case file** must hold "all investigative activity, both exculpatory and inculpatory"; directives for file organisation, evidence, dissemination, access control, retention and protection of sensitive data; a management information system tracking dates, source, category, priority, subjects, techniques used, outcomes and recommendations.
- Appendix A lists the investigator's skills as a lifecycle: receipt and disposition of allegations; assessment, focus and preparation ("determine focus and scope", "identify potential witnesses, suspects, relevant documents"); conduct ("assess progress and re-focus when necessary"); review, organise and evaluate findings ("correlate data, witnesses, and records"); draft, validate, report; post-investigative tasks.

**GAO Government Auditing Standards ("Yellow Book", 2024 revision), performance audits.** The audit *objective* is framed as a question: "Audit objectives can be thought of as questions about the program that the auditors seek to answer based on evidence obtained and assessed against criteria" (§8.08); objectives, scope and methodology may be refined as work proceeds (§8.09); "Planning is a continuous process throughout the audit" (§8.05). Evidence standards (§§8.90–8.115):
- Evidence must be **sufficient** (quantity: "enough ... to persuade a knowledgeable person that the findings are reasonable"; "the greater the audit risk, the greater the quantity and quality of evidence required"; "stronger evidence may allow less evidence") and **appropriate** (quality: **relevance**, **validity** "the extent to which evidence represents what it is purported to represent", **reliability** "consistency ... verifiable or supported"); "a large volume of evidence does not compensate for a lack of relevance, validity, or reliability".
- Evidence is *physical*, *documentary* or *testimonial*, with reliability heuristics: effective internal control > weak; direct observation > indirect; originals > copies; testimony given freely > under intimidation; unbiased, direct-knowledge witness > biased or partial; "a knowledgeable, credible, and unbiased third party is generally more reliable than evidence obtained from management of the audited entity" (§8.104). Testimony and documents corroborate or challenge each other (§8.105).
- Information supplied by the audited entity's officials needs checking: what did they do to assure its reliability; test it or corroborate it; document how conflicting evidence was resolved (§§8.93, 8.96).
- An **overall assessment of the collective evidence** is required and documented; evidence found insufficient "should not [be used] as support"; limitations are met by corroboration, narrowing the objective, *disclosing the limitation in the report*, or reporting the limitation itself as a finding (§§8.108–8.115).
- **Findings** have four elements: **criteria** (the required or expected state: laws, contracts, standards, "expected performance ... benchmarks"), **condition** ("a situation that exists"), **cause** ("the factor or factors responsible for the difference between the condition and the criteria"; the auditor assesses "whether the evidence provides a reasonable and convincing argument for why the stated cause is the key factor"), and **effect** (the consequence of the gap). Only the elements the objective needs are developed: a status question needs only the condition (§§8.116–8.131).
- **Documentation**: detailed enough that "an experienced auditor, having no previous connection to the audit" can understand what was done, the evidence and "its source and the conclusions reached" (§8.132).
- **Views of responsible officials**: auditors "should obtain and report the views of responsible officials of the audited entity concerning the findings, conclusions, and recommendations", including written comments or a verified summary of oral ones (§§9.50–9.51).

| IG (CIGIE QSI) | the **official case file** | lead, pursued "in a timely manner" | focus, objectives, elements of proof (no formal written plan required since 2025) | interview records, evidence log with chain of custody | complaint disposition; documented decision not to investigate; quarterly supervisory reviews | subjects, witnesses, entities in the MIS |
| Performance audit (Yellow Book) | the audit documentation (workpapers) | audit step / procedure | **objectives (questions) + scope + methodology**, criteria | evidence with source and assessment of sufficiency and appropriateness | documented significant judgments; overall assessment of evidence | entity, officials (whose views are sought) |

Common to all of them *(inference across the sources)*: (a) the record is **closed**: every piece of work has a source (an action from a document, a document from an action, a fact from a source) so the whole can be audited and handed over; (b) **facts are separate from evidence** and carry a status or grade; (c) **decisions are a separate log** from activity, written for a future skeptical reader; (d) **entities are indexed once** and linked everywhere; (e) the plan is **hierarchical** (objective → strategy or issue → line → action) and is reviewed on a cadence.

## 3. What failed, and what is warned against

| failure | evidence | the warning |
|---|---|---|
| **Information held but not connected** | Yorkshire Ripper: "the ineffectiveness of the major incident room which became overloaded with unprocessed information"; Sutcliffe was interviewed nine times and his name sat on cards never cross-referenced (Byford report as summarised to Parliament, Hansard HL 19 Jan 1982; search-result summaries). Bernardo: linked information held by separate forces (Public Safety Canada synopsis of MCM origins). | Process every document before acting; index entities once; this is *why* MIRSAP/HOLMES and MCM exist. |
| **Excess credence to one source** | Byford: "excessive credence was given to the letters and tape from a man claiming responsibility" (a hoax), which steered elimination criteria (Hansard 1982). | ABC: assume nothing, believe nothing, challenge everything; reliability needs independent corroboration (College 2023; APP §5.1). |
| **Tunnel vision** | "a tendency ... to focus on a particular theory of a case and to dismiss or undervalue evidence which contradicts that theory"; found in the Marshall, Morin, Milgaard, Sophonow and Driskell inquiries (Public Prosecution Service of Canada, *Innocence at Stake*, ch. 2). The College finds "consistent evidence that an initial hypothesis or initial belief in a suspect's guilt significantly affects interpretation of further evidence" (2023, Guideline 2). | Start from zero; consider the opposite; multiple hypotheses; devil's advocate at briefings; periodic independent review; record why lines were *not* followed (IIO MOI ch. 5; College 2023; PPSC). |
| **Moving too early from deliberation to implementation** | Time pressure and caseload lead to "more cursory processing of information, being less open-minded ..., generating fewer hypotheses, not pursuing all lines of enquiry and moving earlier from a deliberative to an implemental mindset" (College 2023, Guideline 5, citing Gollwitzer). | Plan as a continuous activity; formal reviews at milestones; "it is not an admission of personal failure to change investigative direction" (MIM §2.3.4.6). |
| **Busywork in low-information cases** | "Avoid issuing low-quality actions simply as a way of doing something – this is demoralising ... and risks clogging up the management systems" (MIM §2.6.1). | Ask why information is scarce; seek independent review. |
| **Vague tasking** | "Carry out a full forensic examination" is "meaningless ... the team is setting the investigative strategy, not the SIO" (MIM §2.3.4.3). | Each strategy element has stated objectives; actions are small and achievable. |
| **Over-collection** | Actions that generate more material than the room can process ("a problem with passive data generators ... should be anticipated before the action is raised", MIM §2.3.5.2); "all-embracing" reviews of a whole HOLMES account "should generally be avoided" (MIM §4.7). | Proportionality: Rule 26(b)(1); Sedona Principle 6; Yellow Book "stronger evidence may allow less evidence". |
| **Biased seeds in machine review** | TAR seeded only from issues the team thought of misses the rest (EDRM TAR Guidelines ch. 2). | Mix human, random and machine-chosen examples; validate by sampling; record exclusions. |
| **Machines making context-dependent legal calls** | Privilege: TAR "cannot account for the events surrounding the creation or dissemination of a document" (EDRM TAR Guidelines ch. 3). | Human review for context-dependent judgments. |
| **Volume mistaken for proof** | "Having a large volume of evidence does not compensate for a lack of relevance, validity, or reliability" (Yellow Book §8.102d). | Assess the collective evidence explicitly; disclose limitations. |
| **Trusting the audited entity's own figures** | Yellow Book §§8.93, 8.96, 8.104f. | Test or corroborate; prefer independent third-party sources. |
| **Endless investigation** | "A fair investigation does not mean an endless investigation" (APP §9). | Define and explain scope; reasonable lines, not all conceivable lines; Investigative Maintenance when no viable line remains (MIM Fig. 12). |

## 4. Lessons for a member-run, AI-assisted, evidence-graded civic investigation system

Each lesson is tied to its source; how it maps onto the product is for the phase-2 studies.

1. **Keep a closed, typed record.** Every task should name the document or question that raised it, and every incoming document should name the task that fetched it or the source that sent it. That is what made HOLMES auditable and transferable (MIM §2.3.1; MIRSAP). For a civic case file, provenance runs both ways: document → reading → facts → new tasks → new documents.
2. **Read each document for its purpose, then index it.** The MIR's receiver/reader/indexer pipeline is the human version of Bob's "reader": know why the document is in the file, read it in full, mark what matters, extract the entities (people, organisations, places, dates, money), and *raise actions from what it says* (MIRSAP roles; APP §5.1 "set objectives for the evaluation"). An AI reader should do these as separate, recorded steps.
3. **One entity, one record.** Contractors, officials, agencies, projects and contracts should be indexed once and linked from every fact and document that mentions them (HOLMES nominal indexes; CaseMap short names). The Ripper failure was four unlinked cards for one man (Byford).
4. **Separate facts from evidence, and give each fact a status.** Use CaseMap's model: a fact is a dated proposition with sources, a status (undisputed, disputed by whom, prospective) and links to issues. Grade sources with the Yellow Book heuristics and the APP reliability rules: original over copy, independent third party over the entity's own account, corroborated over uncorroborated (CaseMap Facts fields; Yellow Book §8.104; APP §5.1).
5. **Make the plan hierarchical and explicit.** The objective is the member's question. Under it sit *issues* or *hypotheses*, under those *lines of enquiry*, under those small, achievable *actions*, each with a stated purpose. Never issue vague tasks (MIM §§2.3.4–2.3.5; QSI Planning; Yellow Book §8.08). Bob's "stay focused on the objective" is exactly the SIO's strategy-with-objectives discipline.
6. **Hold competing explanations as first-class objects.** For a slipped school rebuild, generate several hypotheses at the start (design change orders, contractor performance, funding gaps, permitting, scope creep, the district's own management) and, for each one, the lines of enquiry that would confirm or discount it. Apply "consider the opposite" and investigate *towards and away*. Close a hypothesis only on evidence, with the reason recorded (APP §§5.3–5.4; College 2023 Guideline 2; CPIA duty; IIO "starting from zero").
7. **Use the gap matrix as the planner's working view.** Rows 5WH; columns known / not known / conflicts / consistencies. The "not known" and "conflicts" cells generate the next actions. A disagreement among sources is itself a lead: the four-red-cars example (APP §5.2, §3.4).
8. **Use criteria, condition, cause, effect as the shape of an answer.** "What's going on with the school?" is an audit question: *criteria* (the promised one-year schedule, the contract's completion date, the budget), *condition* (two years and not near done), *cause* (the factors that explain the gap, argued from evidence) and *effect* (cost, students displaced). Develop only the elements the member's question needs (Yellow Book §§8.116–8.128).
9. **Log decisions separately from activity, written for a stranger.** For each significant choice record what was known and when, what was decided, by whom (member or machine), and why, including why a line was *not* pursued. In this product the member's accept or reject of a machine proposal is such a decision. Keep a short chronology log for handover (Policy File, MIM §2.5.1; IIO Decision and Investigational Logs; AFP: judge decisions by their quality, not their outcome).
10. **Review on a cadence, at milestones, and independently.** Quarterly supervisory review (QSI), formal review at 28 days and periodically (MIM §4), the IIO file-review questions. A civic analogue: a scheduled machine "case review" that re-asks "what do we know / need to know / where is it", flags stale lines and tunnel vision, and proposes the next steps to the member *(inference)*.
11. **Make go / refer / no-action an explicit intake decision.** Every intake ends in one of three recorded decisions: investigate, refer, or take no further action (QSI Planning; AFP Evaluation). Investigations are re-openable: an *Investigative Maintenance* state waits for new material such as a new status report or a records-request response (MIM Fig. 12; AFP Finalisation).
12. **Be proportionate and bounded.** Weigh each action's likelihood, size, cost and the volume it will generate before raising it (MIM §2.3.5.2). Stop when further work will not change the answer; reasonable, not exhaustive (FRCP 26; Sedona 6; TAR validation; APP §9). This bounds AI runs and cost directly.
13. **Machine ranking with human judgment and validation.** TAR is the closest professional precedent for AI reading at scale: humans set the relevance scope; the machine ranks and proposes; humans code; the process is validated by sampling and the exclusions are recorded. Context-dependent judgments (privilege there; perhaps intent or wrongdoing here) stay with humans. This is Bob's "machine proposes, member accepts" with its known failure modes attached: biased seeds, scope drift, unreadable documents (EDRM TAR Guidelines chs. 1–3).
14. **Look for what is missing.** Early case assessment and review of incoming productions exist to find expected-but-absent documents and to challenge a "data dump" (EDRM TAR Guidelines ch. 3). For a records request, the absence of an expected status report is a finding and a lead. Record redactions and withholdings like a privilege log (FRCP 26(b)(5)(A)).
15. **Report with exculpatory material and the subject's response.** Reports must include exculpatory and mitigating information (QSI Reporting), disclose evidence limitations (Yellow Book §8.115c) and give the responsible officials' views (Yellow Book §9.50). A civic answer about a school district should carry the district's own explanation and the evidence that cuts in its favour; that is fairness, and also credibility.
16. **Keep the person who brought the case informed on a fixed cadence.** Victims (and now suspects) get an expected finish date and 28-day updates (APP §8.4; AFP: communicate decisions to complainants). The member is the complainant and the decision-maker; the system should report progress and pending decisions to them at a cadence, not only when asked *(inference)*.
17. **Roles can collapse into one person but the functions remain.** MCM's command triangle (direction, file coordination, governance) "may" be one person in a small case "but the principles are still maintained" (IIO MOI ch. 2). In a member-run system the member holds direction and acceptance; the machine can hold most of file coordination (registering, indexing, tasking, logging); governance (review, independence) needs an explicit home *(inference)*.
18. **Independence and appearance matter for credibility.** QSI's impairments (preconceived opinions, interference with scope, denial of access) apply to a civic group investigating a body it has views about. Recording them, and seeking the other side, protects the eventual published case (QSI Independence) *(inference for the civic setting)*.
