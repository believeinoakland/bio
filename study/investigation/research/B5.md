## 1. How practitioners actually work

### 1.1 The life of a public works project and the documents each stage leaves behind

OCDS divides a contracting process into **planning, tender, award, contract, implementation** ([OCDS primer](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/primer/how/)). OC4IDS adds a **project** level above the contracts: identification, preparation, implementation, completion, maintenance, decommissioning ([OC4IDS projects](https://standard.open-contracting.org/infrastructure/latest/en/projects/)). A school rebuild is one *project* holding several *contracting processes*: architect, construction manager, inspector of record, testing lab, the main construction contract, and often abatement or demolition packages. The distinction matters because a delay can sit in any one of them, or between them.

The table maps each stage of a California school rebuild to its documents. Under "Publisher / where", *board packet* means the district's agenda and minutes system, where most contract approvals, change-order ratifications and status reports appear as attachments *(general knowledge)*.

Prop 39 limits bond money to the projects listed in the measure ([Contra Costa Grand Jury 1514](https://contracosta.courts.ca.gov/system/files/1514westcontracostaunifiedschooldistrictbondprogram.pdf), Summary) |
| Identification | Campaign material, board presentations, community meetings ("one school year") | District; local press | Often the only source of the member's promise. It is *not* a contract term (see §1.6, baseline) |

**What a member can get, and how.** Almost everything above is a public record. What is posted proactively varies widely. Board packets, the CBOC page, eTracker, the Certification Box, CSLB and DIR are online. Baseline schedules, schedule updates, pay applications, change-order logs, inspector reports and claim correspondence usually need a California Public Records Act request *(general knowledge)*. CoST's split between **proactive** disclosure (40 items) and **reactive** disclosure (26 items, released on request) is a ready-made checklist of what to ask for ([CoST disclosure](https://infrastructuretransparency.org/our-approach/disclosure/), via search; core mapping).

### 1.2 How the owner buys: delivery methods and solicitation types

The **delivery method** decides who carries design risk and schedule risk, which documents exist, and whether "the lowest bid" is even the test. Sources: the [CASBO bidding guide](https://www.f3law.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Updated-CASBO-Bidding-and-Contracting-Guide-2020-002.pdf) §IV.S–U; [DSA IR A-33](https://www.dgs.ca.gov/-/media/Divisions/DSA/Publications/interpretations_of_regs/IR_A-33_rev08-20-19.pdf); [DBIA](https://dbia.org/what-is-design-build/).

| Method | Contracts and selection | Who carries design errors and delay | Investigation consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Design-bid-build (DBB)** | Architect designs; the complete design is bid by IFB; award goes to the *lowest responsive, responsible bidder* (PCC 20111) | The owner warrants the plans: PCC 1104 forbids making bidders responsible for the plans' accuracy except in true design-build (CASBO §IV.E). Design errors become owner change orders | The most common method for California schools (CASBO §IV.T). The 3 years before construction are design, DSA review and bidding, run in sequence. Design-error change orders point back at the architect's contract |
| **Multiple prime** | Design split into bid packages, each bid separately | The owner coordinates the primes and carries the coordination risk (CASBO §IV.T.1) | Delays between primes are the owner's |
| **Design-build (DB)** | One entity designs and builds. RFQ to prequalify or short-list, then RFP; award on low bid or best value (Ed Code 17250.10–17250.55; school projects over $1M) | The design-builder carries design errors | Fewer design-error change orders are expected, so many is itself a signal. CASBO (2020) noted the authority's sunset on 1 Jan 2025; whether it was extended must be checked |
| **Lease-leaseback (LLB)** | The district leases the site to a builder, who builds and leases it back. Since 2017: best-value RFP and a guaranteed maximum price (GMP) set after preconstruction (Ed Code 17400 et seq., 17406) | The builder, within the GMP and its contingencies | The GMP, the contingency draw-down and allowance reconciliation become the money documents. Conflict-of-interest litigation history: *CalTAN v. Taber* (CASBO §IV.T.3); *Davis v. Fresno USD* *(general knowledge)* |
| **CM at-risk (CMAR)** | A construction manager gives a GMP and holds the trade contracts. No express statute for school districts, so its legality turns on whether the CMAR acts as contractor or adviser (CASBO §IV.T.4) | The CMAR, within the GMP | As LLB |
| **CM as agent** | An adviser hired by RFP (Gov Code 4525 et seq.) | The contractor, not the CM, is "directly at risk for the project finishing on time or under budget" (CASBO §IV.S) | The CM writes the status reports. The CM is often also the program manager paid from the bond (WCCUSD paid one firm $86M+, Grand Jury 1514) |

**Solicitation types** *(general knowledge, consistent with CASBO §II.B)*. An **IFB** (invitation for bids) asks for a price on a complete design; award is on price to the lowest responsive, responsible bidder. An **RFQ** asks for qualifications only, to short-list or prequalify. An **RFP** asks for proposals scored on stated criteria (best value). Professional services (architects, CMs, inspectors) are chosen on qualifications under Gov Code 4525 et seq. For an investigator, a *bid tabulation* answers "how many bid and how close"; an *RFP scoring sheet* answers "why this one".

DSA oversight is the same under every method. The design professionals stay responsible under Title 24; the inspector of record, special inspectors and labs are hired directly by the district, never by the builder; and the builder's costs go on the DSA 168 (IR A-33 §§1.2, 2.2).

### 1.3 Contract administration: the mechanics that produce delays and overruns

**Time.** The contract states a contract time and a substantial-completion date. The contractor submits a **baseline CPM schedule** (A201-2017 requires one with "interim milestone dates, and the date of Substantial Completion", per the [AIA summary](https://help.aiacontracts.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500010259162-summary-a201-2017-general-conditions-of-the-contract-for-construction) Art. 3), then **monthly updates**. A time extension needs a request with notice, approved by the architect or owner (A201 Art. 8). Weather may justify one.

**Delay doctrine.** A delay is classified as:

- **excusable** (the contractor gets time);
- **compensable** (the contractor gets time and money, usually because the owner caused it);
- **non-excusable** (the contractor's own; the owner may assess **liquidated damages**, a per-day amount fixed in the contract).

Under **concurrency**, "if concurrent delays exist, typically neither party is entitled to compensable delay damages" ([Long International](https://www.long-intl.com/articles/schedule-analysis-method-2/)). In California, PCC 7102 voids clauses that limit the owner to granting time for an unreasonable owner-caused delay. But *Greg Opinski Construction v. City of Oakdale* (2011) lets the agency collect LDs, even for a delay it caused, when the contractor did not follow the contract's time-extension procedure (CASBO §IV.J). Gov Code 4215 bars LDs for delays caused by unidentified utilities (CASBO §IV.R). PCC 7104 requires a change order for differing subsurface conditions in excavations deeper than four feet (CASBO §IV.I).

**Changes.** Changes are made by **change order** (agreed price and time), by **construction change directive** (unilateral, price settled later) or by a minor change (A201 Art. 7). For school districts, PCC 20118.4 allows changes without new bidding up to the greater of the bid threshold or **10% of the original contract price**. Districts with 400,000+ average daily attendance may go to 25% on rehabilitation, with a 75% board vote above 15%. Beyond that limit, work "entirely distinct" from the original contract must be bid (CASBO §IV.E). Verbal approval does not bind the agency where the contract requires a written change order (*P&D Consultants v. Carlsbad*, CASBO §IV.E). A DSA CCD is the *code-compliance* record of a change; the change order is the *money* record; the two are separate (IR A-6 §1.3).

**Money.** Progress payments come through monthly pay applications against the schedule of values. **Retention** is 5% of each progress payment and is the cap unless the board finds the project "substantially complex" (PCC 7201, 9203). Retention is released within 60 days of acceptance (PCC 7107). Undisputed payment requests unpaid after 30 days earn interest (PCC 20104.50) (all CASBO §IV.Q). A late-paying owner is a known cause of contractor slow-down (§1.6).

**Claims.** PCC 9204 sets a claim process for time, delay penalties or money: submission, meet and confer, then non-binding mediation. It applies to contracts from 2017 and sunsets 1 Jan 2027 unless extended (CASBO §IV.V). Claim files are where both parties write down their competing explanations of a delay.

**Forensic schedule analysis** is how practitioners settle "why is it late". AACE Recommended Practice 29R-03 sorts the methods ([Long International](https://www.long-intl.com/articles/schedule-analysis-method-2/); [Ankura on SCL and AACE](https://ankura.com/insights/harmonizing-scl-dd2-and-aace-29r-03-complementary-frameworks-for-forensic-delay-analysis-in-international-arbitration), via search):

- *observational* methods: as-planned vs as-built; windows analysis on the contemporaneous updates;
- *modeled additive* methods: impacted as-planned; time impact analysis;
- *modeled subtractive* methods: collapsed as-built, giving the "but-for" answer.

Each needs specific records: baseline plus as-built; baseline plus contemporaneous updates; or as-built plus daily records. "Selection of the analytical method should be based primarily on technical considerations related to the purpose, the timing, availability of data." A member's investigation will rarely run CPM software. It can still apply the *observational* logic: compare the baseline's milestone dates with each update and the status reports, find the window where the slip began, and read what happened in that window.

### 1.4 Oversight bodies and what they can and cannot see

| Body | Mandate | Public output | Limits practitioners report |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Citizens' bond oversight committee (CBOC)** under Prop 39 | "Inform the public concerning the expenditure of bond revenues… actively review and report on the proper expenditure". It ensures bond money goes only to listed purposes and never to salaries; it *may* receive the annual performance and financial audits, inspect sites and review cost-saving efforts ([Ed Code 15278](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/education-code/edc-sect-15278/)) | Open meetings; at least one report a year; minutes, all documents received and reports posted on the district's website ([Ed Code 15280](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/education-code/edc-sect-15280/)). The board must answer audit findings within three months (15280(a)(2)) | It reviews after the fact, with no authority over spending or contract awards (Grand Jury 1514). | **Annual performance audit and financial audit** (Cal. Const. art. XIIIA §1(b)(3)(C)) | Independent auditors | Audit reports, usually posted with CBOC material | At WCCUSD two performance auditors "apparently missed the discrepancies in the change order data" (Grand Jury 1514) |

| **Labor Commissioner / DIR; CSLB** | Prevailing wage and registration; licence discipline | Debarment lists; licence records | Discipline only for violations, not for lateness |

### 1.5 Named investigative methods in this domain

**(a) OCP red-flag method** ([OCP 2024](https://www.open-contracting.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/OCP2024-RedFlagProcurement-1.pdf), pp. 10–17), in six steps:

1. Understand the context: the law, the existing monitoring, the stakeholders.
2. Select indicators that are relevant, specific, measurable and achievable. For each, record who it is for, why it matters, when it can be detected and **what action follows**.
3. Identify the data needs: sources, access, quality and coverage. Other sources such as company registries and beneficial ownership can be joined.
4. Calculate with defined formulas and units of analysis (process, bidder, buyer, market), then **validate**. A flag signals risk and "is not evidence that illicit behavior is present"; the reviewer must decide whether the act is "a) not at all illicit or suboptimal; b) not illicit, but suboptimal…; or c) illicit". If 90% of processes are flagged, the indicator is probably producing false positives.
5. Present the results: counts, rankings, combinations of related flags. Do not reuse other contexts' weights. Be transparent about the method.
6. Institutionalise it: users, workflow per flag, monitoring.

There are 73 indicators across six types (low transparency, low competition, fraud, collusion, bid rigging). The ones about **implementation and delay** are:

- R054: a direct award followed by change orders that exceed the competitive threshold;
- R059: the final contract amount differs from the award value by more than a threshold, or by more than the Q3 + 1.5·IQR upper fence;
- R064: the contract has amendments;
- R065 and R066: amendments reduce or increase line items;
- R067: **delivery failure**, `milestones.dateMet > dueDate` on delivery milestones;
- R068: payments exceed the contract amount;
- R069: amendments increase the price;
- R070–R072: subcontracting patterns, including losing bidders hired as subcontractors;
- R073: work completed does not match the specifications. It needs completion certificates, progress reports and final audits, and "might require a manual review of documents".

Its sources include the World Bank's *Warning signs of fraud and corruption in procurement* and the [IACRC guide](https://guide.iacrc.org/).

**(b) CoST assurance** ([CoST Assurance Guidance Note 2020](https://infrastructuretransparency.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Assurance-Guidance-Note-FINAL-1.pdf)) is an *independent review* commissioned by a multi-stakeholder group. In eight steps:

1. The procuring entity discloses data.
2. The data is published.
3. An assurance team checks the data for completeness (a disclosure rate against the CoST IDS) and accuracy ("comparing data from different sources").
4. A sample of projects is chosen, random plus "on the basis of concern or public interest".
5. The team requests missing data and asks for the reactive items. The aim is "not… to obtain as much data… as possible, but rather to record what is, and is not, available, and what has, and has not, been shared, then make sense of it".
6. Site visits compare "reported project status (in terms of physical and financial progress) with what is observed". The team should "avoid making any technical judgements".
7. The team turns the data into "compelling information".
8. It recommends projects for further review by the relevant authority. "If the recommended follow-up action is not taken, that can itself be reported as a fact."

Principle: "presenting facts, not opinions". A red flag "draw[s] attention to an issue without making an associated judgement". Report shape: summary, context, development of findings, recommendations, annexes. Examples of statements that let facts speak (Table 1): "Time elapsed is 400% of original contract duration. But progress is under 70% and PE has not issued any warnings." "Project management consultant was paid 56% more than contract price. There is no documentation justifying the extra payments."

**(c) IACRC investigative steps** ([IACRC steps of proof](https://guide.iacrc.org/proof/steps-of-proof/the-basic-steps-of-a-complex-fraud-and-corruption-investigation/)), from an investigator's case:

1. Begin the case: debrief the complainant fully, or match red flags to potential schemes.
2. Evaluate the allegations.
3. Do due diligence on firms and people: shells, debarments, unexplained wealth.
4. Collect documents and data internally.
5. **Check predication** and organise the evidence by the elements of the offence.
6. Investigate externally: witnesses, third-party documents.
7. Prove payments.
8. Obtain inside witnesses.
9. Interview the subject, seeking admissions.
10. Write the final report with recommended actions.

Implementation-stage red flags: questionable change orders; poor quality goods or works, especially when accepted repeatedly; questionable invoices; unjustified high prices; inadequate payment documentation ([IACRC red flags by project cycle](https://guide.iacrc.org/detection/red-flags-of-corruption-bid-rigging-and-other-schemes/red-flags-listed-by-project-cycle/)).

**(d) Forensic schedule analysis** (AACE 29R-03, §1.3): the professional method for apportioning delay between parties.

**(e) Grand-jury and CBOC practice** (Grand Jury 1514, Appendix A, methodology): interviews with current and former committee members, trustees and staff; minutes and agendas; monthly financial reports; "Items Requiring Board Attention" memos; project status reports; CBOC annual reports; performance audits; audited financial statements; adopted budgets; and a whistle-blower's documents. The jury compared what the district *told* the committee with the underlying records.

### 1.6 What typically explains a delayed or over-budget project

**The baseline question comes first.** "Late" and "over budget" are measured against a reference. Cantarelli, Flyvbjerg et al. measure overrun as "actual out-turn costs minus estimated costs" against the estimate "at the time of the decision to build". They warn that the *formal* decision to build may come after the *real* one: "lock-in", where the earlier estimate was lower ([Cantarelli et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.2176), Methodology). For Bob's case there are at least four baselines, and each can give a different answer:

1. the community promise ("one school year");
2. the bond-measure or board-approved budget and schedule;
3. the **contract time** in the awarded construction contract;
4. the contractor's approved baseline schedule.

"Promised one year" may have been a campaign figure. The contract may say 540 days. The baseline schedule may show 18 months. The investigation must find each and say which promise was broken *(inference, applying the source's point)*.

**Explanation families.** Two taxonomies are used together: who caused it, and why the estimate was wrong.

| Family | Typical causes | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Owner** | Owner-requested scope changes; slow decisions and approvals; late site access or permits; late payment; funding gaps; program budget exhausted | Top-ranked factors in a 98-factor review validated on 141 projects: (1) frequent change orders and design changes, (3) slow decisions and bureaucracy, (4) late progress payments and funding, (5) delays approving design documents. Owner factors were "approximately 50% of the top 22" ([PMC 2025 multistage study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13201550/)). WCCUSD: "70 percent of the overall cost of change orders was due to construction owner requested change orders"; changes approved on "nice to have" grounds (Grand Jury 1514) |
| **Design** | Errors and omissions; incomplete documents; slow RFI and submittal responses; DSA CCD approvals | WCCUSD: 76 of 126 change orders at one school were design errors (Grand Jury 1514). The owner warrants the plans under DBB (PCC 1104) |
| **Contractor** | Poor planning and scheduling; weak site management; low productivity; financial distress; under-bidding; subcontractor default | "Top 10 universal delay factors" ([ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324494045_The_top_10_universal_delay_factors_in_construction_projects), via search); PMC study |
| **External / unforeseen** | Differing site conditions (contamination, utilities, subsurface); weather; price escalation; labour and material shortages; regulatory changes | PMC study, (2) price fluctuations; Gov Code 4215; PCC 7104 |
| **Technical** (estimate error) | Price rises, poor design, incomplete estimates, scope changes, inadequate planning: "'honest' errors" | Cantarelli et al. Table 2 |
| **Psychological** | Optimism bias, planning fallacy | Cantarelli et al.; Flyvbjerg |
| **Political-economic** | Strategic misrepresentation: costs and durations understated to win approval, including bond campaigns | Cantarelli et al.; Flyvbjerg. SDUSD: bonds repeated the same HVAC promises since 2008, and project lists became generic ([Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2025/05/22/grand-jury-finds-faults-in-san-diego-unifieds-bond-program/)) |
| **Integrity** | Change-order abuse, collusion, favouritism, substandard work accepted | OCP R054, R059, R064–R073; IACRC |
| **Information failure** (it is late *and nobody said so*) | Unreported change orders; costs entered as zero; cause codes altered; late or missing reports | WCCUSD: ~$2M of unprocessed change orders entered at zero cost; 400+ proposed change orders in queue; monthly reports withheld for two years (Grand Jury 1514) |

**Each explanation has documents that test it** *(inference from §1.1, §1.3 and §1.5)*:

| If the explanation is… | It should show in… | And would be contradicted by… |
|---|---|---|
| Owner scope change | Change orders with an owner cause code; board ratifications; design revisions | Few owner change orders; a stable scope |
| Design errors | Change orders and RFIs with an E&O cause; CCDs; claims against the architect; architect fee amendments | Low RFI volume; no CCD queue |
| DSA review or CCD backlog | eTracker dates; CCD Category A submission and approval dates | Quick approvals |
| Unforeseen conditions | Early change orders tied to excavation, abatement or utilities; geotechnical report vs findings; PCC 7104 notices | A site well characterised in the bid documents |
| Contractor performance | Schedule updates slipping on the contractor's own activities; inspector reports of thin crews; certified-payroll head-count; LDs assessed; notices to cure; claims against the bond | Time extensions granted for owner causes |
| Contractor financial distress | Stop-payment notices; subcontractor suits; surety involvement; CSLB bond changes | Steady payments and staffing |
| Owner payment delay or funding gap | Pay applications vs payment dates; PCC 20104.50 interest; budget transfers; OPSC fund-release timing; reprioritised bond program | Timely payments |
| Optimistic promise | A promise made before design and bidding; the contract time already longer than the promise | A contract time equal to the promise |
| Integrity problem | Red flags at award (single bid, bid spread, R031); growth after award (R059, R069); subcontracting patterns (R070) | A competitive, well-documented award |

**Delays are usually multi-causal and concurrent** (Long International on concurrency). A good answer apportions the delay over time windows and does not name a single villain *(inference)*.

## 2. Data models and tools practitioners use

### 2.1 OCDS: the contracting process as an append-only record

- **OCID.** One identifier per contracting process: a prefix registered with OCP plus the publisher's local id. It is used to "join up data across the whole contracting process" ([OCDS primer](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/primer/how/)).
- **Releases and records.** "A release is JSON data that is published each time there is a change to a contracting process… Releases are immutable." A **record** indexes all releases for one process. Its **compiled release** gives "the latest value of each field". Its **versioned release** gives "a history of changes for each field" ([releases and records](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/primer/releases_and_records/)). "Publishers must not edit a release after publication" ([schema reference](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/schema/reference/)).
- **Release tags** (closed list): planning, planningUpdate, tender, tenderAmendment, tenderUpdate, tenderCancellation, award, awardUpdate, awardCancellation, contract, contractUpdate, contractAmendment, implementation, implementationUpdate, contractTermination, compiled ([codelists](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/schema/codelists/)).
- **Parties and roles.** Organisations appear once, in `parties`, and are referenced by id. Roles: buyer, procuringEntity, supplier, tenderer, funder, enquirer, payer, payee, reviewBody, interestedParty. OC4IDS adds publicAuthority and administrativeEntity. The **buyer** (whose budget pays) can differ from the **procuring entity** (who runs the procurement) ([OCP 2024](https://www.open-contracting.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/OCP2024-RedFlagProcurement-1.pdf), Table 1).
- **Stage objects.**
  - `planning`: rationale, budget, documents, milestones.
  - `tender`: status, procurementMethod (open, selective, limited, direct), value, tenderPeriod, enquiryPeriod, awardCriteria (priceOnly, costOnly, qualityOnly, ratedCriteria), numberOfTenderers, tenderers, documents, milestones, amendments.
  - `bids/details`: each bid's value, date, status and tenderers.
  - `awards[]`: value, suppliers, status (pending, active, cancelled, unsuccessful).
  - `contracts[]`: value, period, dateSigned, status (pending, active, cancelled, terminated), amendments, `implementation` holding transactions, milestones, documents and finalValue.
- **Milestones.** Type: preProcurement, approval, engagement, assessment, delivery, reporting, financing, payment. Status: scheduled, met, notMet, partiallyMet. A milestone has a `dueDate` and a `dateMet`, which is what makes delay computable (R067).
- **Amendments.** An amendment carries a `description` and a `rationale`. It sits in the tender or the contract and is announced by an amendment release.
- **Documents.** Each is typed by `documentType`, an open codelist of about 40 values, including: plannedProcurementNotice, tenderNotice, awardNotice, contractNotice, completionCertificate, procurementPlan, biddingDocuments, technicalSpecifications, evaluationCriteria, evaluationReports, contractDraft, contractSigned, contractSchedule, physicalProgressReport, financialProgressReport, finalAudit, hearingNotice, clarifications, shortlistedFirms, environmentalImpact, riskProvisions, winningBid, complaints, contractAnnexe, contractGuarantees, subContract, feasibilityStudy, projectPlan, billOfQuantity, bidders, conflictOfInterest, debarments, cancellationDetails ([codelists](https://standard.open-contracting.org/latest/en/schema/codelists/)).
- **Related processes.** Links to other processes: framework, planning, parent, prior, unsuccessfulProcess, subContract, replacementProcess, renewalProcess. A re-bid after a failed tender is a `replacementProcess`.

### 2.2 OC4IDS and the CoST IDS: the project above the contracts

- A **project** has an id, title, status, sector, locations, purpose, budget (with approvalDate and budgetBreakdowns), parties, documents, `contractingProcesses[]` (each a *summary*, with links to full OCDS releases), and **`completion`**: endDate, finalValue, finalScope, and **endDateDetails, finalValueDetails and finalScopeDetails, the reasons for the changes** ([core mapping](https://standard.open-contracting.org/infrastructure/latest/en/cost/ids/core/)).
- Contract-level **`modifications[]`** each have a `type` (value, duration or scope), `date`, `description`, `rationale`, `oldContractValue`/`newContractValue` and `oldContractPeriod`/`newContractPeriod`. Price variations, price escalation, duration variations and scope variations, with their rationales, are **proactive** CoST data points (core mapping, Implementation).
- Further document types for infrastructure: projectScope, budgetApproval, budgetAmendmentApproval, projectCompletionReport, projectEvaluation, technicalAuditReport, financialAuditReport, escalationApprovals, qualityAssuranceReport, paymentCertificate, contractAmendment, projectDesignReport, incorporationCertificate.
- **Disclosure tiers.** 40 proactive items: 20 at project level and 20 at contract level (Assurance Note step 3). 26 reactive items, released on request ([CoST disclosure](https://infrastructuretransparency.org/our-approach/disclosure/), via search).

**What this gives an investigation system** *(inference)*:

- a ready vocabulary for typing every document in Bob's case file by stage and kind;
- a shape for the *project* object that holds many contracts;
- a modification object that records time and money changes *with their stated reasons*, which is exactly the "official explanation" an investigator then tests;
- an immutable, versioned history in which every change is a new dated release, so "what did they say, and when" can always be answered.

US school districts do not publish OCDS. The member's system would have to *build* this structure by reading board packets and PDFs *(inference; no US K-12 OCDS publisher found)*.

## 3. What failed or is warned against

1. **Treating a flag as a finding.** "A red flag is… not proof that anything is wrong" ([OCP via search](https://www.open-contracting.org/events/red-flags-webinar/)). "The indicator signals a risk – it is not evidence that illicit behavior is present" (OCP 2024 p. 14). CoST: present the fact that something is a red flag under independent criteria, "without making an associated judgement" (Box 4).
2. **Noisy indicators.** A flag that fires on 90% of procedures is mostly false positives. Weights copied from another context may not fit. Prefer few, specific, measurable flags (OCP 2024 pp. 11, 14–15). Change-order flags in particular (R064) fire on most construction contracts, because change orders are normal. Here the *rate*, the *cause mix* and the *threshold* (10% under PCC 20118.4) carry the information, not the presence of a change order *(inference)*.
3. **Data that is late, incomplete or wrong, and reports that hide it.** At WCCUSD (Grand Jury 1514):
   - monthly reports were withheld for about two years;
   - the master plan had not been updated since 2007;
   - fund summaries mixed bond money with other money;
   - unprocessed change orders were entered at **zero** cost;
   - change orders were recorded only after a project closed;
   - a committee member suspected cause codes were being changed;
   - records arrived the day before a vote, with pages missing and out of order;
   - a whistle-blower showed status reports "greatly understated actual spending".

   Lesson: the official status report is a *claim to be checked*, not ground truth.
4. **Oversight structurally after the fact.** CBOCs have no authority over spending or awards and review after decisions are made. They depend on the district for data and training; their members are appointed by the board they oversee (Grand Jury 1514; search summary of other grand juries). Auditors also missed the change-order discrepancies.
5. **The wrong baseline.** Overrun figures change with the reference estimate. Lock-in makes the earliest public estimate the lowest (Cantarelli et al.). Measuring against the campaign promise and measuring against the contract time give different stories, and both must be shown.
6. **Single-cause stories.** Delay is usually concurrent and multi-party. Under concurrency neither side may recover delay damages (Long International). Procedure can override fault: *Opinski* lets an agency collect LDs for a delay it caused if the contractor missed the notice procedure (CASBO §IV.J). "Who is to blame" and "who pays" are different questions.
7. **Technical judgements by non-experts.** CoST site visitors are told to "avoid making any technical judgements" and to limit quality comments to whether quality systems exist and are used (Note step 6). Assurance does not duplicate official accountability; it strengthens it (Box 1).
8. **Records that are closed or partial.**
   - Prequalification questionnaires and financial statements are not public (PCC 20111.6).
   - CSLB discloses only complaints that led to citations, accusations or referral after a probable serious violation was found, and states that "a complaint… is only an allegation" ([CSLB disclosure](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/ComplaintDisclosure.aspx?LicType=LIC&LicNum=988918), via search). A clean CSLB record is weak evidence of good performance *(inference)*.
   - Bid evaluation reports may be commercially confidential. CoST teams sometimes see them on condition of non-publication (Note step 5).
9. **Accepting the district's word that a fix was made.** In 2012 WCCUSD answered a recommendation "Had already been implemented"; the 2015 jury found it had not (Grand Jury 1514).
10. **Vague promises that cannot be tested.** Generic bond project lists and repeated identical promises (SDUSD HVAC language since 2008) make accountability hard ([Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2025/05/22/grand-jury-finds-faults-in-san-diego-unifieds-bond-program/)).
11. **Legal status drifts.** Design-build authority (sunset noted for 2025) and the PCC 9204 claim process (sunset 2027) change over time. An engine must date every legal premise *(inference from CASBO §§IV.T.2, IV.V)*.

## 4. Lessons for a member-run, AI-assisted, evidence-graded civic investigation system

Each lesson names its source. The design consequences are *(inference)* unless stated.

| # | Lesson | Source |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | **Model the investigation's subject as a project holding contracting processes, each moving through planning → tender → award → contract → implementation, and a completion record.** Use the OCDS/OC4IDS stage names and `documentType` codelist to type every document the member or the engine adds. "This is the bid tabulation of the main construction contract" is then a typed slot, and an empty slot is a visible gap | OCDS codelists; OC4IDS core mapping |
| L2 | **Keep history append-only, dated releases.** Each new fact (an amendment, a revised completion date, a new status report) is a new dated entry, never an overwrite, so the system can show what was claimed when and how the story changed. Compiled and versioned views come from the same entries | OCDS releases and records |
| L3 | **Make the CoST proactive/reactive list the default "documents to find" plan for any public-works investigation.** Proactive items are searched for online; reactive items become drafted records requests for the member to approve. The disclosure rate is reported as a finding in its own right | CoST IDS core mapping; Assurance Note step 3 |
| L4 | **Log every request and refusal as evidence.** "Record what is, and is not, available, and what has, and has not, been shared". A refused or ignored request, or an unanswered recommendation, "can itself be reported as a fact" | Assurance Note steps 5, 8 |
| L5 | **Red flags are leads, never conclusions.** Each flag the engine computes (R059, R067, R068, R069, R054, the 10% change-order threshold, a single bid, the bid spread) carries its definition, required data, source and the next step it triggers. The member sees a flag as "this warrants a look, because…". The graded conclusion must come from documents that test an explanation | OCP 2024 pp. 10–15; CoST Box 4 |
| L6 | **Fix the baselines before measuring the slip.** For time and cost alike, find and show up to four: the public promise, the approved budget or schedule, the contract time or sum, and the baseline schedule. Name which promise is at issue. In Bob's case "one school year" must be traced to a source; it may be a campaign promise, not a contract term | Cantarelli et al. (decision to build, lock-in); A201 Art. 3 and 8 |
| L7 | **Split the elapsed time into windows and explain each.** Bob's three years before construction break into identification, design, DSA review, bidding and award, each with documented dates (eTracker, board award date, notice to proceed). The two years of construction break into schedule-update windows. This is the observational forensic method in lay form; it suits members and avoids pretending to CPM expertise | AACE 29R-03 via Long International; DSA eTracker |
| L8 | **Hold competing explanations explicitly and test each against named documents.** The §1.6 table is a starter library: owner change, design error, DSA or CCD backlog, unforeseen conditions, contractor performance, contractor distress, owner payment or funding, optimistic promise, integrity problem, information failure. Expect concurrency and apportion over windows rather than pick one culprit. Keep "who caused it" apart from "who pays" (PCC 7102, *Opinski*, LDs) | PMC 2025 study; Cantarelli et al.; Long International; CASBO §IV.J |
| L9 | **Treat official status reports as claims.** Cross-check them against independent records: board-ratified change orders vs the change-order log; pay applications vs reported percent complete; inspector reports and certified-payroll head-counts vs claimed progress; DSA 168 vs the district's final cost. Accuracy is assessed by "comparing data from different sources" | Grand Jury 1514; Assurance Note step 3 |
| L10 | **Follow the change orders.** Rate as a percentage of the original contract, cause mix (owner, design error, unforeseen), timing, pending vs processed, and board ratification. Pending or unprocessed change orders are where overruns hide | Grand Jury 1514; PCC 20118.4; OCP R064–R069 |
| L11 | **Contractor history is several partial sources, none complete.** CSLB (disclosure-limited), DIR registration and debarment, other agencies' board packets and claims, court dockets, LDs assessed elsewhere. The prequalification answers under oath are not public. Grade a "clean record" as weak evidence | CSLB disclosure policy; PCC 20111.6; DIR model questionnaire |
| L12 | **Use the institutions as allies and channels.** The CBOC (open meetings, an annual report, documents posted by law), the board's required response to audits within three months, DSA's Certification Box, the county grand jury. An answer often ends in an action: a question at the CBOC, a public comment, a records request, a grand jury complaint. Assurance "strengthens existing accountability mechanisms rather than… duplicating" them | Ed Code 15278, 15280; CoST Box 1; Grand Jury 1514 |
| L13 | **Write findings as observation plus factual comment.** "Time elapsed is 200% of contract time; reported progress 55%; no notice of default issued." Facts, not opinions; technical judgements left to experts; a short narrative summary for non-specialists | Assurance Note Table 1, §7 |
| L14 | **Combine a random sample with a "concern" sample** if the system later monitors many projects (a district's whole bond program) rather than one. Rank by flag counts with care, and be transparent about the method | Assurance Note step 4; OCP 2024 p. 14 |
| L15 | **Date every legal and procedural premise.** Statutes sunset or change: DB authority, PCC 9204, the LLB rules of 2017. The reading of a 2019 contract must use the rules in force then | CASBO §§IV.T, IV.V |
| L16 | **Mind the incentive problem without assuming it.** Strategic misrepresentation and optimism bias are documented explanations for broken promises. The system can test them (was the promise shorter than any contract time ever set? were later bonds sold on the same promises?) but must grade them as hypotheses until documents support them | Cantarelli et al.; Voice of San Diego (SDUSD) |
| L17 | **The member stays the decider at the escalation points.** The IACRC step "check predication" before going external maps naturally onto the member accepting the engine's proposal to file a records request, contact the district, or take a finding to the CBOC or press | IACRC steps 5–6 |

**Bob's case, compressed into the steps above** *(inference)*:

1. Intake. Which district and school? Where did "one school year" come from? Is the school a DBB, LLB or DB project?
2. Typed slots. Bond measure; board approvals; DSA application number; bid tab; contract (time, LDs); baseline schedule; status reports; change-order log; CBOC reports.
3. Search online: board packets, eTracker, the CBOC page, DIR, CSLB.
4. Drafted records requests for the reactive items.
5. Pre-construction timeline split into windows. Construction timeline split into update windows.
6. Flags computed: contract-time elapsed vs percent complete, change-order rate vs 10%, payments vs contract sum, pending CCDs.
7. Explanations tested against documents, each graded.
8. An answer naming the promise, the slip per window, the best-supported causes and their grades, open questions, and proposed next actions (CBOC question, records request, board comment).
