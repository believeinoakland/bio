## 1. How practitioners work, step by step

### 1.1 Intelligence analysis tradecraft: the shape of the work

The intelligence profession's own account of why structure is needed is about the mind, not the data. Heuer's core argument, repeated in the CIA's 2009 *Tradecraft Primer*: analysts "assimilate and evaluate information through the medium of 'mental models'"; mind-sets mean that "analysts perceive what they expect to perceive; once formed, they are resistant to change; new information is assimilated, sometimes erroneously, into existing mental models; and conflicting information is often dismissed or ignored" (Primer, Introduction, https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf). The Primer tabulates the biases the techniques target: expectation, resistance, ambiguity (perceptual); consistency, missing information, discredited evidence (evaluating evidence); availability, anchoring, overconfidence (probabilities); rationality and attribution (causality), the last being "Randomness, accident and error tend to be rejected as explanations for observed events" (same source). That last bias matters directly for civic questions: a delayed school rebuild is often error and accident, not scheme.

Heuer's Chapter 5 adds the finding that shapes any collection strategy: "Once an experienced analyst has the minimum information necessary to make an informed judgment, obtaining additional information generally does not improve the accuracy of his or her estimates. Additional information does, however, lead the analyst to become more confident in the judgment, to the point of overconfidence" (Heuer, *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, ch. 5, https://www.crest-approved.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Psychology-of-Intelligence-Analysis-1.pdf). He rejects the "mosaic theory" (collect every piece and the picture emerges) in favour of **medical diagnosis**: observe symptoms, generate hypotheses, run tests chosen to discriminate between them, diagnose. "Collection is focused narrowly on information that will help to discriminate the relative probability of alternate hypothesis." He cites medical students whose "research strategy stressed thorough collection of information (as opposed to formation and testing of hypotheses)" being "significantly below average" in diagnostic accuracy: "the explicit formulation of hypotheses directs a more efficient and effective search for information" (same, ch. 5). He sorts new information into four kinds: more detail on known variables; additional variables; new values of known variables; and information about which variables matter and how they relate (the mental model). Only the last two improve accuracy; the first two mostly raise confidence (same, ch. 5).

Heuer's Chapter 4 names the strategies analysts use to go beyond the data: **situational logic** (the case on its own terms, cause-effect and means-ends), **theory** (generalisation from many cases; "theory economizes thought"), **comparison/analogy** (dangerous: "the most productive uses of comparative analysis are to suggest hypotheses and to highlight differences, not to draw conclusions"), and the non-strategy of **data immersion** ("information cannot speak for itself") (Heuer ch. 4, same URL). A civic engine uses all three real strategies: situational logic (this contract, this district), theory (how public construction projects typically slip), comparison (other districts' rebuilds).

### 1.2 The Tradecraft Primer's techniques (diagnostic, contrarian, imaginative)

The Primer groups twelve techniques by purpose: "diagnostic techniques are primarily aimed at making analytic arguments, assumptions, or intelligence gaps more transparent; contrarian techniques explicitly challenge current thinking; and imaginative thinking techniques aim at developing new insights" (Primer, How to use).

| technique | purpose (Primer's words, shortened) | when | method in brief | artefact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Key Assumptions Check** | "List and review the key working assumptions on which fundamental judgments rest" | "most useful at the beginning of an analytic project"; recheck "prior to finalizing judgments" | 1 write down the current analytic line; 2 articulate all premises, stated and unstated; 3 challenge each ("why it 'must' be true"); 4 refine to those that "must be true", and under what conditions they would not hold | a table: assumption / assessment of confidence / what would undermine it (the DC sniper example: "The sniper is driving a white van. Possible... worthy of continuing scrutiny") |
| **Quality of Information Check** | "Evaluates completeness and soundness of available information sources"; "an ongoing, continuous process" | periodically; before a major assessment | keep a database "stored according to source type and date, with additional notations indicating strengths or weaknesses"; review all sources for accuracy; find the most critical sources; "check for sufficient and strong corroboration of critical reporting"; "reexamine previously dismissed information"; flag recalled reporting; check that ambiguous information is caveated; state confidence in sources | source register with grades and notes; "what we know" / "what we do not know" |
| **Indicators or Signposts of Change** | "Periodically review a list of observable events or trends to track events... and warn of unanticipated change" | whenever an issue is tracked over time; also to "'depersonalize' the argument" when views divide | identify competing hypotheses or scenarios; list expected observables for each; "regularly review and update the indicators lists"; judge by which indicators are changing; optionally two lists per hypothesis (is / is not emerging) | indicators matrix by period (the Primer's instability example rates each indicator per quarter from negligible to serious concern, plus "trigger mechanisms") |
| **Analysis of Competing Hypotheses** | "Identification of alternative explanations (hypotheses) and evaluation of all evidence that will disconfirm rather than confirm hypotheses" | "when there is a large amount of data"; "controversial issues when analysts want to develop a clear record"; good for deception | see 1.3 | matrix of evidence × hypotheses, C/I/N cells, weights, inconsistency score |
| **Devil's Advocacy** | "building the best possible case for an alternative explanation" | to challenge a consensus or key assumption on an issue "one cannot afford to get wrong" | outline mainline judgment and assumptions; pick the most challengeable; review information for validity, deception, gaps; present; label the product clearly as Devil's Advocate "otherwise, the reader can become confused as to the current official view" | contrarian paper |
| **Team A/Team B** | separate teams argue two strongly held views | two competing mind-sets of comparable strength | analysis phase (best case for each, missing information, explicit assumptions, evidence, logic); debate phase before "an independent 'jury of peers'" who "recommend possible next steps for further research and collection" | paired papers, debate record |
| **High-Impact/Low-Probability** | "Highlights a seemingly unlikely event that would have major policy consequences" | when an event is judged unlikely but its consequences unexamined | define the outcome; devise pathways; add triggers; indicators per pathway; factors that would deflect it | pathways with observables |
| **"What If?" Analysis** | assume the event happened; explain how | when "a judgment rests on limited information or unproven assumptions" | assume it; triggers; chain of argument; "think backwards"; pathways; indicators; consequences; monitor | scenario narrative with indicators |
| **Brainstorming** | structured divergence then convergence | start of a project and at critical points | divergent phase (focal question, notes, no censoring) then convergent (cluster, label, vote, set priorities); include an "outsider" | clustered idea list, priorities |
| **Outside-In Thinking** | the full range of external forces shaping an issue | at conceptualisation; also when designing database fields ("Often analysts realize only too late that some additional information categories will be needed and then must go back and review all previous files and recode the data") | list STEEP forces; then controllable factors; assess each; check against evidence | forces list |
| **Red Team** | replicate how the other side thinks; avoid "mirror-imaging" | forecasting an actor's behaviour | first-person questions; papers written as the actor | first-person memos |
| **Alternative Futures** | multiple ways a situation can develop | high complexity and uncertainty | focal issue; forces; pick two critical uncertainties as axes; 2×2 matrix; stories; signposts | four scenarios with signposts |

(all rows: Primer, https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf)

The Primer's **timeline** is itself a planning artefact: at the start, brainstorming, key assumptions check, outside-in thinking, high-impact/low-probability; while hypotheses form, contrarian techniques and a review of intelligence gaps; at the end, re-check assumptions, a last brainstorm "to insure that no plausible hypothesis has been dismissed", devil's advocacy if consensus has gone unchallenged, and a list of indicators. "Some techniques like Indicators and Signposts or Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) can be useful throughout a project and revisited periodically as new information is absorbed... ACH, in particular, is a good tool to use throughout a project to prevent premature closure" (Primer, Strategies for Using).

### 1.3 Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Heuer, ch. 8)

Heuer's eight steps, verbatim headings (Heuer ch. 8, https://www.crest-approved.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Psychology-of-Intelligence-Analysis-1.pdf):

1. "Identify the possible hypotheses to be considered. Use a group of analysts with different perspectives to brainstorm the possibilities."
2. "Make a list of significant evidence and arguments for and against each hypothesis."
3. "Prepare a matrix with hypotheses across the top and evidence down the side. Analyze the 'diagnosticity' of the evidence and arguments."
4. "Refine the matrix. Reconsider the hypotheses and delete evidence and arguments that have no diagnostic value."
5. "Draw tentative conclusions about the relative likelihood of each hypothesis. Proceed by trying to disprove the hypotheses rather than prove them."
6. "Analyze how sensitive your conclusion is to a few critical items of evidence."
7. "Report conclusions. Discuss the relative likelihood of all the hypotheses, not just the most likely one."
8. "Identify milestones for future observation that may indicate events are taking a different course than expected."

The working rules inside the steps are what a system must encode:

- **Unproven is not disproved.** "Early rejection of unproven, but not disproved, hypotheses biases the subsequent analysis, because one does not then look for the evidence that might support them. Unproven hypotheses should be kept alive until they can be disproved." Concealment is the standard example: "If deception is planned well and properly implemented, one should not expect to find evidence of it readily at hand."
- **Number of hypotheses**: no correct number; more with more uncertainty or impact; "More than seven hypotheses may be unmanageable"; group them for a first cut.
- **Evidence includes assumptions and deductions**, not only documents: "Such assumptions often drive your final judgment, so it is important to include them in the list of 'evidence.'"
- **Expected-evidence questioning**: "If this hypothesis is true, what should I expect to be seeing or not seeing?... If you are not seeing this evidence, why not? Is it because it has not happened, it is not normally observable, it is being concealed from you, or because you or the intelligence collectors have not looked for it?" Absence of evidence is evidence only when the evidence would be expected to be visible ("the dog did not bark").
- **Work across rows, then down columns.** Step 3 takes one item of evidence against all hypotheses; step 5 takes one hypothesis against all evidence.
- **Diagnosticity** (the medical analogy: a high temperature tells you the patient is sick, not which illness). "If an item of evidence seems consistent with all the hypotheses, it may have no diagnostic value." Optional per-cell refinements: a probability ("If this hypothesis is true, what is the probability that I would be seeing this item of evidence?"), an importance scale, and a scale of "the ease with which items of evidence could be concealed, manipulated, or faked".
- **Refutation over confirmation.** "The hypotheses with the fewest minuses is probably the most likely one... It does not follow that the hypothesis with the most pluses is the most likely one."
- **The matrix does not decide.** "You, not the matrix, must make the decision... When the matrix shows that a given hypothesis is probable or unlikely, you may disagree. If so, it is because you omitted from the matrix one or more factors... Go back and put them in." The finished matrix is "an audit trail showing how you arrived at your conclusion" and "can be used to highlight the precise area of disagreement."
- **Removed evidence is kept**: non-diagnostic items are deleted from the matrix but "Save these items in a separate list as a record of information that was considered."
- **Report all hypotheses**, and the critical assumptions conclusions depend on; "any written argument for a certain judgment is incomplete unless it also discusses alternative judgments that were considered and why they were rejected."
- **Pre-commit to milestones**: "Specifying in advance what would cause you to change your mind will also make it more difficult for you to rationalize such developments."
- **The phrase test**: "Whenever an intelligence analyst is tempted to write the phrase 'there is no evidence that ...,' the analyst should ask this question: If this hypothesis is true, can I realistically expect to see evidence of it?"
- **Honest uncertainty**: ACH "attributes greater likelihood to alternative hypotheses than would conventional analysis. One becomes less confident of what one thought one knew." Its payoff is focusing "on the few items of critical evidence that cause the uncertainty or which, if they were available, would alleviate it. This can guide future collection."

The Primer's version adds explicitly: "Ask what evidence is not being seen but would be expected for a given hypothesis to be true," and "Report all the conclusions, including the weaker hypotheses that should still be monitored as new information becomes available" (Primer, ACH). Its worked example (Tokyo sarin, 1995) shows the scoring form: evidence rows E1–E4 with weights (MEDIUM), hypothesis columns H1–H4, C/I/N cells and a weighted inconsistency score per hypothesis (−1.0, −1.0, −2.0, −3.0).
