## 1. How practitioners work, step by step

### 1.1 Intelligence analysis tradecraft: the shape of the work

The intelligence profession's own account of why structure is needed is about the mind, not the data. Heuer's core argument, repeated in the CIA's 2009 *Tradecraft Primer*: analysts "assimilate and evaluate information through the medium of 'mental models'"; mind-sets mean that "analysts perceive what they expect to perceive; once formed, they are resistant to change; new information is assimilated, sometimes erroneously, into existing mental models; and conflicting information is often dismissed or ignored" (Primer, Introduction, https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf). The Primer tabulates the biases the techniques target: expectation, resistance, ambiguity (perceptual); consistency, missing information, discredited evidence (evaluating evidence); availability, anchoring, overconfidence (probabilities); rationality and attribution (causality), the last being "Randomness, accident and error tend to be rejected as explanations for observed events" (same source). That last bias matters directly for civic questions: a delayed school rebuild is often error and accident, not scheme.

Heuer's Chapter 5 adds the finding that shapes any collection strategy: "Once an experienced analyst has the minimum information necessary to make an informed judgment, obtaining additional information generally does not improve the accuracy of his or her estimates. Additional information does, however, lead the analyst to become more confident in the judgment, to the point of overconfidence" (Heuer, *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, ch. 5, https://www.crest-approved.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Psychology-of-Intelligence-Analysis-1.pdf). He rejects the "mosaic theory" (collect every piece and the picture emerges) in favour of **medical diagnosis**: observe symptoms, generate hypotheses, run tests chosen to discriminate between them, diagnose. "Collection is focused narrowly on information that will help to discriminate the relative probability of alternate hypothesis." He cites medical students whose "research strategy stressed thorough collection of information (as opposed to formation and testing of hypotheses)" being "significantly below average" in diagnostic accuracy: "the explicit formulation of hypotheses directs a more efficient and effective search for information" (same, ch. 5). He sorts new information into four kinds: more detail on known variables; additional variables; new values of known variables; and information about which variables matter and how they relate (the mental model). Only the last two improve accuracy; the first two mostly raise confidence (same, ch. 5).

Heuer's Chapter 4 names the strategies analysts use to go beyond the data: **situational logic** (the case on its own terms, cause-effect and means-ends), **theory** (generalisation from many cases; "theory economizes thought"), **comparison/analogy** (dangerous: "the most productive uses of comparative analysis are to suggest hypotheses and to highlight differences, not to draw conclusions"), and the non-strategy of **data immersion** ("information cannot speak for itself") (Heuer ch. 4, same URL). A civic engine uses all three real strategies: situational logic (this contract, this district), theory (how public construction projects typically slip), comparison (other districts' rebuilds).

### 1.2 The Tradecraft Primer's techniques (diagnostic, contrarian, imaginative)

The Primer groups twelve techniques by purpose: "diagnostic techniques are primarily aimed at making analytic arguments, assumptions, or intelligence gaps more transparent; contrarian techniques explicitly challenge current thinking; and imaginative thinking techniques aim at developing new insights" (Primer, How to use).

| technique | purpose (Primer's words, shortened) | when | method in brief | artefact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Key Assumptions Check** | "List and review the key working assumptions on which fundamental judgments rest" | "most useful at the beginning of an analytic project"; recheck "prior to finalizing judgments" | 1 write down the current analytic line; 2 articulate all premises, stated and unstated; 3 challenge each ("why it 'must' be true"); 4 refine to those that "must be true", and under what conditions they would not hold | a table: assumption / assessment of confidence / what would undermine it (the DC sniper example: "The sniper is driving a white van. Possible... worthy of continuing scrutiny") |
| **Quality of Information Check** | "Evaluates completeness and soundness of available information sources"; "an ongoing, continuous process" | periodically; before a major assessment | keep a database "stored according to source type and date, with additional notations indicating strengths or weaknesses"; review all sources for accuracy; find the most critical sources; "check for sufficient and strong corroboration of critical reporting"; "reexamine previously dismissed information"; flag recalled reporting; check that ambiguous information is caveated; state confidence in sources | source register with grades and notes; "what we know" / "what we do not know" |
| **Indicators or Signposts of Change** | "Periodically review a list of observable events or trends to track events... and warn of unanticipated change" | whenever an issue is tracked over time; also to "'depersonalize' the argument" when views divide | identify competing hypotheses or scenarios; list expected observables for each; "regularly review and update the indicators lists"; judge by which indicators are changing; optionally two lists per hypothesis (is / is not emerging) | indicators matrix by period (the Primer's instability example rates each indicator per quarter from negligible to serious concern, plus "trigger mechanisms") |
| **Analysis of Competing Hypotheses** | "Identification of alternative explanations (hypotheses) and evaluation of all evidence that will disconfirm rather than confirm hypotheses" | "when there is a large amount of data"; "controversial issues when analysts want to develop a clear record"; good for deception | see 1.3 | matrix of evidence × hypotheses, C/I/N cells, weights, inconsistency score |
| **Devil's Advocacy** | "building the best possible case for an alternative explanation" | to challenge a consensus or key assumption on an issue "one cannot afford to get wrong" | outline mainline judgment and assumptions; pick the most challengeable; review information for validity, deception, gaps; present; label the product clearly as Devil's Advocate "otherwise, the reader can become confused as to the current official view" | contrarian paper |
| **Team A/Team B** | separate teams argue two strongly held views | two competing mind-sets of comparable strength | analysis phase (best case for each, missing information, explicit assumptions, evidence, logic); debate phase before "an independent 'jury of peers'" who "recommend possible next steps for further research and collection" | paired papers, debate record |
| **High-Impact/Low-Probability** | "Highlights a seemingly unlikely event that would have major policy consequences" | when an event is judged unlikely but its consequences unexamined | define the outcome; devise pathways; add triggers; indicators per pathway; factors that would deflect it | pathways with observables |
| **"What If?" Analysis** | assume the event happened; explain how | when "a judgment rests on limited information or unproven assumptions" | assume it; triggers; chain of argument; "think backwards"; pathways; indicators; consequences; monitor | scenario narrative with indicators |
| **Brainstorming** | structured divergence then convergence | start of a project and at critical points | divergent phase (focal question, notes, no censoring) then convergent (cluster, label, vote, set priorities); include an "outsider" | clustered idea list, priorities |
| **Outside-In Thinking** | the full range of external forces shaping an issue | at conceptualisation; also when designing database fields ("Often analysts realize only too late that some additional information categories will be needed and then must go back and review all previous files and recode the data") | list STEEP forces; then controllable factors; assess each; check against evidence | forces list |
| **Red Team** | replicate how the other side thinks; avoid "mirror-imaging" | forecasting an actor's behaviour | first-person questions; papers written as the actor | first-person memos |
| **Alternative Futures** | multiple ways a situation can develop | high complexity and uncertainty | focal issue; forces; pick two critical uncertainties as axes; 2×2 matrix; stories; signposts | four scenarios with signposts |

(all rows: Primer, https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Tradecraft-Primer-apr09.pdf)

The Primer's **timeline** is itself a planning artefact: at the start, brainstorming, key assumptions check, outside-in thinking, high-impact/low-probability; while hypotheses form, contrarian techniques and a review of intelligence gaps; at the end, re-check assumptions, a last brainstorm "to insure that no plausible hypothesis has been dismissed", devil's advocacy if consensus has gone unchallenged, and a list of indicators. "Some techniques like Indicators and Signposts or Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) can be useful throughout a project and revisited periodically as new information is absorbed... ACH, in particular, is a good tool to use throughout a project to prevent premature closure" (Primer, Strategies for Using).

### 1.3 Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (Heuer, ch. 8)

Heuer's eight steps, verbatim headings (Heuer ch. 8, https://www.crest-approved.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Psychology-of-Intelligence-Analysis-1.pdf):

1. "Identify the possible hypotheses to be considered. Use a group of analysts with different perspectives to brainstorm the possibilities."
2. "Make a list of significant evidence and arguments for and against each hypothesis."
3. "Prepare a matrix with hypotheses across the top and evidence down the side. Analyze the 'diagnosticity' of the evidence and arguments."
4. "Refine the matrix. Reconsider the hypotheses and delete evidence and arguments that have no diagnostic value."
5. "Draw tentative conclusions about the relative likelihood of each hypothesis. Proceed by trying to disprove the hypotheses rather than prove them."
6. "Analyze how sensitive your conclusion is to a few critical items of evidence."
7. "Report conclusions. Discuss the relative likelihood of all the hypotheses, not just the most likely one."
8. "Identify milestones for future observation that may indicate events are taking a different course than expected."

The working rules inside the steps are what a system must encode:

- **Unproven is not disproved.** "Early rejection of unproven, but not disproved, hypotheses biases the subsequent analysis, because one does not then look for the evidence that might support them. Unproven hypotheses should be kept alive until they can be disproved." Concealment is the standard example: "If deception is planned well and properly implemented, one should not expect to find evidence of it readily at hand."
- **Number of hypotheses**: no correct number; more with more uncertainty or impact; "More than seven hypotheses may be unmanageable"; group them for a first cut.
- **Evidence includes assumptions and deductions**, not only documents: "Such assumptions often drive your final judgment, so it is important to include them in the list of 'evidence.'"
- **Expected-evidence questioning**: "If this hypothesis is true, what should I expect to be seeing or not seeing?... If you are not seeing this evidence, why not? Is it because it has not happened, it is not normally observable, it is being concealed from you, or because you or the intelligence collectors have not looked for it?" Absence of evidence is evidence only when the evidence would be expected to be visible ("the dog did not bark").
- **Work across rows, then down columns.** Step 3 takes one item of evidence against all hypotheses; step 5 takes one hypothesis against all evidence.
- **Diagnosticity** (the medical analogy: a high temperature tells you the patient is sick, not which illness). "If an item of evidence seems consistent with all the hypotheses, it may have no diagnostic value." Optional per-cell refinements: a probability ("If this hypothesis is true, what is the probability that I would be seeing this item of evidence?"), an importance scale, and a scale of "the ease with which items of evidence could be concealed, manipulated, or faked".
- **Refutation over confirmation.** "The hypotheses with the fewest minuses is probably the most likely one... It does not follow that the hypothesis with the most pluses is the most likely one."
- **The matrix does not decide.** "You, not the matrix, must make the decision... When the matrix shows that a given hypothesis is probable or unlikely, you may disagree. If so, it is because you omitted from the matrix one or more factors... Go back and put them in." The finished matrix is "an audit trail showing how you arrived at your conclusion" and "can be used to highlight the precise area of disagreement."
- **Removed evidence is kept**: non-diagnostic items are deleted from the matrix but "Save these items in a separate list as a record of information that was considered."
- **Report all hypotheses**, and the critical assumptions conclusions depend on; "any written argument for a certain judgment is incomplete unless it also discusses alternative judgments that were considered and why they were rejected."
- **Pre-commit to milestones**: "Specifying in advance what would cause you to change your mind will also make it more difficult for you to rationalize such developments."
- **The phrase test**: "Whenever an intelligence analyst is tempted to write the phrase 'there is no evidence that ...,' the analyst should ask this question: If this hypothesis is true, can I realistically expect to see evidence of it?"
- **Honest uncertainty**: ACH "attributes greater likelihood to alternative hypotheses than would conventional analysis. One becomes less confident of what one thought one knew." Its payoff is focusing "on the few items of critical evidence that cause the uncertainty or which, if they were available, would alleviate it. This can guide future collection."

The Primer's version adds explicitly: "Ask what evidence is not being seen but would be expected for a given hypothesis to be true," and "Report all the conclusions, including the weaker hypotheses that should still be monitored as new information becomes available" (Primer, ACH). Its worked example (Tokyo sarin, 1995) shows the scoring form: evidence rows E1–E4 with weights (MEDIUM), hypothesis columns H1–H4, C/I/N cells and a weighted inconsistency score per hypothesis (−1.0, −1.0, −2.0, −3.0).


### 1.4 The Pirolli-Card sensemaking loop

Pirolli and Card's cognitive task analysis of all-source intelligence analysts (PARC, 2005) is the most-cited descriptive model of how analysts actually turn documents into findings, and the one closest to a software architecture (Pirolli & Card 2005, https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Pirolli,%20Card%20-%202005%20-%20The%20sensemaking%20process%20and%20leverage%20points%20for%20analyst%20technology%20as.pdf).

**The data flow** (their Figure 2), from raw to reported, with rising structure and effort:

| stage | what it is (their words, shortened) |
|---|---|
| External data sources | "the raw evidence, largely text" |
| Shoebox | "the much smaller subset of that external data that is relevant for processing" |
| Evidence file | "snippets extracted from items in the shoebox" |
| Schema | "the re-representation or organized marshalling of the information so that it can be used more easily to draw conclusions" (a timeline, a who/what/when/where/why/how table, a link chart) |
| Hypotheses | "the tentative representation of those conclusions with supporting arguments" |
| Presentation | the work product |

**The processes**, in two loops. The *foraging loop* (search and filter → read and extract) "involves processes aimed at seeking information, searching and filtering it, and reading and extracting information... possibly into some schema"; the *sensemaking loop* (schematize → build case → tell story) "involves iterative development of a mental model (a conceptualization) from the schema that best fits the evidence." Every forward step has a backward, top-down partner: **re-evaluate** (client feedback reopens the theory), **search for support** (re-examine the schema), **search for evidence** (re-examine the evidence file or search anew), **search for relations** ("Nuggets of information in an evidence file may suggest new patterns (e.g., people linked to other people) that generate hypotheses... These hypotheses may generate new searches"), **search for information** ("New hypotheses... may cause the analyst to dig deeper in the raw data"). "Top-down and bottom-up processes are invoked in an opportunistic mix", with "lots of back loops". The compact formula: "Information → Schema → Insight → Product." The authors also note that "much day-to-day intelligence mainly consists of extracting information and repackaging it without much actual analysis" (same source).

**Expertise is schemas.** Experts carry domain schemas (market players, report types; for an analyst predicting a coup, "a large collection of descriptions of people and organizations") into which incoming information is re-represented; for an expert "it can actually be the lack of expected features of a situation that can trigger sensemaking" (same source, §2.1). Behind this sits Russell et al.'s "learning loop complex": search for a representation, encode data into it, notice the **residue** that does not fit, and shift the representation (same source, Figure 1).

**Leverage points** the authors name for technology (same source, Leverage Points):

- Foraging: the **exploration–enrichment–exploitation trade-off** (widen recall; narrow to a high-precision set; read deeply), and its cost structure; the cost of scanning and assessing relevance (helped by highlighting entities and by "rerepresenting documents (e.g., by summaries) appropriate to the task"); the cost of shifting attention to a new task; the cost of follow-up searches. Experts "set their filters for information lower (thereby accepting more irrelevant information)" because they reject noise quickly.
- Sensemaking: **span of attention** for evidence and hypotheses ("Reasoning about evidence and hypotheses has an exponential cost structure"; offload to external representations); **generation of alternative hypotheses** ("people typically fail to generate hypotheses"); **confirmation bias** ("People typically fail to consider the diagnosticity of evidence... A leverage point for new tools is to distribute more attention of the analyst to highly diagnostic evidence and to the search for disconfirming relations").

The model has been carried into other fields: a 2025 study builds it into bespoke tools for digital history, treating the shoebox / evidence file / schema stages as tool features (https://www.redalyc.org/journal/589/58980067002/html/; search-result abstract only, not read in full).

### 1.5 Evidence grading: Admiralty/NATO, UK 3x5x2, US analytic standards

**The Admiralty (NATO) system** grades every report on two independent axes (Admiralty code, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_code, citing NATO AJP-2.1 and US FM 2-22.3):

| source reliability | definition | information credibility | definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Completely reliable | "No doubt of authenticity, trustworthiness, or competency; has a history of complete reliability" | 1 Confirmed by other sources | "Confirmed by other independent sources; logical in itself; Consistent with other information" |
| B Usually reliable | "Minor doubt...; has a history of valid information most of the time" | 2 Probably true | "Not confirmed; logical in itself; consistent with other information on the subject" |
| C Fairly reliable | "Doubt...but has provided valid information in the past" | 3 Possibly true | "Not confirmed; reasonably logical in itself; agrees with some other information" |
| D Not usually reliable | "Significant doubt...but has provided valid information in the past" | 4 Doubtful | "Not confirmed; possible but not logical; no other information on the subject" |
| E Unreliable | "Lacking in authenticity, trustworthiness, and competency; history of invalid information" | 5 Improbable | "Not confirmed; not logical in itself; contradicted by other information" |
| F Reliability cannot be judged | "No basis exists for evaluating the reliability of the source" | 6 Truth cannot be judged | "No basis exists for evaluating the validity of the information" |

The rule that matters: "each descriptor is considered in isolation to ensure that the reliability of the source does not influence the assessed accuracy of the report" (same source). A report is graded e.g. "B2". F and 6 are honest "unknown" values, not low grades.

**UK police 3x5x2** (College of Policing, intelligence report guidance, replacing 5x5x5 from about 2016): three axes (https://pdacounterfraud.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/College-of-Policing-3x5x2-Intelligence-Report-Guidance.pdf, read whole):

- **Source evaluation**: 1 Reliable ("believed to be competent and information received is generally reliable"); 2 Untested ("has not previously provided information... or has provided information that has not been substantiated. The source may not necessarily be unreliable... corroboration should be considered"); 3 Not reliable ("reasonable grounds to doubt... authenticity, trustworthiness, competence or motive... or confidence in the technical equipment"; the grounds "should be specified").
- **Information/intelligence assessment**, which is about *how the source came to know it*: A Known directly to the source ("first-hand... Care must be taken to differentiate between what a source witnessed themselves and what a source has been told"); B Known indirectly but corroborated; C Known indirectly; D Not known ("no means of assessing"); E Suspected to be false (rationale must be documented).
- **Handling code**: P (lawful sharing permitted) or C (permitted with conditions, e.g. covert development only; sanitisation by delegated authority or only after consulting the originator); conditional reports "should remain under review to ensure that wider dissemination can occur as soon as is feasible."
- Content rules: write to "5WH, i.e. what, when, where, why, who and how"; "understood without the need to refer to other information sources"; record the research done to corroborate and "the databases that have been researched"; "Items of information from the same source but concerning totally different matters should be recorded on separate IRs"; each report gets a unique reference number "to provide an audit trail"; a sanitised version gets its own number "cross-reference[d] to the original" (same source).

A comparative review of police grading schemes (Netherlands 4x4x4 and its successor, UK 5x5x5 and 3x5x2, Europol 4x4) finds recurring defects: **variable conflation** (one grade mixing accuracy, the source's access, the relaying officer's knowledge and corroboration), **undervalued corroboration** (distance-based grades ignore triangulation), inconsistent top-grade wording, and haphazard roll-out; the Dutch redesign grades information by *distance from origin* on the reasoning that "Every transfer of information most likely will – intentionally or not – change the content"; the author suggests open-source material may need a different scheme from one built to protect confidential human sources (https://www.blockint.nl/methods/law-enforcement-use-of-information-grading-systems/).

**US Intelligence Community analytic standards (ICD 203, 2015, amended 2022)** govern the *product* rather than the report (https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-203.pdf, read whole). Five standards (objective; independent of political consideration; timely; based on all available sources; exhibits the tradecraft standards) and nine tradecraft standards, of which these bind any graded-evidence system:

1. "Properly describes quality and credibility of underlying sources, data, and methodologies" (factors: "accuracy and completeness, possible denial and deception, age and continued currency of information... source access, validation, motivation, possible bias, or expertise"; a **source summary statement** "explain[s] which sources are most important to key analytic judgments").
2. "Properly expresses and explains uncertainties", separating **likelihood** of an event from **confidence** in the basis for the judgment, with a fixed scale: almost no chance (01–05%), very unlikely (05–20%), unlikely (20–45%), roughly even chance (45–55%), likely (55–80%), very likely (80–95%), almost certain (95–99%); and "must not combine a confidence level and a degree of likelihood... in the same sentence."
3. "Properly distinguishes between underlying intelligence information and analysts' assumptions and judgments" ("Products should state assumptions explicitly when they serve as the linchpin of an argument or when they bridge key information gaps").
4. "Incorporates analysis of alternatives", with indicators "that, if detected, would affect the likelihood of identified alternatives".
5. Customer relevance and implications. 6. "Uses clear and logical argumentation" (main message up front; "acknowledge significant supporting and contrary information"). 7. "Explains change to or consistency of analytic judgments" ("make clear how new information or different reasoning led to the judgments"). 8. "Makes accurate judgments" ("should not avoid difficult judgments in order to minimize the risk of being wrong"). 9. Effective visual information.

The standards also restrict personal data: analysts "should include PII in products only as it relates to a specific analytic purpose" (same source, D.5).

### 1.6 Argument mapping and evidence charts

**Argument mapping** puts the claim in a box at the top and arranges reasons, objections, co-premises and rebuttals beneath it as a tree, so that each box can be judged on its own. Pherson and Heuer's eight-step recipe (as quoted by Johansson 2024): write the lead hypothesis as one sentence; list key reasons and objections beneath; link reasons (green); link counter-evidence to the reasons it contradicts (red); link objections to the claim (red); link evidence for the objections; add rebuttals (orange), since "An objection, challenge, or counterevidence that does not have an orange-line rebuttal suggests a flaw in the argument"; evaluate for clarity and completeness (Johansson, "Argument Mapping in Intelligence Analysis", *Security and Intelligence* 9(2), 2024, https://gsis.scholasticahq.com/article/125995-argument-mapping-in-intelligence-analysis.pdf, read whole). Claimed benefits: it shows exactly where disagreement sits, exposes hidden assumptions and gaps, separates facts, assumptions, value judgments and tentative judgments, and helps keep cause and correlation apart (same source, citing Pherson & Heuer 2021). Johansson's own corrections: **one main claim per map**, never itself a reason for something else; competing hypotheses belong in **separate maps**, chosen first by ACH or Bayesian analysis ("ACH and Bayesian analysis should be performed before the argument map"); and putting numbers on every box "might produce misleading results", since boxes hold different kinds of things (facts, value judgments, testimony). Tecuci, Schum et al.: "All evidence has three major credentials or properties: relevance, believability or credibility, and inferential force or weight"; and "we have evidence in search of hypotheses, hypotheses in search of evidence, and the testing of hypotheses all going on at the same time" (quoted in Johansson 2024). Software that computes support up the tree (e.g. Uncharted's Argument Mapper, grading relevance and credibility "no support, very low, low, medium, high and very high") exists; Johansson warns such numbers can give "a false sense of confidence" when relevance arguments are not made explicit (citing Tecuci et al. 2009).

Van Gelder's critique of ACH from the argument-mapping side, as summarised on Wikipedia: ACH demands too many discrete judgments, "mischaracteriz[es]... evidence-hypothesis relationships", treats hypotheses as a flat list rather than a hierarchy, cannot represent the argument behind an item of evidence, and disorients at real scale (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_competing_hypotheses).

**Legal evidence charts.** Wigmore's chart method (1913), revived by Anderson, Twining and Schum, builds an inference network from a "key list" of propositions: the ultimate hypothesis, interim propositions, and each item of evidence, with the inferential steps between them made explicit. A 2024 comparison on the Kercher murder case sets three graphical methods side by side and finds each suits a different audience: "Wigmore charts graph arguments; Bayesian networks depict items evidence, propositions and the relationship among them; Chain event graphs depict time dependent actions and stories", useful respectively to lawyers, forensic scientists and police. A Wigmore chart encodes, for "X says Y about Z": "Is X in general truthful? Was X in a position to know Y about Z? Is there contrary evidence...? Is there other supporting evidence...? ...What inferential steps must be accepted to proceed from X's testimony to the conclusion?" The authors note that a full chart of a real case "would be very large and unwieldy" and should be broken "into a number of separate modules, loosely connected"; and that extracting propositions from court records "is an arduous task... a custom-made tool would have been invaluable, at least to generate starting points for a supervised method" (Dawid et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.16628; abstract, §1–2.2 and §9 conclusions read).

### 1.7 How investigators plan: objectives, lines of enquiry, requirements, re-planning

**Police investigative doctrine.** The UK's ACPO *Core Investigative Doctrine* (2005) defines the **investigative mindset** as "a disciplined approach that ensures the decisions made are appropriate to the case, are reasonable and can be explained to others", built of understanding the source of material, planning and preparation, examination, recording and collation, and evaluation (quoted in College of Policing, *Conducting effective investigations: rapid evidence assessment*, 2022, https://assets.college.police.uk/s3fs-public/2022-07/Conducting-effective-investigations-rapid-evidence-assessment.pdf; the doctrine PDF itself was unreachable, see Sources). The resulting guideline asks investigators "to follow all reasonable lines of enquiry, be professionally curious, ask questions" and to be aware of their own biases (same source, open-mindedness discussion).

The Australian Federal Police *Investigations Doctrine* sets out the full planning cycle (https://www.afp.gov.au/sites/default/files/PDF/IPS/AFP%20Investigations%20Doctrine.pdf, ch. 3–5 read):

| stage | what happens | artefact |
|---|---|---|
| Initiation | report, response, intelligence-led, or spun off from another investigation ("One investigation will often initiate another") | record of the matter |
| Evaluation | decide whether to investigate, refer, record without investigating, or reject; assign priority; consider "what investigative strategy may yield the optimal outcome with respect to the impact, cost and time balance"; "regardless of the result of the evaluation, decisions should be recorded" | evaluation record: matter, how initiated, decision, "context and reasons" |
| Planning | "organised around an Investigation Plan"; an **investigative strategy** "that gives all activity a coherent and uniform sense of purpose and direction. It is by reference to the investigative strategy that all taskings should be derived, explained, sequenced and justified"; identify outcome(s), scope, risks, partners, impact on victims and community; "Templates are not a substitute for the application of critical thinking" | plan, scalable: "Not all investigations will require... a formalised Investigation Plan. However, in all cases, a written record of the planning process should be created" |
| Phases and milestones | "Each phase should have a specific measurable objective... A phase should also be time bound. Inability to achieve the objective within the timeframe should prompt re-evaluation of whether further investment in a specific avenue of inquiry is justified"; milestones are "opportunities to review... and possibly to re-evaluate and/or re-plan" | phase objectives, milestone reviews |
| Investigative activity | organised against the elements to be proven; "explore possible sources of inculpatory and exculpatory evidence to challenge the strength of any investigative hypotheses"; **avenues of inquiry** are "the sources of information from which investigators seek to derive... evidence... Each investigation will need to be considered on its own merits to determine and sequence the appropriate avenues"; "A single avenue of inquiry will rarely yield all of the evidence"; "Following the money" is named as a highly effective strategy | task list per avenue |
| Records | "Any information management system used needs to be able to record information and identify sources, credibility and any assessment of its truth and relevance"; record decisions "and the context and reason for these decisions"; "if a concluded investigation can be re-opened in the future, the investigative records will be the starting point" | information store; decision log |
| Outcome | prevention, disruption, prosecution, or inquiry; several intermediate outcomes are possible | brief, referral |
| Finalisation | can happen at any stage; may be re-opened "on the basis of new information" | archived file |
| Review (throughout) | self, peer, supervisory, formal internal, formal external; scope can include "past and potential investigative strategies" and "the balance between the impact, cost and time" | review record |

"Planning is not a discrete activity that is only conducted at the beginning of an investigation, it is a continuous process... Investigators should regularly review the investigative and supporting strategies to ensure that their assumptions and basic approach remains valid" (AFP doctrine, ch. 4). Decision-making follows define the situation → choice → justification → action and review → record → communicate; "A decision should be judged on the quality of the decision-making, not on the outcome", and victims "need to be kept advised of progress" (same, ch. 5).

**Intelligence collection planning** decomposes questions top-down. US Army doctrine breaks a **priority intelligence requirement (PIR)** or information requirement into **indicators** ("information reflecting an adversary's intention or capability regarding a specific course of action") and then **specific information requirements (SIRs)**, which "specify the information required, based on an indicator, within a specific time and location", and SIRs become tasks for collectors (https://www.army.mil/article/285410/priority_intelligence_requirement_management_in_divisions_and_corps). ATP 2-01 (as summarised in practitioner notes) requires each requirement to state who needs it, what, where, when, why, and a **latest time information is of value (LTIOV)**, reverse-planned from the decision it serves; it validates each requirement for **necessity** (first "check databases to see if someone has already collected the information"), **feasibility** and **completeness**; it screens returns for relevance, completeness and timeliness, correlates "reporting to requirements", and **retires** satisfied or obsolete requirements so "the staff [can] redirect assets to unfulfilled requirements" (https://arielsheen.com/index.php/2021/10/14/notes-from-plan-requirements-and-assess-collection-atp-2-01/, first 100,000 characters read). Older doctrine (FM 34-2) insists the collection plan "has no prescribed doctrinal format", should "Be a working document", "Cover only priority requirements", and that "When the collection requirement is satisfied, the card is removed" (https://irp.fas.org/doddir/army/fm34-2/Appa.htm). Recorded failures: "Underdeveloped PIRs, poor G2 synchronization, and infrequent PIR updates" (army.mil, same).

**Journalism's hypothesis method** gives the civic-scale version of the same decomposition. Story-Based Inquiry (Hunter et al., UNESCO 2011): state the story as a hypothesis of no more than three sentences (the present harm; how it came about; what happens if nothing changes); "take apart the hypothesis and see what separate, specific claims it makes. Then, we can verify each of those claims in turn"; define each term ("What exactly do we mean by 'corruption?'"); start where it is easiest; the hypothesis makes the project manageable, with a **worst case** (quickly shown false, project ended cheaply), a **minimum** (true and quickly verified) and a **maximum** (true, and others "must logically follow"). And the warning: "to make the world worse, all you need to do is leave out the facts that disprove your hypothesis... Try to disprove them as well as prove them"; "neither cling too hard to a mistaken hypothesis, nor leap in a new direction at the first contrary fact. The best sign that something is wrong comes when you are finding a fair amount of information, but it doesn't make sense" (https://bird.tools/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/SBI_Manual.pdf, ch. 2 read). The manual also uses "the official version as a hypothesis", a natural starting point for Bob's school question (the district's stated reasons for delay).

**Re-planning as evidence arrives**, across all of these, has the same triggers: a milestone or phase deadline (AFP), a satisfied or obsolete requirement (ATP 2-01), an indicator changing state (Primer), a diagnostic item of evidence or residue that does not fit the schema (Heuer step 6; Pirolli-Card), and client or reviewer feedback (Pirolli-Card "re-evaluate"). The College of Policing's evidence review adds why re-planning must be deliberate: investigators pass **decisional "tipping points"** (choosing main strategies and lines of enquiry; naming a suspect) at which they move "from a deliberative or open frame of mind to an implemental mindset", with "significant drops in creativity and open-mindedness" (REA 2022, citing Fahsing & Ask and Gollwitzer).

What the "case file", "lead", "plan" and "document log" are in this tradition, gathered from the sources above. Field lists are the practitioners' own where quoted; the composite at the end is *(inference)*.

**Tools.** PARC ACH 2.0 (built with Heuer; analysts "enter evidence and rate its credibility and relevance"), and open-source and spreadsheet ACH templates with weighted inconsistency counting; collaborative and Bayesian variants (CACHE, Veriluma/subjective logic) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_of_competing_hypotheses). Argument-mapping software (Rationale; Uncharted's Argument Mapper) (Johansson 2024). Bayesian networks and chain event graphs for evidence (Dawid et al. 2024). Link-chart and timeline tools as schema aids are named generically by Pirolli and Card.

**A composite model** *(inference, drawn from the rows above)*. Across the traditions the same small set of objects recurs, and an engine could hold exactly these:

1. **Question/objective**, decomposed into **requirements** (each: what, why it matters to the objective, by when it is of value, status open/satisfied/retired).
2. **Hypotheses** (≤7 live at once, each with status live / unlikely / disproved, never deleted, with the reason for any change).
3. **Assumptions** (each with confidence, what would undermine it, and which hypotheses or judgments rest on it).
4. **Sources** (each with a reliability grade and the basis for it) and **documents** (the shoebox, each with provenance and why it was collected).
5. **Extracts** (evidence-file items: the passage, its location in the document, the low-level inference drawn, an information grade, and how the source knew it: direct, indirect, indirect-corroborated, unknown, suspected false).
6. **Evidence-to-hypothesis ratings** (C/I/N or likelihood, with a one-line reason), from which diagnosticity is read.
7. **Indicators** (observable, expected under which hypothesis, current state, last checked).
8. **Lines of enquiry / tasks**, each derived from a requirement and a hypothesis.
9. **Decisions and reviews** (decision log), and **judgments** (claim, likelihood term, confidence and basis, linchpin assumptions, alternatives, change from last time).

## 3. What failed, or is warned against

| failure | evidence | source |
|---|---|---|
| **Satisficing / premature closure**: picking the first hypothesis that fits, then looking only for support | "Satisficing means picking the first solution that seems satisfactory"; most supporting evidence is also "consistent with other explanations" | Heuer ch. 8 |
| **Rejecting the unproven as if disproved** | "Early rejection of unproven, but not disproved, hypotheses biases the subsequent analysis"; the 1998 Indian test: "there was no indication" confused unproven with disproved | Heuer ch. 8 |
| **"No evidence of X" without asking whether X would be visible** | the "there is no evidence that..." test | Heuer ch. 8 |
| **More information ≠ more accuracy** | handicappers' accuracy flat from 5 to 40 variables while confidence rose; thorough collectors among medical students diagnosed worse | Heuer ch. 5 |
| **Mosaic theory** (collect all the pieces and the picture appears) | "analysts typically form a picture first and then select the pieces to fit" | Heuer ch. 5 |
| **Unchallenged key assumptions** | the Primer's list of strategic surprises (Pearl Harbor, Yalu, Cuba, Yom Kippur, Iraq WMD); DC sniper "white van" | Primer |
| **Over-reliance on a single ambiguous source; caveats forgotten** | Iraq WMD commission: "over-reliance on a single, ambiguous source"; judgments "anchored to weak information, and any 'caveats'... forgotten or ignored over time" | Primer, QoI |
| **Multiple sources mistaken for good sources** | "Having multiple sources on an issue is not a substitute for having good information"; grading schemes undervalue genuine triangulation and confuse distance-from-origin with corroboration | Primer; blockint.nl review |
| **Conflating source reliability with information credibility** | the Admiralty code's independence rule; Europol 4x4 "mix[es] three (or four) different elements" | Wikipedia; blockint.nl |
| **ACH done mechanically** | trained analysts "did not follow all of the steps"; ACH users identified diagnostic information better (r = .68 vs .17) yet were slightly less accurate and less coherent than controls; a systematic review found ACH has "little to no benefit... and may even harm it"; coherentizing and averaging judgments of several analysts cut error by up to 61% | Mandel, Karvetski & Dhami 2018, https://jbaron.org/journal/18/18803/jdm18803.html; search-result abstracts for Dhami et al. 2019 and the 2024 critical review |
| **ACH's structure itself** | too many discrete judgments; flat hypothesis lists; no way to show the argument behind an evidence item; disorienting at scale; static snapshot that misses time; assumes passive, not deceptive, evidence | Wikipedia (van Gelder; deception critics) |
| **False precision** | numbers on every argument-map box "might produce misleading results"; Good's numeric extension of ACH "may provide analysts with a false sense of confidence" | Johansson 2024 (citing Tecuci et al.) |
| **Mixing likelihood and confidence** | ICD 203 forbids the two in one sentence | ICD 203 |
| **Confirmation bias in investigators, measurably** | initial belief in guilt predicted evaluations of ambiguous evidence and final belief ("bias snowball"); witness evidence against the main hypothesis judged less reliable; need for closure increases it | College of Policing REA 2022 |
| **Tipping points into an implemental mindset** | at choice of main lines of enquiry and at naming a suspect | College of Policing REA 2022 |
| **Pressure to move on; resource decisions driven by solvability** | detectives' effort follows "the certainty (or uncertainty) of a successful case resolution", not seriousness | College of Policing REA 2022 |
| **Templates instead of thinking** | "Templates are not a substitute for the application of critical thinking" | AFP doctrine |
| **Requirements that never change** | "infrequent PIR updates"; requirements delegated to one cell | army.mil PIR article |
| **Pursuing a subject instead of a story** | "'I want to investigate corruption' is not a great proposition"; a subject burns "time and money for a very uncertain result" | Story-Based Inquiry ch. 2 |
| **Leaving out disconfirming facts** | "to make the world worse, all you need to do is leave out the facts that disprove your hypothesis" | Story-Based Inquiry ch. 2 |
| **Data overload** | analysts narrow from high-recall to high-precision sets and miss relevant items outside the narrowed set; costs of attention switching and follow-up searches | Pirolli & Card 2005 |
| **Unwieldy whole-case graphs** | a full Wigmore/BN representation "would be very large and unwieldy"; modularise | Dawid et al. 2024 |
| **Rationality bias** | "Randomness, accident and error tend to be rejected as explanations" | Primer |

## 4. Lessons for a member-run, AI-assisted, evidence-graded civic investigation system

Each lesson is tied to its source. Where a lesson names a product idea it is *(inference)*; the product itself is studied in `notes/`, not here.

1. **Plan from hypotheses, not from a document wish-list.** Collection should be "focused narrowly on information that will help to discriminate" between hypotheses (Heuer ch. 5); hypothesis-driven search beats thorough collection (Heuer ch. 5, medical students). For Bob's school: the engine's first plan is a short set of competing explanations of the slip (e.g. scope change after award; contractor performance; funding or bond timing; permitting/inspection hold-ups; an unrealistic original promise; site conditions), each with the documents that would discriminate between them, rather than "fetch the RFP, the bids, the contract, every report" *(inference, applying Heuer)*.

2. **Keep 3–7 hypotheses alive, including the boring ones; never silently drop one.** Unproven ≠ disproved; >7 is unmanageable (Heuer ch. 8). Include accident and error, which people under-weight (Primer, rationality bias). A hypothesis's status changes only with a recorded reason *(inference, from Heuer step 4's "save these items in a separate list")*.

3. **Ask, per hypothesis, "what would I expect to see, and would it be visible to us?"** (Heuer ch. 8). This single question generates both the lines of enquiry and the honest interpretation of a gap: a missing change-order log may be "not normally published", "withheld", or "not yet requested", and the engine should say which *(inference)*.

4. **Rate evidence across all hypotheses, and surface diagnosticity to the member.** The machine's distinctive contribution is the matrix work humans skip (Pirolli & Card: span of attention; confirmation bias as leverage points). Show the few items that actually discriminate; mark items consistent with everything as non-diagnostic, but keep them (Heuer steps 3–4).

5. **Do not let the matrix decide, and do not trust ACH to debias by itself.** "You, not the matrix, must make the decision" (Heuer ch. 8); experimental evidence shows ACH alone does not improve accuracy and can hurt coherence, while aggregating several independent judgments and enforcing probability coherence helps a great deal (Mandel et al. 2018). For a member-in-the-loop system this argues for: the engine proposes ratings with one-line reasons; the member accepts or changes them; independent second opinions (another run, another member) are combined rather than one opinion being trusted *(inference)*.

6. **Grade source and information on separate axes, with "cannot be judged" as an honest value.** Admiralty's independence rule; F and 6 (Wikipedia). For public records, how the source knows matters more than track record: a district's own progress report is first-hand about what the district says and second-hand about site reality. The 3x5x2 information axis (direct / indirect-corroborated / indirect / not known / suspected false) fits documents well (College of Policing guidance); open-source material may need its own scheme (blockint.nl review).

7. **Corroboration means independent origin, not count.** "Having multiple sources... is not a substitute for having good information" (Primer); news stories repeating one press release are one source. The engine should trace extracts to their origin before calling something corroborated *(inference, from Primer and the Dutch distance-from-origin rationale)*.

8. **Write extracts so they stand alone, with provenance, one matter each.** 5WH; "understood without the need to refer to other information sources"; separate reports for separate matters; unique references and cross-referenced derived copies (College of Policing). This is the evidence-file discipline of Pirolli and Card, and it is what lets a later reader (or a later run) re-use an extract *(inference)*.

9. **Make assumptions first-class objects, checked at the start and again before any finding.** Key Assumptions Check timing (Primer); ICD 203 requires linchpin assumptions to be stated with what happens if they are wrong. For the school case: "the one-school-year promise was a contractual commitment" is an assumption to check, not a fact (it may have been a board aspiration) *(inference)*.

10. **Decompose the member's question into requirements with a "by when", and retire them.** PIR → indicators → SIRs → tasks; who/what/where/when/why; LTIOV; necessity check (has it already been collected?), feasibility, completeness; retire when satisfied (ATP 2-01 notes; army.mil). Civic analogues of LTIOV are real: a board meeting, a bond vote, a public-comment deadline, a records-request response window *(inference)*.

11. **Plan in phases with measurable, time-bound objectives; review at milestones; record every decision with its reason.** "Inability to achieve the objective within the timeframe should prompt re-evaluation" (AFP ch. 4); a written planning record is required even when no formal plan is (AFP ch. 4); decisions judged "on the quality of the decision-making, not on the outcome" (AFP ch. 5). In a member-run system the decision log is where the member's acceptances and rejections live *(inference)*.

12. **Make re-planning event-driven.** Triggers: a milestone; a requirement satisfied or obsolete; an indicator changing; a diagnostic item arriving or a residue that does not fit; a member's challenge (AFP; ATP 2-01; Primer; Heuer step 6; Pirolli & Card). The engine re-ranks hypotheses and regenerates lines of enquiry on those events, not on a schedule alone *(inference)*.

13. **Guard the tipping points.** Choosing main lines of enquiry and naming a responsible party are where open minds close (College of Policing REA). At those two moments the engine should require a contrarian pass (devil's advocacy or a key assumptions re-check) before the member accepts (Primer timeline) *(inference)*.

14. **Pre-commit to indicators.** State in advance what would change the conclusion (Heuer step 8; ICD 203). For an ongoing civic matter (a project still unfinished) indicators become the watch list after the first answer: e.g. a change order over a threshold, a new completion date in board minutes, a stop-work notice *(inference)*.

15. **Report all hypotheses, likelihood and confidence separately, with the source summary.** ICD 203 tradecraft standards 1–4 and its seven-step likelihood scale; Heuer step 7. A member-facing answer can use the same discipline in plain words: what we think happened, how likely, how sure we are and why, what else could explain it, what we could not see *(inference)*.

16. **Model the work as Pirolli-Card's pipeline and put the AI where the costs are.** The expensive steps are scanning and relevance judgment, extraction, follow-up search, keeping many hypotheses and relations in view (Pirolli & Card leverage points). Those are the AI's jobs; framing the question, accepting hypotheses and judgments, and the final story are the member's. Pirolli and Card's stages map directly onto objects: shoebox → collected documents; evidence file → graded extracts; schema → timeline, money table, actors map; hypotheses → the ACH set; presentation → the answer *(inference)*.

17. **Use schemas that experts bring.** Experts' value is domain schemas that tell them what to look for and notice what is missing (Pirolli & Card §2.1; Heuer ch. 4 on theory). For procurement and construction, the engine should carry domain schemas (the stages of a public works project; what a change order, a notice of delay, a liquidated-damages clause are) so it can notice absences *(inference)*.

18. **One claim per argument map; argument maps after hypothesis selection, not instead of it.** (Johansson 2024.) An answer's justification can be shown as a small map (claim, reasons, objections, rebuttals) built from accepted extracts; competing explanations get their own maps *(inference)*.

19. **Prefer words with defined meanings over fake numbers.** Numeric scores on every node give false confidence (Johansson 2024, citing Tecuci); ICD 203's fixed vocabulary of likelihood with ranges is the middle path.

20. **Keep it modular and small.** Whole-case graphs become unwieldy; modularise (Dawid et al. 2024). The CIA Primer's techniques are a menu chosen by stage, not a checklist to run every time (Primer timeline; Heuer: once practised, "the entire eight-step procedure may be unnecessary, except on highly controversial issues").

21. **State the story as a short, testable hypothesis and start with the official version.** ≤3 sentences; break into claims; define terms; worst/minimum/maximum outcomes make the effort bounded (Story-Based Inquiry ch. 2). Taking the district's stated explanation as the first hypothesis is both fair and efficient *(inference)*.

22. **Personal data only for a purpose.** ICD 203 limits PII to "a specific analytic purpose"; police handling codes control onward sharing (College of Policing). A civic system naming contractors and officials needs a rule of the same kind *(inference)*.
